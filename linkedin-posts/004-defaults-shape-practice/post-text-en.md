When the right action became the default, more people did it. The results didn't always follow.

In 11 U.S. hospitals, a palliative care consult was ordered automatically for older patients with advanced chronic illness, and doctors could cancel it. The share of patients who got a consult rose from 16.6% to 43.9%. Length of hospital stay did not change.

In another trial, smoking treatment started automatically for every inpatient who smoked, unless they said no. Use of quit-smoking medication rose from 34% to 60%. But by six months, quit rates were almost the same.

Defaults don't always work. In a trial with surgeons, only 21% of patients got the pre-filled prescription. Surgeons typed the rest by hand.

My question for you:
Which default in your systems decides what your team does today, without anyone noticing?
