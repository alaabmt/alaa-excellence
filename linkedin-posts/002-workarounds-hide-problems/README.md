## Evidence pack

Updated 18 Oct 2026: slide 6 now uses Vanbelleghem S, De Regge M, Van Nieuwenhove Y, Gemmel P. *J Adv Nurs* 2026 (PMID 42504057): 80 nurses in simulation, failures detected 96.2%, second-order problem solving 21.9%, an emotive narrative did not increase escalation. The former slide 6 (three practices) moved to the website article.


Source: Tucker, A. L., & Edmondson, A. C. (2003). Why Hospitals Don't Learn from Failures: Organizational and Psychological Dynamics That Inhibit System Change. *California Management Review*, 45(2), 55–72. PDF: https://www.utsouthwestern.edu/employees/leadership-programs/leadership-foundations/why-hospitals-dont-learn.pdf

| Claim on slides | Location in paper |
|---|---|
| 239 hours, 26 nurses, 9 hospitals; most chosen for nursing excellence | p. 57–58, "Research Base" |
| 194 failures; problems were simple, routine (missing meds, diabetic trays, supplies) | p. 58, p. 62 |
| Definitions of first- and second-order problem solving; linen example | p. 60–61 |
| 93% of problems: "do what it takes to continue — no more, no less", not communicated | p. 61 |
| Only 7% of responses met even lenient second-order criteria | p. 62 |
| 33 minutes lost per 8-hour shift | p. 60 |
| 9 of 10 nurses observed a full shift stayed 45 min unpaid | p. 63 |
| 7 of 9 interviewed felt gratified working around obstacles | p. 60 |
| Three causes: individual vigilance, unit efficiency, empowerment | p. 63–64 |
| 70% of interviewed believed manager expected them to cope alone | p. 65 |
| Levers: manager presence each shift, psychological safety, organizational responsiveness | p. 66–67 |

**Evidence limits:** qualitative observational study; small sample (26 nurses), US hospitals, 2003; non-representative sample by design; percentages refer to observed problems. The paper reports interviews with 12 nurses but cites "7 of 9" for gratification; both figures are reproduced as published.
