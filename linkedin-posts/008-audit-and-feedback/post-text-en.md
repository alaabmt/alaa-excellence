A doctor may work for years without seeing numbers on their own practice. Many health systems now measure how their doctors practice and show them the results. This is called audit and feedback.

Cochrane is an international network of researchers that gathers the studies on one question and summarizes the results. In its review of 292 randomized trials, clinicians who received their data followed recommended practice more often than those who did not, by about 6 percentage points on average; for example, from 50% to 56%. But the effect varied widely, and at least a quarter of the results showed no improvement. None of the five study abstracts behind the article mentions a trial in an Arab country.

In my reading, the numbers are more likely to help when they are about the person's own work, are discussed with someone from the same workplace whom they know, and end with a specific step.

My question for you:
Three years from now, should performance reports in your organization be documents people forget, or a regular review of each person's numbers with a colleague they know?
