Operational Excellence · What the research says

Many hospitals created teams that rush to a patient whose condition gets worse.

A review of 29 studies linked these teams to fewer deaths. But two trials that used chance to assign hospitals or wards found no statistically significant difference in their main outcomes.

What might explain the gap? Here is what five studies, published from 2005 to 2024, show.

The idea

What is a rapid response team?

A team that staff call when a patient shows specific warning signs. The team comes to the patient to assess and treat them before their condition gets worse.

A before-and-after study compares results in the same place before and after a change. In a randomized trial, chance decides which hospitals or patients go into each group, so that the comparison is fair.

Maharaj et al., 2015 · Zhang et al., 20242 / 8
A review of 29 studies

Teams were linked to fewer deaths, but most studies were before-and-after

13%

fewer deaths, roughly, among adult patients where teams existed.

35%

fewer cardiac arrests, roughly, among adult patients where teams existed.

These are relative reductions: for every 100 adult deaths without a team, there were about 87 among a similar number of patients with one. The authors noted that results varied widely between studies.

Maharaj et al., Crit Care, 20153 / 8
The Australian trial

In 23 hospitals assigned by chance, the main results were similar in both groups

5.31

serious events per 1,000 admissions in hospitals with teams, compared with 5.86 in hospitals without them.

The measure combined three events: cardiac arrest, unexpected death, and unplanned admission to intensive care. The difference was not statistically significant, so it may have been due to chance. Results were followed for only six months.

Hillman et al., Lancet, 20054 / 8
The Belgian trial

Across about 70,000 admissions, no statistically significant difference

Researchers introduced a standard system for monitoring patients and responding when they got worse. It started in stages, at times set by chance, in 28 wards across 7 hospitals.

There was no statistically significant difference in unexpected deaths, cardiac arrests, or unplanned admissions to intensive care. These events were less common at the start than expected, which made any difference harder to detect.

Haegdorens et al., Resuscitation, 20185 / 8
Why is the team not always called?
30%

of patients who met the calling criteria and were later admitted to intensive care had the team called, in the Australian trial.

A review of 25 U.S. studies of nurse-led teams found:

  • Calls were more likely when nurses saw the team's value and knew when to call.
  • Worry about the team, and doubt about whether a patient was sick enough, held calls back.
Hillman et al., 2005 · Hecht et al., 20246 / 8
The author's reading of the studies

What can leaders do?

  • 1Write the calling criteria clearly, and train staff on them.
  • 2Track the times the criteria were met but the team was not called.
  • 3Show that management and doctors support the team, and address staff concerns about calling it.

These are three of the six steps in the article. The author inferred them from the studies, and no study tested their combined effect.

Three years from now, do you want your response team to stay a phone number people hesitate to call, or do you want every employee to know when to ask for help, and to ask without fear?

Sources and limits

What this evidence can and cannot tell us

  • Most studies in the first two reviews were before-and-after studies, which cannot show that the teams caused the improvement.
  • The Belgian trial planned to include 14 hospitals, but the final analysis covered only 7.
  • The published abstracts of the five studies do not mention any Arab hospital, and none of the studies tested the idea outside hospitals.
