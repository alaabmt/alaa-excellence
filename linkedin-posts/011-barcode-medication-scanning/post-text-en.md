In hospitals, a patient can receive the wrong medicine or the wrong dose. That is why many hospitals now ask nurses to scan a barcode on the patient's wristband and another on the medicine. The system warns them if either does not match the doctor's order.

In one U.S. medical center, errors other than timing errors happened in 11.5% of administrations before the system and 6.8% after it. That is about 115 errors per 1,000 administrations before, and about 68 after. But the study compared the same units before and after, with no units that went without the system.

In a U.K. study of five wards, staff scanned 5.6% of medicines on one ward and 67% on another. The reasons they recorded for not scanning included barcodes that could not be read and scanners that were not available.

In my reading, buying a checking system is not the change itself. I expect the real change to come when scanning becomes the normal way of working, and when the obstacles that push staff to work around it are removed.

My question for you:
Three years from now, will your organization still have a checking system that you paid for but people rarely use? Or will checking be a normal part of every important step, because you removed what got in the way?
