Operational Excellence · What the research says

In many hospitals, nurses now scan the medicine and the patient's wristband before giving a dose.

In one U.S. medical center, some medication errors fell by about 41% after the system started. In a U.K. study of five wards, staff on one ward scanned only about 6% of medicines.

Why is the scanner not always used? Here is what five studies, published from 2008 to 2025, show.

The idea

What does the scanner do?

Before giving a medicine, the nurse scans a barcode on the patient's wristband, then one on the medicine package. The system checks both against the doctor's order and warns if the medicine, the dose, or the patient is wrong.

A barcode is a set of printed lines that a scanner reads to identify a medicine or a patient.

Poon et al., 2010 · Koppel et al., 20082 / 8
The U.S. medical center

Some medication errors became less common after the system started

6.8%

of administrations had an error other than a timing error after the system, compared with 11.5% before.

1.6%

had a non-timing error that could harm a patient after the system, compared with 3.1% before.

In other words, for every 1,000 administrations, there were about 115 such errors before and about 68 after. But this was a before-and-after study in one center: it compared the same units in two periods.

Poon et al., N Engl J Med, 20103 / 8
The reviews

Two reviews say barcoding may reduce errors, with low certainty

  • The first review gathered 10 studies, all before-and-after studies, and all showed a positive effect.
  • The Cochrane review's barcoding result rested on two studies with more than 50,000 participants.

Low certainty of evidence means the true effect may be quite different from what the studies found.

Hutton et al., 2021 · Ciapponi et al. (Cochrane), 20214 / 8
Five hospitals

Researchers found 15 ways staff work around the system

In some cases, a copy of a patient's wristband barcode was stuck on a computer cart, a door frame, or a nurse's belt ring. In others, several patients' medicines were scanned in advance.

Causes included unreadable barcodes, broken scanners, missing wristbands, medicines with no barcode, and emergencies.

Koppel et al., J Am Med Inform Assoc, 20085 / 8
Five U.K. wards, after the system started
5.6%

of medicines were scanned on one of five wards; on another ward, 67% were.

  • Scanning declined over the first 16 months.
  • Reasons staff recorded for not scanning included unreadable barcodes and scanners that were not available.
  • Higher scanning was linked to a local improvement initiative on the ward.
Williams et al., BMJ Health Care Inform, 20256 / 8
The author's reading of the studies

What can leaders do?

  • 1Measure each month the share of medicines scanned on each ward.
  • 2Ask why scanning did not happen, and fix the causes: unreadable barcodes, broken scanners, missing wristbands.
  • 3Support an improvement initiative on each ward, led by the staff themselves.

These are three of the six steps in the article. The author inferred them from the studies, and no study tested their combined effect.

Three years from now, will your organization still have a checking system that you paid for but people rarely use? Or will checking be a normal part of every important step?

Sources and limits

What this evidence can and cannot tell us

  • The U.S. study and the 10 studies in the first review were all before-and-after studies, so they cannot show that the system alone caused the drop.
  • The workaround study was published in 2008, and the U.K. study covered only five wards.
  • The published abstracts of the five studies do not mention any Arab hospital, and none of the studies tested the idea outside hospitals.
