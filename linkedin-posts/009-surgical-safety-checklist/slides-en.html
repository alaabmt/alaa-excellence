Operational Excellence · What the research says

The World Health Organization designed a checklist for surgical teams.

In eight hospitals, deaths fell after it was introduced. In 101 Ontario hospitals, death and complication rates showed no clear change after adoption.

What might explain the gap? Here is what five studies, published from 2009 to 2025, show.

The idea

What is the Surgical Safety Checklist?

A 19-item list the team reviews at three points: before anesthesia, before the first incision, and before the patient leaves the operating room.

A before-and-after study compares results in the same place before and after a change, with no group where the change did not happen.

Haynes et al., N Engl J Med, 20092 / 8
The first study

Eight hospitals tested the checklist, including one in Amman

0.8%

death rate after the checklist was introduced, down from 1.5% before.

7%

rate of complications during the hospital stay after the checklist, down from 11%.

Patients were 16 or older and had surgery other than heart surgery. Both drops were too large to be explained by chance alone, but this was a before-and-after study: it compared two periods in the same hospitals, with no hospitals that did not use the checklist.

Haynes et al., N Engl J Med, 20093 / 8
The Ontario study

In 101 Ontario hospitals, no clear change after adoption

0.65%

adjusted death rate after adoption, compared with 0.71% before.

3.82%

adjusted complication rate after adoption, compared with 3.86% before.

Adjusted rates take account of differences between patients in the two periods. Neither difference was statistically significant, so chance alone could explain either one. These figures should not be compared with the previous slide, because the patients and the definition of complications differed.

Urbach et al., N Engl J Med, 20144 / 8
How is the checklist used?

The checklist is not always used at every step

38%

of 300 studies gave at least some description of how the checklist was actually used.

61%

average compliance for the step before the first incision, in a review of 13 studies. It was 76% for the step before anesthesia.

Compliance asks whether the checklist was used; definitions varied between studies.

Armstrong et al., 2022 · Habtie et al., 20255 / 8
What went with higher compliance?

In a review of 13 studies, training, management support, and follow-up were linked to higher compliance

  • Earlier experience with the checklist, and training.
  • Management support and a positive work environment.
  • Regular monitoring, with feedback to teams.

Barriers included too few staff, heavy workloads, and no one owning the checklist.

Habtie et al., BMC Health Serv Res, 20256 / 8
The author's reading of the studies

What can leaders do?

  • 1Measure how the checklist is used, not just whether it was signed.
  • 2Focus on the steps with lower compliance, and name someone in each operating room who owns the checklist.
  • 3Do not rely on a policy alone; train teams and follow up on their compliance.

These are three of the six steps in the article. The author inferred them from the studies, and no study tested their combined effect.

Three years from now, do you want checklists in your organization to stay forms that are signed and filed, or to become a real pause where the team reads each item and anyone can raise a concern?

Sources and limits

What this evidence can and cannot tell us

  • The eight-hospital study and the Ontario study were both before-and-after studies; no hospitals were assigned to go without the checklist for comparison.
  • One of the eight hospitals was in Amman, but the study abstract does not report results for each hospital separately.
  • None of the five studies tested the checklist outside surgery. Applying it to other sectors is the author's untested inference.
