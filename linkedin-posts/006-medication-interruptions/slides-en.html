Hospitals tried a “Do not interrupt” vest to protect nurses giving medications.

A randomized trial found no evidence that it reduced errors.

That is what five studies, published from 2021 to 2025, show.

The medication round

What happens during a medication round?

Nurses are interrupted again and again: a colleague has a question, a doctor needs something, or a missing item has to be found.

In a review of 22 studies, 16 found a significant link between interruptions and more medication administration errors.

The studies defined interruptions and errors in different ways, and a link alone does not prove that interruptions cause errors.

Schroers et al., J Adv Nurs, 20252 / 8
Where do interruptions come from?

From colleagues, missing supplies, and phone calls

182

interruptions recorded in 15 medication rounds in a Saudi hospital. Nurses, doctors, and obstacles were the most frequent sources.

20+

interruptions an hour for pharmacists in some studies, mostly from phone calls and patient questions.

The problem goes beyond nursing.

Schroers 2025 · Eid 2022 · Ayanaw 20253 / 8
The trial

A “Do not interrupt” vest in four French hospitals

Nurses wore the vest during medication rounds, and a poster at the unit entrance explained it to patients and families.

Researchers randomly assigned 29 units: 14 used the vest and 15 kept their usual practice. Trained observers watched 383 medication rounds.

Berdot et al., BMC Nurs, 20214 / 8
Findings

No fewer errors, and no effect on interruptions

7.09%

of doses had an error on vest units, compared with 6.23% on the other units.

15.04%

of doses were interrupted on vest units, up from 13.4% before the trial. On the other units, the rate fell from 23.78% to 20.75%.

The researchers concluded that the vest had no impact on errors or on interruption rates.

Berdot et al., BMC Nurs, 20215 / 8
Why did the vest not work?

Nurse-to-nurse interruptions stayed common

In a review of 14 studies, nurses interrupting other nurses stayed common even with vests and campaigns, and the effect of these measures on errors remained unclear.

A vest targets only one source, and many interruptions are a symptom of a system that does not provide supplies or information on time.

The last sentence is the author's interpretation of these studies, not a measured result.

Zhang et al., Worldviews Evid Based Nurs, 20256 / 8
What the studies suggest

What can leaders do?

  • 1Start by measuring: who interrupts, when, and why?
  • 2Remove the causes at the source: put supplies in place, and send questions to a colleague who is not giving medications.
  • 3Measure the errors themselves, not how many people wore the vest.

Sources and limits

What this evidence can and cannot tell us

  • The French trial collected its data in 2017 and tested only the vest and poster.
  • The reviews drew on studies with different definitions, so results were not pooled.
  • Of the five studies, only one took place in an Arab hospital. Applying the idea to other sectors is an inference.
