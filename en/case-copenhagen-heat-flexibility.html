How Copenhagen Tested Buildings as Heat Stores to Ease Peak Heating Demand

2026-11-26

Executive snapshot

On cold winter mornings, buildings across a district heating network can demand heat at the same time, forcing operators to consider additional production. Copenhagen Municipality and its heating utility tested another option: warm buildings ahead of the peak and temporarily reduce heat delivery when demand rises. Walls and floors retain heat, so some buildings can change when they draw it, provided occupants remain comfortable. [2, 4, 6]

In a trial involving 120 municipal buildings over two heating seasons, participants reported a 13.2% reduction in average building-level morning peak demand. A later project reported 150 participating buildings and 6–8 megawatts (MW) of available flexibility. Neither finding establishes citywide savings or an equivalent reduction in the network peak. The management significance is a new role for buildings: they can help determine when energy is needed rather than simply consuming it. [1, 2]

The problem is not just how much heat is needed, but when

District heating networks produce heat at shared facilities and deliver it to buildings through hot-water pipes. When demand concentrates at certain times of day, an operator may need additional production for a relatively short period. A prolonged shortage would present a different challenge. A temporary peak creates an opportunity to change the timing of some demand.

Buildings already have a useful physical property. Their walls, floors and other materials retain heat and release it gradually, a property known as thermal mass. Some buildings can therefore stay warm for a limited period after heat input is reduced. How much flexibility is available depends on insulation, weather and building use; a single control setting cannot suit every property. [3, 4]

Copenhagen tested an alternative to responding to every rise in demand solely by increasing production. It explored how some heating demand could be moved in time without compromising the service. This is an additional operating option, not a substitute for producing heat.

What it took to make buildings part of the network

The intervention shifts some heating to an earlier time, followed by a temporary reduction at the peak. Operators need to know how much heat each building can retain, when supply can be reduced and where the comfort limits lie. Shifting demand is not the same as eliminating it: some demand can return once the reduction ends.

The arrangement was not a stand-alone device installed in each property. It depended on a division of responsibilities across organizations. [4, 6]

From individual properties to coordinated demand

  1. Building owners opt into the arrangement, with attention to building use and occupant comfort.
  2. Controls providers use building and weather data to determine when heat input can be adjusted.
  3. An aggregator combines the flexibility available across participating buildings and coordinates it with network needs.
  4. Heating utility HOFOR and heat-production dispatch organization Varmelast coordinate demand flexibility with network operation and production.
  5. The municipality coordinates its departments and engages building owners in the arrangement.

The ability to coordinate these roles matters as much as the technology. Contracts, data sharing and clear responsibilities make participation possible. The sources do not describe a single algorithm controlling every network decision. The innovation is the ability to combine flexibility across separate buildings into an operating decision.

What the trials actually established

Early trial: changes in peak demand and heating consumption

The early trial covered 120 municipal buildings in the 2021–22 and 2022–23 heating seasons. Participants reported an average 13.2% reduction in morning peak demand at the building level. They also reported a 5–7% decline in heating consumption with predictive solar compensation, which uses weather forecasts to anticipate warming from sunlight and reduce supplied heat accordingly. These are two distinct measures: one concerns the maximum rate of heat demand at a particular time; the other concerns the amount of heat consumed. They come from a participant-authored account, not an independent randomized evaluation. The reported building-level peak reduction cannot be assumed to apply to the network as a whole.

Later project: flexibility available for activation

For the subsequent Flexumers4Future project, the municipality reported 150 buildings, approximately one million square meters of floor area and 6–8 MW of available heat flexibility. [1]

A megawatt measures power at a point in time, not the amount of energy saved over hours or days. The later figures indicate an ability to coordinate a portion of demand. They do not establish that all available flexibility was activated or that the early trial's percentage reductions were replicated.

A 2020 academic simulation of a low-energy residential building in Nordhavn supports the physical feasibility of shifting heat demand through thermal mass. It does not constitute field validation of the later project. [3]

Why successful experimentation does not guarantee scale

A Copenhagen council decision on May 30, 2024 set an ambition of 1,000 participating buildings and a planned period from May 2024 to April 2026. The closing account reports 150 buildings. The sources do not establish a common participation definition for the two figures or explain the difference in sufficient detail. Treating the comparison as a completion rate, or attributing it to one particular cause, would be unjustified. [1, 5]

The documented obstacles nonetheless show why scaling is an organizational challenge. Supplier systems must work together; owners need workable contracts, support and clear arrangements for sharing customer data. More complex building controls also require further experimentation. Project accounts identify commercial and legal barriers as well. Demonstrating that one building can adjust its demand is different from making the arrangement repeatable across hundreds of diverse properties. [4, 6]

Three unresolved questions matter to executives: who pays to install and maintain the controls, what makes continued participation worthwhile for owners, and how are network benefits measured and shared? Technical feasibility on its own does not answer them.

The Tamayuz 10X interpretation

Redefining value and strategic direction

In the conventional heating relationship, a building receives heat. In Copenhagen's experimental model, it may also provide a useful service to the network by changing the timing of demand. A reduction in peak load is not necessarily the same as a reduction in total consumption. Value now includes when heat is used, not simply how much is used.

Leadership, governance and capability building

Owners, controls providers, the utility and the municipality have different knowledge and authority. None can deliver the arrangement alone. Scaling calls for rules on participation, data sharing, comfort responsibilities and measurement. The case supports an analysis of shared governance and capability building, but does not establish a transformation of institutional culture or quantify any multiplication of capability.

Experimentation and institutional and technical intelligence

Weather forecasts and building data create the opportunity to act ahead of demand. Their value depends on translating information into an appropriate control decision, coordinating that decision with the network and measuring its effect. The trials demonstrate early capability in that chain, not mastery of every building and operating condition.

Reinventing the operating model, not yet proving a business model

Voluntary participation changes the role of the customer from a service recipient to a potential contributor to demand management. Aggregation and coordination introduce new operating roles. This is evidence of an emerging operating-model change, not a proven sustainable commercial model. Incentives, contracts and benefit allocation remain conditions for scale.

Foresight and sustainable outcomes

If heat production becomes more closely linked to variable renewable electricity, matching demand to supply may become increasingly valuable. Working back from that possible future identifies today's necessary capabilities: reliable building data, adaptable controls, participation agreements and network-level measurement. This is a forward-looking interpretation, not a claim that Copenhagen used a formal foresight methodology. Existing results do not establish citywide financial benefits, avoided construction or long-term sustainability.

The core shift

There is no demonstrated tenfold improvement here. The 10X significance lies in challenging the assumption that responding to rising demand must always mean adding production. The alternative operating capability still needs to prove its value at scale.

Could the principle apply to district cooling in the UAE?

The corresponding question for the United Arab Emirates is about cooling rather than heating. Could selected buildings connected to district cooling be cooled ahead of peak demand, then temporarily reduce their cooling demand while keeping indoor conditions comfortable? This is a proposed local experiment, not a demonstrated outcome in Abu Dhabi or Dubai. Climate, humidity, building characteristics and cooling systems may change the result.

Executives and network operators could test the proposition before committing to widespread implementation:

  1. Set the objective: reduce the coincident network peak without degrading service or occupant comfort.
  2. Establish a baseline: record hourly demand, indoor and outdoor temperatures and building-use patterns, then account for weather differences when comparing results.
  3. Select a diverse pilot: include different building uses and insulation characteristics; agree on comfort boundaries and stop conditions with owners.
  4. Test coordination: shift some cooling earlier, reduce demand at the peak, then monitor whether demand rebounds after the reduction period.
  5. Evaluate total value: compare network peaks, energy use across the full day, service quality, complaints, and the cost of controls and participation before deciding whether to scale.

Success cannot be defined by an isolated building's peak. If demand simply shifts to another network peak or service deteriorates, the intended value may not materialize. The leadership decision is to build a measurable capability and expand it only once the benefits are demonstrated.

What remains unproven

Published accounts do not provide enough detail on averaging methods, variation across buildings, comfort thresholds or complaints to reproduce the results. The available independent evidence is insufficient to establish a reduction in the overall network peak, citywide financial savings or avoided construction of production facilities. Scale also depends on interoperable controls, agreements and regulation.

Copenhagen is worth studying because it tested a new role for buildings in an energy network, not because it has solved every challenge. The case for transformation becomes stronger when effects on the network, service and cost can be demonstrated repeatedly.

10X question: When demand for your organization's services rises sharply for a short period, is your first response to add resources—or could you redesign when the service is delivered and use existing resources more intelligently without compromising quality?
