Shu'ayb: What Are We Shortchanging Without Noticing?

When a fair scale is part of setting things right

Methodological note: This is a contemporary reflection on verses from Surah Hud (11:84–88), read in light of the classical commentaries. It does not offer a new interpretation of the verses, and it does not attribute modern management concepts to the Qur’an.

Have you ever had your work judged by a standard that missed the most important thing you did? Or seen your effort credited to someone else, with no one meaning to wrong you?

That simple, human question takes us to an old scene in the land of Madyan. Their prophet, Shu'ayb, peace be upon him, told them plainly: Indeed, I see you in prosperity. They had a comfortable living and plenty of wealth. Yet one fault kept repeating in their dealings: when they measured out goods for other people, they gave short measure.

Their problem was not a lack of means. It was the scale they used with everyone else. So the command came:

﴿وَيَا قَوْمِ أَوْفُوا الْمِكْيَالَ وَالْمِيزَانَ بِالْقِسْطِ ۖ وَلَا تَبْخَسُوا النَّاسَ أَشْيَاءَهُمْ وَلَا تَعْثَوْا فِي الْأَرْضِ مُفْسِدِينَ﴾Meaning in English: “And O my people, give full measure and weight in justice and do not deprive the people of their due and do not commit abuse on the earth, spreading corruption.”Surah Hud, 11:85

The previous verse had already forbidden them to cut the measure and the scale. Here the command to give full measure is followed by a prohibition with wider wording: “do not deprive the people of their due.”

What the commentaries say

Al-Tabari explains the prohibition as not reducing the rights people are owed, “whether by measure, by weight or otherwise,” and quotes Qatadah: “Do not wrong people in what is theirs.” [1] Ibn Kathir notes that Shu'ayb first forbade them to give short measure when they sold to others, then told them to give full measure in justice, both “when taking and when giving.” [2]

Al-Qurtubi says the point of justice here is “that everyone who has a share receives that share,” and reads the prohibition as: do not reduce anything people are entitled to. [3] Al-Saadi defines justice in this verse as “the fairness you would accept being given to you.” [4]

His people answered with mockery, asking whether his prayer told them “…or not do with our wealth what we please?” Hud, 11:87. Al-Saadi explains what they meant: this is our money, we do with it as we like, and you have no say over it. [5] Shu'ayb replied:

﴿وَمَا أُرِيدُ أَنْ أُخَالِفَكُمْ إِلَىٰ مَا أَنْهَاكُمْ عَنْهُ ۚ إِنْ أُرِيدُ إِلَّا الْإِصْلَاحَ مَا اسْتَطَعْتُ ۚ وَمَا تَوْفِيقِي إِلَّا بِاللَّهِ ۚ عَلَيْهِ تَوَكَّلْتُ وَإِلَيْهِ أُنِيبُ﴾Meaning in English: “And I do not intend to differ from you in that which I have forbidden you; I only intend reform as much as I am able. And my success is not but through Allāh. Upon Him I have relied, and to Him I return.”Surah Hud, 11:88

Ibn Kathir quotes Qatadah's reading: “I would not forbid you something and then do it myself.” He explains “I only intend reform as much as I am able” as reform pursued “with all my effort and strength.” [6] Al-Saadi adds that Shu'ayb never forbade them anything without being the first to give it up himself, and that “my success is not but through Allāh” guards against any hint of self-praise. [7]

The scale begins with worship

Shu'ayb opened his call with O my people, worship Allāh; you have no deity other than Him, and only then forbade them to cut the measure and the scale. Al-Saadi notes that, alongside their idolatry, they used to give short measure, so he forbade them that too. [8] Fair dealing does not appear in these verses as a separate business rule. It comes after the call to worship Allah alone.

He then told them: What remains [lawful] from Allāh is best for you, if you would be believers Hud, 11:86. The early scholars described “what remains from Allah” in different words. Ibn Kathir reports that Ibn Abbas said it means Allah's provision, and Mujahid said obedience to Allah. He also reports Ibn Jarir al-Tabari's view: the profit left to them after giving full measure is better for them than taking other people's money. [9] Al-Saadi says: what Allah has left you of good is enough for you. [10]

There is a deep matter of faith to reflect on here: lawful provision, even when it looks smaller, is better than extra gained by cutting what others are owed. A fair scale is a trust and an act of worship before it is a management skill.

The shortfall no scale can show

Short measure in weighing goods is visible. Another scale can catch it. But some shortfalls may never appear on any scale: mentioning someone's work without crediting them, judging their contribution by what is easy to count while ignoring what is hard to count, or holding others to a standard we would never accept for ourselves.

Al-Saadi's definition offers a simple, demanding test: justice is “the fairness you would accept being given to you.” [4] Before you judge someone else's work, ask: would I accept my own work being measured this way?

A fair scale does not change between taking and giving.

When money is kept apart from values

Their reply suggests that Shu'ayb's people were not only arguing about the details of measuring. They rejected the principle itself: that faith should have any say in how they earned and traded. They wanted to keep what they worshipped apart from what they did with their money.

That split can creep into our lives today. We may treat work as a zone governed only by numbers, and fairness and honesty as private matters for after hours. Al-Qurtubi explains the reform Shu'ayb sought as setting right “your worldly life through justice, and your hereafter through worship.” [11] Fairness in the scale is part of doing right, not something separate from it.

A 10X reflection: what does your organization measure, and what does it shortchange?

Every organization has its scales: performance indicators, annual appraisals, progress reports. These scales can decide who gets seen and who does not, who is rewarded and who is forgotten.

A hypothetical example: in a customer service unit, staff performance is measured by the number of transactions completed each day. One employee reviews complex requests carefully and catches errors that would have come back as complaints and rework. Her number in the report is lower than her colleagues', and her most valuable work never shows up on the scale at all.

No one set out to shortchange her. The scale itself is short.

These are questions from modern management, not an interpretation of the verses. Still, reflecting on “do not deprive the people of their due” may lead us to check our own scales. Do they give everyone what they are owed, or only what is easiest to measure?

Excellence that goes beyond small improvements may start here: not by adding one more indicator to a long list, but by rethinking what we measure in the first place, so the scale shows the work that truly creates value, not only the work that is easy to count.

Reform as much as you are able

Shu'ayb's answer holds two lessons for anyone trying to change an established way of working.

First, start with yourself. “I do not intend to differ from you in that which I have forbidden you.” A leader who holds the team to a standard they do not keep may lose the team's trust before any reform begins.

Second, know the limits of your ability. “As much as I am able.” Al-Qurtubi notes that Shu'ayb said this “because ability is a condition of action, not of intention.” [11] The intention can be complete, while the action depends on capacity. So do not wait to have everything before you start, and do not load yourself with more than you can carry.

Then the verse closes: “And my success is not but through Allāh. Upon Him I have relied, and to Him I return.” Reform is the effort a person makes. Success in it comes from Allah, glorified and exalted is He.

The 10X excellence question

What scale do you want to build in your organization?

If, years from now, you want every member of your team to feel that their work is weighed fairly, which scale do you need to start fixing today? And if your own work were judged by that scale alone, would you accept the result?

A final reflection

Shu'ayb spoke to a people who were doing well: “Indeed, I see you in prosperity.” Ibn Kathir explains this as prosperity in their living and provision, and says Shu'ayb feared they would lose what they had. [12] Prosperity was no excuse to be careless with the scale. If anything, it was a stronger reason to keep it true.

What we might carry from this scene into our own lives is simple: weigh fairly what we take and what we give, start with ourselves before others, reform what we can, and rely on Allah for what lies beyond us.
