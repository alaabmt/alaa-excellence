FMEA · Failure Mode & Effects Analysis · Manufacturing · Electronics · Quality Control

What happened to circuit-board defects when an Indian factory applied FMEA?

Electronics factories test their circuit boards before shipping. The study says a failed board can cause financial losses and safety problems. At one factory in Haryana, India, testing recorded 366 defects among 40,398 boards from November 2021 to March 2022. The study does not define a defect.

The factory applied Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA), which checks how each step could fail. Among its changes were a zoom machine (a magnifying device) for visual inspection and a software-controlled test jig. It appears that this makes inspection depend less on one person’s eye and judgment.

The study gives no date for the changes. A split at April 2022 fits its totals; this split is calculated here. From April to September, testing recorded 58 defects among 40,820 boards. With no comparison factory, the drop cannot be credited to the changes alone. Even so, a simple monthly count made the change easy to track.

What problem did the factory face?

The factory belongs to a company the study names MS Electrovision Pvt Ltd, in Manesar, Haryana. It makes printed circuit boards (PCBs), which carry electronic components and join them with copper tracks. The study lists 22 steps, from receiving materials to shipping, including soldering (joining parts with melted metal), visual inspection, and testing. [1]

The study describes one incident on an air-conditioner board. Tiny solder balls caught between a chip’s legs and caused a short circuit that final testing missed. [1]

The monthly counts cover boards the study calls “display PCB,” in a table titled “Testing defect.” It does not define a defect, name the counting test, or say whether a board counts once or once per defect. It appears that a defect is a board that failed the factory’s test; the study does not confirm this. [1]

How did the team analyze the steps and score risk?

FMEA examines each step before harm occurs: how could it fail, and what follows? A failure mode is one way a step can go wrong. According to the study, the production team and its author scored each failure mode together. [1]

FMEA usually gives each failure mode three scores. Severity is how serious the effect would be. Occurrence is how likely it is. Detection is how well current checks would catch it before it reaches the customer; a higher score means a weaker check. Scales usually run from 1 to 10.

The study took its severity and occurrence scales from the Automotive Industry Action Group (AIAG) manual. It does not show its detection scale, so the direction and range above are the usual convention. Multiplying the three scores gives the risk priority number (RPN). What did the factory change in inspection and testing?

The study ties the changes to the short-circuit incident rather than to the score ranking. It names two tools among “a number of initiatives.” [1]

  • The factory used a zoom machine (a magnifying device) for visual inspection instead of an ordinary magnifying lens.
  • It used a software-controlled test jig, which holds a board, connects it to test equipment, and decides whether it passes, instead of a manual jig. The study does not expand “FCT”; in electronics it usually means functional circuit test.

The study adds that defects in conformal coating (a protective layer) and “pre-testing” fell, and that the risk priority number fell, without counts or new scores. [1]

It appears that the two tools change how defects are detected, not how boards are made. Magnification may catch a fault at visual inspection, so fewer reach the counting test, and the new jig may count differently. The mechanism is not explained.

The study gives monthly counts from November 2021 to September 2022 but no change date. [1] Its totals match a split at April 2022, calculated here: five months before and six after. The study mapped the whole process, scored the risks only in part, and named two tools among its initiatives. It shows no causes, detection methods, or new scores, so it shows a lower recorded defect rate, not a lower calculated risk.

The limits of what is known

Confidence in this result is limited. It rests on one before-and-after comparison at one factory, with no comparison factory. [1]

  • The two periods cover different months of the year, so seasonal or demand effects are possible.
  • The new jig may be part of the counting test, so the drop may partly reflect a change in measurement.
  • The study lists the two tools among “a number of initiatives” and under general quality-control improvement. The effect of each tool is unknown, other changes are possible, and monthly variation within each period is not shown.
  • The incident was described on an air-conditioner board, and the counts are for display boards. No customer failures, costs, or risk scores after the change are reported.

How can you start a similar small trial?

Here is a simple way to start.

  • Pick one production line. Agree on what counts as a defect and which test counts it, and record the units tested.
  • Score each failure mode with the people who run the step, on all three scores, and write down the direction of the detection scale.
  • Change no more than two things, and record each date.
  • Track the defect rate monthly, re-score the risks, and compare with an unchanged line if you can.

What does this case add to a 10X view?

The study’s changes were an inspection tool and a test tool, with no new production line. The recorded defect rate fell to about one-sixth. That is a large change on one measure, not a tenfold gain, and the study does not show that it lasted. It appears that better detection can lower recorded defects when a fault is caught before the counting point.

The Preventive Engineering question

Where in your work does catching an error depend on someone looking or testing by hand? Which tool or software check could support that person? And which monthly defect rate would show whether the gain lasts?
