FMEA · Failure Mode & Effects Analysis · Manufacturing · Electronics · Quality Control

What happened to circuit-board defects when an Indian factory applied FMEA?

A faulty circuit board that reaches a customer can halt the customer’s product and cost the maker money. So a factory in Haryana, India, tests its boards before shipping. From November 2021 to March 2022, its tests found defects in 366 of 40,398 display boards, about 91 per 10,000. These are defects the factory’s own testing recorded, not documented failures at customers. The factory then applied Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA), which checks how each step could fail, and changed how it inspects and tests boards. From April to September 2022, testing found 58 faulty boards among 40,820, about 14 per 10,000. The study has no comparison factory, however, so we cannot credit the drop to the changes alone.

What problem did the factory face?

The factory is an electronics design and manufacturing company, MS Electrovision, in Manesar, Haryana. It builds printed circuit boards (PCBs), the boards that carry electronic components and join them with copper tracks. A board passes through about 22 steps, from receiving materials to dispatch. These include soldering, visual inspection, and testing. [1]

The study says a failed board can cause financial losses and safety problems. It describes one incident on an air-conditioner board. Tiny solder balls caught between the legs of a chip and caused a short circuit that final testing missed. [1]

The study reports monthly defect counts for one product only, the display PCB. It titles its table “Testing defect” and does not define a defect. In our reading, it is a board that failed the factory’s test, not a documented failure at a customer. [1]

How did the team analyze the steps and score risk?

Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA) examines each step before harm occurs. How could this step fail? What follows if it does? The production team and the study’s author scored each failure mode, meaning each way a step can go wrong. [1]

FMEA usually scores a failure mode on three things, the S/O/D triad. Severity is how serious the effect would be. Occurrence is how likely it is. Detection is how likely current checks are to catch it before it reaches the customer. The team used scales from the Automotive Industry Action Group (AIAG) manual, but the study does not print its detection scale. The product of the three scores is the risk priority number (RPN). What did the factory change in inspection and testing?

The study ties the changes to the short-circuit incident. It does not show that the score ranking decided the choice. The factory added two tools. [1]

  • The first is a zoom machine for visual inspection, replacing an ordinary magnifying lens.
  • The second is a software-controlled test jig (an FCT jig). It holds a board, connects it to test equipment, and decides whether it passes. It replaced a manual jig.

The study adds that defects in conformal coating (a protective layer on the board) and in pre-testing fell, without counts. It says the risk priority number fell, but shows no new scores. [1]

In our reading, the two tools change how defects are detected, not how boards are made. Magnification shows what the eye misses, and software decides in place of the inspector. This is our interpretation, not the study’s claim.

What did the study measure after the change?

The factory counted testing defects in display boards each month from November 2021 to September 2022. The study does not give the date of the change, but its table totals match two periods: November to March before, and April to September after. In our reading, the change came around April 2022. The defect rate fell from about 0.91% to about 0.14%, roughly one-sixth of its earlier level. That is a drop of 0.76 percentage points, which the study writes as “0.76%.”

The study reports a second result. The lot reject rate (LRR) fell from 5,500 to 900 parts per million. But it shows no data for this rate or its period. We treat it as a claim, not a documented result. [1]

Where was the method completed, and where not?

The method is usually taught in nine stages. The table below, our summary, shows how far the study got.

The nine stages as they appear in the study (our summary)
StageWhat the study reports
1. Define the scopeOne factory and a process of 22 steps, from receiving materials to dispatch.
2. List components and tasksA process-flow diagram, with a required function for each step.
3. Failure modes, effects, and causesFailure modes and their effects on the customer and the factory; causes do not appear in the tables.
4. Current prevention and detectionIn-process quality control is mentioned in general terms; detection methods are not listed row by row.
5. Score the riskSeverity, occurrence, and detection, then their product; the detection scale is not printed.
6. Set prioritiesRanking by risk priority number; it is unclear how the items acted on were chosen.
7. Design and carry out actionsTwo tools: a zoom machine and a functional test jig.
8. Verify effectivenessTesting-defect rate compared across two periods; the new risk scores are not shown.
9. Review and updateNot reported.

Mapping, scoring, and acting were mostly completed. Causes, detection methods, and re-scoring were not shown. The result therefore shows a lower defect rate, not a lower calculated risk.

What we do not know

Confidence in this result is limited. It rests on a before-and-after comparison at one factory, with no comparison factory, over two periods in different months. [1]

  • The date of the change is not stated, and the two tools came within a general “improved quality control,” so we cannot tell what each contributed.
  • The monthly table repeats the April row in May (17,284 boards and 22 defects). We matched the study’s totals only by counting April once. Counting both rows would leave the rate at about 0.14%.
  • The incident was described on an air-conditioner board, and the counts are for display boards.
  • The study does not define a defect. It shows no customer failures, costs, or risk scores after the change.

How can you start a similar small trial?

  • Pick one line and one defect that testing can count, and record the units tested.
  • Score each failure mode with the people who run the step, on all three dimensions.
  • Change no more than two things, and record each date.
  • Track the same rate for several months, re-score the risks, and compare with an unchanged line if you can.

What does this case add to a 10X view?

The two changes were an inspection tool and a test tool, not a new production line. The recorded testing-defect rate fell to about one-sixth. That is a large change on one measure in one factory, but it is not a tenfold gain, and the study does not show that it lasted. In our reading, prevention may pay off first where detection depends on an inspector’s eye or hand. That is an inference from one site.

The Preventive Engineering question

In which step of your work does catching an error depend on an inspector’s eye or hand? What would it take for a tool to magnify the view and software to decide the result? And what monthly count, against the same denominator, would show whether the gain holds?
