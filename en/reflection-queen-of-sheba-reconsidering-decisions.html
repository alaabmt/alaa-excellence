The Queen of Sheba: The Courage to Reconsider

When is changing your mind an act of courage?

العربية

Methodological note: A contemporary reflection informed by classical tafsir, not a new interpretation or an attribution of modern management concepts to the Qur’an.

“She said, ‘O eminent ones, advise me in my affair. I would not decide a matter until you witness [for] me.’”

An-Naml 27:32 — Saheeh International

There is a moment in leadership when the hardest thing to say is not “I have decided.” It is “I need to hear more.”

The Queen of Sheba has received a letter from Sulayman. Its implications reach beyond her personal authority to the future of her kingdom. She could issue an order. Instead, she brings the matter before her council.

She is not asking because she lacks the power to decide. She is choosing not to confuse authority with complete understanding.

What happens when everyone expects you to be certain, but the situation deserves another question?

Strength Is Not Yet a Strategy

“They said, ‘We are men of strength and of great military might, but the command is yours, so see what you will command.’”

An-Naml 27:33 — Saheeh International

The council offers a reassuring answer: we have the power to fight. The queen turns instead to the consequences of conflict. In verse 34, she describes the destruction and humiliation that can follow when kings enter a city.

She does not deny her people's strength. She questions whether strength settles the matter.

It is a distinction worth carrying into a boardroom. An organization may have the capital to enter a market, the technology to automate a service, or the influence to force through a change. None of those capabilities, on their own, establishes that the choice is wise.

“Can we do this?” is a capability question. “What will happen if we do?” is a judgment question.

The Decision That Leaves Room to Learn

“But indeed, I will send to them a gift and see with what [reply] the messengers will return.”

An-Naml 27:35 — Saheeh International

She chooses to learn more before committing her kingdom to confrontation. Classical commentators discuss the gift as a way of testing Sulayman's response and discerning the nature of his mission. His answer makes clear that material inducement will not turn him from it.

Notice what this episode can prompt us to ask without turning the Qur'anic narrative into a management manual: Do we gather evidence to improve our decision, or to protect the decision we have already made?

Both activities can look identical. There may be meetings, reports, research and consultation. The difference appears only when the evidence contradicts the original plan. Does the plan change—or does the organization find a reason to dismiss the evidence?

When New Understanding Changes Everything

Later, the queen encounters her throne in an unfamiliar setting and responds with the measured words recorded in verse 42: “[It is] as though it was the very one.” The account then moves to the glass-floored palace and concludes with her declaration:

“My Lord, indeed I have wronged myself, and I submit with Solomon to Allah, Lord of the worlds.”

An-Naml 27:44 — Saheeh International

This is the story's defining conclusion: her submission to Allah. It must not be reduced to an analogy about strategic agility. The religious meaning stands on its own.

Yet the sequence invites a separate contemporary reflection. What does it take to reconsider a belief when a fuller understanding becomes available? How much of our resistance comes from the evidence itself—and how much comes from the fact that we have publicly committed to an earlier view?

The Cost of Being Consistent

Imagine a company that has spent years refining a successful business model. Its executives are experienced. Its metrics are reassuring. Its strategy has been approved, funded and communicated.

Then customer expectations shift. A new technology changes the economics of the industry. Frontline staff begin reporting problems that the official dashboards barely register.

The company does not necessarily lack intelligence. It may lack a way to revise its convictions without treating revision as failure.

Ask a more revealing set of questions. Who can challenge a strategic assumption without risking their standing? Which signals from customers never reach the people making decisions? When did the leadership team last identify a belief that new evidence had made obsolete?

And what happens to the person who discovers that an expensive initiative should stop?

The 10X Question

At Tamayuz 10X, foresight is valuable only when it changes what an organization is prepared to become. A different future may require a different value proposition, new capabilities, or a business model that makes yesterday's strengths less central.

That creates a difficult tension. Organizations celebrate ambition while often protecting the assumptions that define their current success. They invest in transformation but hesitate to reconsider the choices that transformation would actually have to change.

What would we do differently today if we were not obliged to defend yesterday's strategy?

The Qur'anic account does not prescribe a contemporary strategic-planning framework. But reflecting on its moments of consultation, consequence and new understanding can help us ask whether our decision processes allow genuine learning.

Three Questions Before Your Next Big Decision

What must be true for this decision to work? Name the assumption that matters most. If it proves false, would you still make the same choice?

What have we not yet learned? Identify whose perspective, which evidence, or what limited experiment could meaningfully change your understanding.

What would make us reconsider? Agree in advance on the signals that justify changing course. Without them, commitment can quietly become attachment.

These questions do not eliminate uncertainty. They make learning possible before the cost of certainty becomes too high.

The Courage to Reconsider

Return to the queen's opening words: she will not settle the matter without hearing her council. The story proceeds far beyond consultation, ultimately reaching a profound declaration of faith. Its full meaning belongs to that Qur'anic context.

For our own decisions, one question remains: What are we still defending simply because we once believed it?

Conviction matters. So does the ability to recognize when the evidence, the circumstances, or our understanding has changed.

Sometimes courage is making a difficult decision. Sometimes it is having the integrity to revisit one.
