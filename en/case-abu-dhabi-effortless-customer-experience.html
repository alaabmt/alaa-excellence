10X Case · Abu Dhabi · Government Services

When the Transaction Disappears: How Abu Dhabi Redesigned the Government Service Experience

What if the best service improvement is not to make a transaction faster, but to remove the steps and visits that should not exist in the first place?

Executive Snapshot

Abu Dhabi launched its Effortless Customer Experience programme in 2022 as a government-wide model for redesigning services around the effort people expend, rather than around organisational boundaries. It began with pilots in three government entities; official sources report that those pilots streamlined customer journeys by almost 50% on average. The model then expanded across approximately 65 government entities and companies. [1][2]

By 2025, the Department of Government Enablement (DGE) reported approximately 400,000 fewer service-centre visits, a 23% reduction in service steps, 36% fewer required fields, and a 64% reduction in customer requests. DGE’s 2025 year review repeated the results and reported that more than 600,000 customer-feedback data points informed the expanded strategy. [2][3]

The Challenge: When a Digital Service Preserves the Old Process

A service can be fully digitised while preserving the real burden. The form moves to a phone, but people may still need to identify the authority, choose the service, re-enter information government already holds and navigate steps that create no value for them.

Customer Experience (CX) is the end-to-end experience a person has while obtaining a service. The Customer Effort Score (CES) focuses on how easy or difficult it is to get the required outcome. Abu Dhabi’s model made effort a design and measurement principle rather than merely asking for satisfaction after a transaction. [1]

The 10X Move: Do Not Improve a Step Before Asking Why It Exists

The model is organised around four connected dimensions: direction, design, development and delivery. It is supported by common policy and standards, journey prioritisation, a design lab, capability building and a measurement model centred on customer effort. [1][4]

The move was not “make the form faster.” It was “redesign the journey so unnecessary steps, requests and visits disappear.”

Pilots preceded scale. The programme moved from three entities into a broader government implementation, while the later strategy added shared measurement, customer-feedback data, design programmes and innovation mechanisms. [1][2]

Evidence of Impact: What Is Established, and What Is Not?

The published operating metrics point in a consistent direction: fewer visits, steps, fields and requests. These measures are more directly connected to friction than a satisfaction score alone. An independent professional report on government customer-experience trends also documents Abu Dhabi as an institutional case integrating direction, design, development and delivery. [5]

Evidence limit: the principal impact figures are government-reported. No publicly available independent academic study was found that recalculates the 400,000 visits or the 64%, 23% and 36% figures from raw data. They are therefore treated as repeatedly published operational results, not as independent causal estimates. Public sources also do not provide enough denominator and calculation detail to reproduce every percentage.

What Can Abu Dhabi Government Now Do Differently?

The programme created a stronger ability to treat the customer journey as an improvement unit that can cross agency boundaries, collect broad feedback, translate it into design priorities, and measure effort. As the strategy scaled, the operating logic moved beyond channel improvement toward a shared government capability to identify friction and remove it. [2][3]

See friction across the journey → measure effort → redesign jointly → remove steps and requests → measure the result.

The 10X Lens

Future Foresight and Ambition

The intended future is proactive, seamless government that requires less knowledge of bureaucracy.

Redefining Value

Value is expressed in effort removed, not simply transactions digitised.

Leadership and Governance

Common policy, standards and measurement made redesign a cross-government discipline.

Capability Multiplication

Design labs, training, tools and methods allow entities to redesign journeys.

Operating-Model Reinvention

The journey, rather than the procedure inside one agency, becomes the unit of design.

Institutional and Technological Intelligence

More than 600,000 feedback data points informed the expanded strategy.

Value and Sustainable Results

Visits, steps, requests and fields are tangible operating indicators of effort removed.

From the Future Back to Today

Desired future: people and businesses should not need to understand government structure to obtain the outcome they need, and services should become simpler and more proactive.

← Required capabilities: cross-entity journey insight, trusted feedback data, service design, effort measurement, redesign skills and governance that permits requirements to be removed.

← What must be built now: common standards, design labs, workforce capability, measurement tools, data integration and clear ownership of the whole journey.

← Different way of working: start from the outcome a person needs, then work backwards to remove what does not serve it.

← Results and impact: fewer steps, requests, fields and visits, while continuing to test quality, equity and whether effort has simply moved elsewhere.

Practical Use and Transferability

Leaders can map one end-to-end journey and challenge every step: Is it legally or operationally necessary? Does the organisation already hold the data? Could the action be proactive? Does the user need to see this step at all? Does the current KPI reward completing a transaction or removing the need for it?

The logic transfers to banking, healthcare, education, utilities and internal corporate services: measure real effort, not screens; design around the outcome, not departmental boundaries.

Institutional-Perspective Critical Review

A simulated review from the perspective of a senior customer-experience leader identified four risks. First, the story could be reduced to digitisation, so the final version makes policy, measurement, capability building and the design lab explicit. Second, 400,000 fewer visits could be mistaken for an independently causal estimate, so it is clearly labelled as a DGE-reported operating result. Third, the scaling path needed the three-entity pilot before broader implementation. Fourth, reduced effort alone does not prove service quality, so the final analysis retains the need to monitor quality, equity and burden shifting.

Still not publicly verifiable in sufficient detail: the exact calculation method for each percentage, complete baselines and denominators for some metrics, distribution of results by entity and service, programme cost and financial return, redesigns that failed, and differences in experience across population groups.

The 10X Question

What would your organisation look like if customers or employees no longer had to understand your departments and procedures to get the outcome they need? If that is the future you want, what data, skills, decision rights, design standards, systems and governance must you build now so you can remove effort at its source rather than move it onto a better-looking screen?
