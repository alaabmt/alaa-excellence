Does a 75% Drop in a Risk Score at an LPG Filling Station Mean the Explosion Risk Fell?

At a station in Rayong Province, Thailand, workers fill liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) cylinders and move them to storage. One leak there can become an explosion or a fire. The operators saw the problems but had not collected data to analyze them in an organized way.

Two researchers analyzed eight failure modes using failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA). The actions included new valves, pressure-sensor checks, gas detectors, and automatic fire suppression. The highest risk priority number (RPN, where lower is better) was 225. After the actions, scores fell by 67% to 78%. But the paper does not explain where the after scores came from, and Severity did not change in any row.

What problem did the station face?

The study covers an LPG filling and transport station run by a company in Rayong Province, Thailand. It fills 15-kilogram cylinders. LPG is highly flammable, so a leak can cause an explosion or a fire. [1]

The researchers say the operators saw defects and risks in their work. They had no data and no structured way to address them. [1]

The researchers mapped the ten steps of filling a cylinder. After collecting views from supervisors and operators, they chose three main processes because an accident there would be severe: filling, moving cylinders to the warehouse, and storing them. [1]

How did the study analyze the risks?

The team listed causes of failure by brainstorming in four areas: people, machines, materials, and methods. Then it scored each failure mode, meaning each way a step can go wrong, on three scales known together as S/O/D. Severity is how serious the effect is. Occurrence is how often it happens. Detection is how hard it is to notice. Each runs from 1 to 10, and a Detection score of 10 means the failure cannot be detected. [1]

The product of the three is the RPN. Management, safety experts, and operators agreed on three bands. Above 200 is high risk and needs immediate action. From 150 to 199 is medium risk and should be fixed in a suitable time. Below 150 is low risk and can be monitored. A score of exactly 200 falls into no band. [1]

Before the actions, only two rows were above 200: the storage leak (225) and the filling leak (216). Afterward, all eight rows were between 40 and 54, below the 150 line. The drop in every row comes from Occurrence (after the actions: 3, 3, 2, 2, 3, 3, 3, 2, in table order) and from Detection (2 in all eight rows). Severity stayed the same. [1]

The study says the scores fell after these measures were put in place:

  • Seal and valve checks before filling, and higher-quality valves.
  • Pressure-sensor calibration every six months.
  • Cylinder locking devices and standard racks.
  • A gas detection system.
  • Automatic fire suppression and emergency-plan training. [1]

What do we not know?

The result rests on one station, and the study reports no measurement after the actions, so it is a limited trial. The paper does not say how the after scores were set. They read as estimates, which is our inference. [1]

  • The paper says the measures were implemented. It gives no dates, no record of which were finished, no name for who re-scored the risks, and no incidents or inspection results behind the numbers.
  • The after Detection score is 2 in every row, and the after Occurrence score is 2 or 3. That sameness suggests the scores were assigned by judgment, which is our inference.
  • Severity stayed at 10 in three rows, including the fire. An RPN of 40 does not mean the consequence of an accident is smaller.
  • Before the actions, the fire row and the cylinder-drop row (120 each) were in the “monitor” band despite Severity 10. The study proposed actions for them anyway.
  • The lower Severity levels (1 to 6) in the study’s criteria describe patients and treatment, which does not fit a gas station. The study does not explain how it adapted them.
  • There is no limitations section, and the paper is published in Thai with an English abstract.

In an independent source, the US National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health ranks controls from most to least effective: elimination, substitution, engineering controls, administrative controls, and protective equipment. It says the first three work better because they control exposure without significant human interaction. [2] Engineering controls change the equipment. Administrative controls change procedures. By our classification, the station’s measures combine engineering controls, such as new valves and automatic suppression, and administrative ones, such as inspection, training, and scheduled calibration.

How can you start a small pilot of your own?

These steps are our suggestion, not the study’s.

  • Pick one high-risk process and log real incidents and near misses, meaning events that could have caused harm but did not, with dates.
  • Write what each S/O/D score means in your own workplace before you score.
  • Agree on priority bands, and add a rule: any failure mode with severe consequences gets a review whatever its score.
  • After the change, re-score with new evidence, such as inspection results and alarm tests, not by the opinion of whoever scored before.
  • Decide who re-scores and when, and compare the result with the incidents that actually happen.

What does this case add to the 10X view?

The study gives a clear list of hazards and an action for each. A percentage drop in an RPN does not by itself show that risk fell. That is our reading, and the study gives no evidence either way. The question before accepting the drop is what evidence changed the score.

What is your preventive engineering question?

In your riskiest process, what evidence would change an Occurrence or Detection score after an improvement, other than the opinion of whoever scored it before?
