From Waiting for Field Intervention to Restoring Power in Under a Minute: How DEWA Redesigned Distribution Fault Response

The shift was not merely faster repair. It was the grid's ability to locate a fault, isolate the affected section and automatically restore supply to healthy sections. DEWA's journey shows how digital infrastructure, communications and control can become an operating capability that compresses restoration time—while the evidence still requires careful separation between system-specific results and overall network reliability.

OrganisationDubai Electricity and Water Authority (DEWA)
CapabilityASGR within the wider FLISR architecture
Reported operating resultRestoration time below one minute for restorable healthy sections

Executive Snapshot

DEWA launched its Automatic Smart Grid Restoration System in 2022. The application detects a distribution fault, isolates the affected section, and then restores supply to customers on healthy sections through adjacent circuits where network conditions permit. [1][2]

DEWA's 2025 Smart Grid Report states that the FLISR/ASGR capability has reduced restoration time to below one minute. The same report shows that this capability sits inside a much broader operating system involving SCADA monitoring and control, motorised switching equipment, switching-procedure management and field-crew management. [2] A peer-reviewed IEEE review independently supports the underlying fault-location, isolation and restoration logic, but it does not validate DEWA's specific results. [6]

The challenge: a local fault can create a wider service interruption

In a distribution network, one damaged section of a feeder can interrupt customers who are connected to otherwise healthy sections downstream. In a more traditional response model, operators must identify the location, assess the network state, dispatch or coordinate field work, execute switching steps, and then restore supply.

Every minute spent locating the fault or deciding how to reconfigure the network extends the interruption experienced by customers whose local assets may be healthy. The operating question is therefore different from “How fast can we repair the fault?” It is “How quickly can we contain the fault and restore the rest of the network?”

The desired future, then backcasting to today

DEWA's smart-grid strategy evolved from a technology-led approach to a value-driven approach, with a stated ambition for an interconnected network that supports automated operational decision-making around the clock. [3] The intended future is not a grid “without people”; it is a grid that does not have to wait for a sequence of manual actions before every first response to a fault.

Desired future ← required capabilities ← what must be built today ← different way of working ← impact:
A grid that contains faults rapidly ← real-time visibility, remote control, reliable communications and automated decision logic ← intelligent devices, controllable switches, communications, control-centre systems and trusted operational data ← automatic isolation and reconfiguration rather than sequential manual switching ← faster restoration of healthy sections and reduced customer impact.
The 10X MoveMove fault response from “detect the outage, then execute a sequence of manual switching actions” to “identify the affected section, isolate it, and automatically restore healthy sections within network safety and capacity constraints.”

How the capability works

ASGR — Automatic Smart Grid Restoration is DEWA's application for locating a distribution fault, isolating the affected section and automatically restoring supply to healthy sections. [1][4]

FLISR — Fault Location, Isolation and Service Restoration is the operating framework behind that sequence: identify where the fault occurred, disconnect the damaged section, and re-energise as much healthy network as possible through an alternative configuration. Independent academic and professional literature describes the same core logic. [6][7]

SCADA — Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition is the monitoring and control system that gives control centres operational visibility and remote command over grid equipment. DEWA uses SCADA across 33 kV, 11 kV and 6.6 kV primary and secondary substations. [2]

RMU — Ring Main Unit is medium-voltage switching equipment used to sectionalise or reroute distribution supply. DEWA reports replacing units with motorised RMUs to enable self-healing functions. [2]

In a June 2026 operational description, DEWA stated that substations along the feeder are equipped with IED — Intelligent Electronic Device, a device that monitors equipment status and performs protection and control functions, and RTU — Remote Terminal Unit, a field unit that sends site data to the control centre and receives remote operating commands. Modern communication protocols link substations to one another and to the distribution control centre. When a feeder is interrupted, the system isolates the affected section and transfers restorable customers to adjacent circuits. [4]

What “self-healing” does not mean: the system does not physically repair a damaged cable or failed asset. Restoration means isolating the faulted section and re-energising healthy sections that can be supplied through another path. Repairing the cause, field safety and decisions outside automation limits remain human and operational responsibilities.

From idea to operation and scale

DEWA developed its first smart-grid strategy in 2014. Between 2015 and 2017 it automated its transmission network and expanded an RF Mesh communications network that later provided connectivity to more than 4,200 distribution substations. In 2021 the strategy was updated through 2035 and shifted from a technology-led to a value-driven model. [3]

In June 2022 DEWA announced the ASGR launch. Smart Grid reports in 2023, 2024 and 2025 document its integration with distribution automation, smart or motorised RMUs, control systems, switching management and field operations. By 2025 the technical report explicitly stated that restoration time had become less than one minute. [1][2][5]

What is not publicly documented is equally important: the reviewed sources do not disclose the duration of an initial pilot, the number of feeders in an early test cohort, a circuit-level before/after baseline, or the exact learning cycle that preceded wider implementation. Those details should not be inferred from the time between strategy announcements and public launch.

Evidence of impact: what is known and what is not

< 1 minuteRestoration time reported in DEWA Smart Grid Report 2025 for the FLISR/ASGR capability. [2]
0.82 minDEWA-wide CML in 2025, approximately 49 seconds per customer per year. [8]
6.88 → 0.82DEWA-wide CML from 2012 to 2025; the downward trend clearly predates ASGR's 2022 launch. [8]

CML — Customer Minutes Lost is the average duration of electricity interruption experienced by a customer over a year. It is a whole-network reliability indicator, not a metric that belongs to ASGR alone.

After ASGR's launch, DEWA reported CML of 1.19 minutes in 2022, 1.06 in 2023, 0.94 in 2024 and 0.82 in 2025. But the trend was already improving before 2022: it stood at 1.43 minutes in 2021 and 6.88 in 2012. The defensible conclusion is therefore that ASGR became one component of a mature reliability system—not that it alone caused the entire improvement. [8]

CEER's independent benchmarking provides a European framework for continuity-of-supply indicators such as SAIDI/CML, but it does not audit DEWA's specific number or validate its global ranking. [9] EPRI and IEEE support the technical plausibility of rapid FLISR restoration, but they do not evaluate DEWA's implementation directly. [6][7]

Evidence limit: no public independent evaluation was found that measures DEWA ASGR events one by one, restoration success rate, feeder coverage, false-operation rate, or the share of CML reduction attributable solely to ASGR. “Under one minute” therefore remains a DEWA-reported operating result, while CML remains a network-level outcome.

The 10X Lens

01

Future foresight and ambition

The 2035 smart-grid direction established an operating future before the specific application matured, shaping the foundational capabilities that followed.

03

Leadership and future governance

Automated restoration is not a standalone tool. It depends on operating rules, safety limits, control-centre coordination, field responsibilities and trusted data.

05

Reinventing the operating model

The critical change is that part of the restoration sequence moved from sequential manual intervention into automated network decision and action.

07

Maximising institutional and technical intelligence

Real-time visibility, communications, controllable devices and restoration logic are combined so that operational data can become action within a very short time window.

What leaders and organisations can use

Do not start with the algorithm

Start with the decision that must become faster, then identify the data, devices, communications and authority needed to execute it safely.

Separate fault containment from fault repair

The biggest value may come from limiting the blast radius and restoring the rest of the service, even when the original cause still requires field repair.

Measure process and outcome separately

Isolation and restoration time measures the new capability. CML measures the total customer outcome. Treating them as the same metric overstates causality.

Design a safe fallback path

Critical automation needs operating conditions, explicit boundaries, monitoring and a human intervention route when automatic reconfiguration is not appropriate.

Transferability

Critical infrastructure

The principle can transfer to water, telecommunications, airports and data centres: locate the failure, isolate it, and keep the healthy part operating rather than waiting for full repair.

Digital operations

Digital services can apply the same logic by isolating a failing component and shifting load to an alternative path—provided real-time monitoring and controls prevent fault propagation.

The future-focused 10X questionWhat future does your organisation want when a critical service fails? What would that future look like in practice if the system could contain the failure and keep the rest of the service running? If that is the desired future, what data, skills, systems, governance and ways of working must be built today to reach it?
