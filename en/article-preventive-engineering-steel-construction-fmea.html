FMEA · Failure Mode & Effects Analysis · Construction · Occupational Safety · Risk Priority Number

How did risk scores change at a steel construction project after its team ranked hazards with FMEA?

A steel construction project runs six kinds of work at once, including erecting steel frames, building scaffolds, and pouring concrete. Researchers from two Iranian universities listed 54 occupational hazards at one such project. Any safety manager faces the same question: where to start when resources are limited.

The team used Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA), which asks how each hazard could happen and what it would cause. They scored every hazard and acted first on the highest scores. They added training, engineering controls, administrative rules, and protective equipment, then scored the hazards again the same way.

The score for a worker falling during steel erection dropped from 720 to 112. Three other hazards fell from 720 to between 140 and 168. But the study measures scores that the team estimated. It does not report injuries or accidents.

What problem did the project face?

The study covered six kinds of work: steel erection, excavation, rebar work (placing steel reinforcing bars), formwork (building temporary molds for concrete), concrete pouring, and scaffolding. It identified 54 occupational hazards. Steel erection and scaffolding had the largest share, mostly from work at height, crane operations, and material handling. [1]

Four researchers from the Birjand and Shahid Sadoughi (Yazd) Universities of Medical Sciences in Iran wrote the study, led by first author F. Laal. It appeared in Persian in the Occupational Medicine Quarterly Journal. [1] It does not name the project, give its location, or say how long it ran. The authors call the design quasi-experimental, meaning there was no random assignment and no comparison group.

How did the team rank the hazards?

FMEA asks two questions before harm occurs: how could this hazard happen, and what would follow? A failure mode is one way an accident can happen, such as a worker falling from a height. [1]

Each failure mode gets three scores, known as the risk triad (Severity, Occurrence, Detection — S/O/D). Severity is how bad the harm would be. Occurrence is how likely the event is. Detection is how well the team can spot the problem before it causes harm. The study scored each from 1 to 10. A severity of 10 means a catastrophic outcome that may include death. A detection score of 10 means early spotting is almost impossible. [1]

Multiplying the three scores gives the Risk Priority Number (RPN). Its highest possible value is 1,000. The study says the number has no absolute meaning and is only for comparing and ranking. The team ranked hazards from highest to lowest and chose the top ones for action. [1]

What measures were carried out?

For the highest-ranked hazards, the team used four groups of measures. It trained workers for high-risk tasks. It added engineering controls, such as better access systems and protective devices on site. It set administrative rules, such as work permits, instructions, and supervision. It also required workers to wear personal protective equipment. The study says the team followed the hierarchy of controls. [1]

The hierarchy of controls ranks solutions from strongest to weakest: remove the hazard, replace it, add engineering controls, change work rules, and last, issue protective equipment. The US National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) calls protective equipment the least preferred level. It says employers should not rely on it alone when other effective controls exist. [2]

What does the study say drove the drop?

The study says the drop came mainly from lower Occurrence and Detection scores. Events became less likely, and problems became easier to spot (on this scale, a Detection score of 10 means almost impossible to spot). Severity stayed almost the same because the nature of the work did not change. Its examples of better detection include trained operators and supervisors for cranes, and inspection protocols and tags for scaffolds. [1]

A guardrail, for example, makes a fall less likely but not less deadly. This is our reading. The study does not publish the three scores for each hazard, so the split cannot be checked.

What do we not know?

The result rests on a before-and-after comparison at one project with no comparison group. We label it a limited trial. [1]

  • The study measured scores estimated by a team. It did not measure injuries, near misses, or field inspection results.
  • The same team rescored with the same criteria. The study does not say whether raters saw the first scores, how many there were, or how much time passed between rounds.
  • All four groups of measures were applied together, so the effect of each one is unknown.
  • The table covers ten of 54 hazards, and none of them is a formwork hazard. It also lists crane operation as an activity, although the text does not name it among the six kinds of work.
  • The study says falls from height stayed at the top after the measures. Yet in the table, falling loads (225) score higher than every row about people falling (up to 144). We cannot settle this, because the other rows are not shown.

How can you start a small pilot of your own?

  • Pick one activity. List its hazards with the people who do the work and the safety officer.
  • Score each hazard with the risk triad (Severity, Occurrence, Detection — S/O/D). Keep all three scores, not only their product.
  • Act on the top three hazards, starting with the highest level of the hierarchy of controls you can use: removal, then replacement, then engineering controls.
  • Record field data alongside, such as near misses and inspection results. Rescore after a set period, using a rater who has not seen the first scores.

What does this case add to the 10X view?

The study does not show that accidents fell. It does show a practical way to direct safety resources: rank the hazards, act on the highest, score them again, and then ask for field evidence that shows whether the drop is real.

What is your preventive engineering question?

Which hazard in your work has the highest score today? What field evidence, such as a near miss or an inspection result, would show in three months that the new measure really lowered it?
