From Financial Signals to Early Warning: FMEA for Corporate Failure Prediction and Audit Risk

Can a reliability method from engineering strengthen financial prediction? A 2025 study proposes an FMEA-based audit architecture that connects liquidity, profitability, fraud and internal-control evidence to earlier review of corporate failure risk.

What does the paper actually propose?

A 2025 paper in the International Journal of Applied Decision Sciences, by Saeed Askary, Davood Askarany and Yusuf J. Ugras, proposes bringing Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA) into financial auditing. The objective is to make the audit process more preventive: identify ways financial reporting or going-concern assessment can fail, connect them to causes and controls, and use liquidity, profitability and fraud indicators as structured early-warning evidence. [1]

The useful shift is from “what is the ratio?” to a chain of operational questions: what decision can fail, what is the effect, what can cause it, what prevents it, what detects it, and what evidence should trigger action? In finance, the failure mode may be a misleading going-concern conclusion, fictitious revenue, an unexplained cash imbalance or a financial statement that does not reflect the underlying books.

Evidence boundary: the paper proposes a framework, scoring table and worked audit examples. It does not report an out-of-sample predictive experiment showing that FMEA improves corporate-failure forecast accuracy versus an alternative financial model. It is best treated as an auditable early-warning architecture, not a validated standalone forecasting algorithm.

Liquidity and profitability can become warning signals, not just reported outcomes

The authors focus on liquidity — the organization's ability to meet near-term obligations — and profitability — its ability to generate earnings. They recommend using historical company data, industry comparisons and trend analysis across cash, receivables, payables, inventory and other financial items. They also discuss long-run profitability analysis and the use of predictive analytics as part of a more informative going-concern assessment. [1]

The paper cites prior work using Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) neural networks for going-concern prediction. Those are sequence models that can learn patterns over time. Importantly, the paper does not itself validate those models. A model probability should therefore be treated as evidence for review, not automatically translated into an FMEA Occurrence score.

How the paper adapts FMEA to financial auditing

The proposed checklist begins with an account or account group, then identifies an assumed fraud or failure mode, potential causes, symptoms and local effects, the effect on the financial statements, detection provisions, avoidance or corrective action, severity and occurrence. The paper links the checklist to audit guidance including AS 2401 and ISA 240 as cited by the authors. [1]

What changes? “High liquidity risk” is no longer the end of the analysis. The team must connect the risk to a failure mode, cause, current control, detection method and action that can later be tested for effectiveness.

The paper does score Severity, Occurrence and Detection — but governance matters

The study publishes a 1–10 scale for Severity (S), Occurrence (O) and Detection (D). Severity describes business, audit and compliance consequence. Occurrence is linked to progressively higher event rates. Detection is reverse-oriented: higher D means the condition is harder to detect, with 10 representing extremely poor detection capability. [1]

It also uses Risk Priority Number (RPN), calculated as S × O × D, and proposes three bands: 1–89 low, 90–200 moderate, and above 200 high. The authors note that detectability depends heavily on professional audit experience. That creates an important implementation problem: without calibration examples and evidence rules, different auditors can assign very different ratings to the same fact pattern.

Practical control: do not treat the paper's RPN bands as a universal organizational policy. High-severity effects should not disappear behind multiplication, and organizations should use their approved prioritization method — including Action Priority where appropriate — rather than relying on RPN alone.

Prediction probability is not Occurrence

If a statistical model estimates an 18% probability of financial distress within 12 months, that does not mean O should automatically become 2, 3 or any other rating. Model probability ≠ Occurrence score. The prediction target, horizon, calibration, error profile and population fit must be understood before the output becomes evidence in an FMEA review.

Likewise, feature importance is not a root cause. If receivables, profit margin or cash flow are influential predictors, they may be signals or proxies rather than causal mechanisms. Causation still requires audit and operating evidence: contract terms, revenue-recognition practice, collection behavior, control failure or demonstrated manipulation.

One continuous case: profit looks healthy while cash weakens

The case below is an educational construction, not a result reported by the study. The company and numbers are illustrative. The purpose is to translate the paper's concept into a complete preventive-engineering workflow.
1

Define the analysis scope

What are we doing? Protect one specific financial decision.

Case: assess the going-concern conclusion before the annual audit report is signed. Scope includes operating liquidity, revenue quality, receivables, cash flow and the controls that support those judgments.

Common mistake: defining the scope as “the company's financial position” without a decision, horizon or sensitive accounts.

Output: a bounded question: can the entity meet obligations and continue under the approved assessment horizon?
2

Define components and required functions

What are we doing? Convert accounts into functions that must work.

Case: sales must represent real transactions, receivables must be collectible, cash balances must represent available resources, and cash-flow forecasts must use supportable assumptions.

Common mistake: starting with a ratio before stating the function the data are supposed to perform.

Output: account/process → required function map.
3

Separate failure mode, effect and cause

What are we doing? Keep the failure, consequence and mechanism distinct.

Case: failure mode: the going-concern assessment relies on profit that is not converting into cash. Effect: the audit conclusion may understate financial distress. Possible causes: inflated receivables, weak collection terms, premature revenue recognition or overly optimistic cash assumptions.

Common mistake: treating “low liquidity” as the root cause without asking why liquidity deteriorated.

Output: function → failure mode → effect → causes requiring verification.
4

Document current prevention and detection controls

What are we doing? Establish what prevents or detects the problem today.

Case: review revenue controls, credit limits, receivables aging, external confirmation, cash reconciliation, forecast review and audit-committee oversight. A written policy is not an effective control unless there is evidence it operates.

Common mistake: confusing policy existence with control effectiveness.

Output: evidence-backed current controls with clear ownership.
5

Rate S/O/D

What are we doing? Translate evidence into calibrated ratings.

Educational baseline: assume S=9, O=4 and D=4, giving RPN=144. New evidence then appears: liquidity deteriorates, overdue receivables rise, operating cash flow weakens while accounting profit remains positive, and current controls did not surface the divergence early. In the educational reassessment, S remains 9, O rises to 7 and D rises to 7, giving RPN=441.

Common mistake: raising Severity because the signal worsened, or converting a model probability directly into O.

Output: a documented explanation of what changed, what did not, and why.
6

Set treatment priority

What are we doing? Decide whether the new evidence requires escalation before the audit conclusion.

Case: a higher risk rating does not itself prove failure. It triggers stronger procedures: subsequent-cash testing, receivable confirmation, forecast challenge, financing review, covenant analysis and industry comparison.

Common mistake: turning RPN into a final audit opinion.

Output: prioritized verification work before the final decision.
7

Design and implement treatment

What are we doing? Change the cause or the control architecture.

Case: treatment may include tighter revenue-recognition controls, credit limits, monthly quality-of-earnings review, an integrated profit/cash/aging dashboard, independent challenge of going-concern assumptions and automatic escalation when approved thresholds are breached.

Common mistake: adding another metric without changing who reviews it, who decides and when escalation occurs.

Output: preventive and detective barriers with owner, timing and escalation rule.
8

Verify effectiveness and reassess

What are we doing? Prove the control system detects risk earlier and improves decision quality.

Educational post-action example: if collections improve, unexplained exceptions fall and the new controls surface abnormal patterns earlier, an illustrative reassessment might be S=9, O=4 and D=3, giving RPN=108. Those scores must never be lowered in real use without measured evidence.

Common mistake: reducing risk immediately because a dashboard was installed.

Output: before/after data on lead time, misses, false alarms and residual risk.
9

Keep the model alive

What are we doing? Reopen analysis when business conditions or model performance change.

Case: review triggers include new revenue policies, market entry, credit-policy shifts, refinancing, supply-chain disruption, collection deterioration or measurable drift in the prediction model itself.

Common mistake: freezing thresholds and risk ratings while the business model changes.

Output: a living link between financial signals, audit review and going-concern decisions.

How should prediction be integrated without creating an automatic judgment?

Financial & operating data→Trend analysis or predictive model→Risk signal→Auditor/expert verification→FMEA reassessment→Action and effectiveness evidence

A predictive model can serve as an early sensor, not the final authority. Data quality, calibration and context must be reviewed before the signal changes an FMEA rating. If machine learning is used, the pilot should track not only overall accuracy but also precision, recall/sensitivity, false alarms, missed distress cases and warning lead time. A model that floods auditors with false positives can consume capacity without improving assurance.

A small pilot for finance or internal audit

Begin with one consequential decision, such as going concern or revenue quality, instead of applying FMEA to every financial statement line at once. The team should combine audit, finance, risk, internal control and data analytics, with a clearly named decision owner.

What the paper establishes — and what it does not

The paper establishes a plausible architecture for connecting financial accounts, fraud modes, internal controls and FMEA-style risk ratings. It provides explicit examples for revenue, cash, financial-statement fraud and going concern, together with a proposed 1–10 S/O/D scale and RPN bands.

It does not empirically demonstrate fewer corporate failures, higher predictive accuracy, or superiority over Altman-style distress models, regression, LSTM, GRU or other forecasting methods. It also does not externally validate its RPN thresholds. In professional use, “failure prediction” should therefore mean a data-supported early-warning and review system, not a claim that FMEA alone forecasts insolvency.

What finance adds to preventive engineering

In financial systems, failure may not be a broken component. It can be a wrong decision, a number that no longer represents economic reality, a control that fails to detect misstatement, or a prediction signal that never reaches an accountable owner. The protected function is decision quality itself.

The operating chain becomes: financial data → signal → verification → failure mode → effect → cause → control → S/O/D → priority → action → effectiveness evidence. That is the point at which financial analytics becomes part of a preventive system rather than another reporting layer.

The Preventive Engineering question

If reported profit remains strong while cash conversion and collection begin to weaken, does your organization have a defined rule for when that signal becomes a risk review, who validates it, what control changes, and how the decision is later tested for effectiveness — or does the signal remain a metric that everyone notices and no one owns?
