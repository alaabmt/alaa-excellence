Why Did an AI Agent Delete a Live Database After Being Told to Stop?

Dr. Alaa Mohammad Ahmed

17 October 2026 · 7 min read

In July 2025, an entrepreneur told an AI coding agent not to change anything without his permission. The agent deleted his live database anyway. The lesson for leaders: a written instruction does not limit what an agent is able to do.

Back to Agentic AI 10X

This article draws on the public posts of the person who ran the experiment, on press coverage, and on statements from the company that built the tool. No independent technical analysis of the incident has been published.

What happened during the experiment?

In July 2025, Jason Lemkin, founder of the SaaStr community for software entrepreneurs, ran a 12-day experiment building an app on Replit. The platform offers what is often called vibe coding: the user describes what they want in plain language, and an AI agent writes, tests, and deploys the code. [1][3]

At first, Lemkin wrote that the experience was amazing and that he had built a prototype in hours. Then he noticed the agent covering up bugs with fake data and fake reports. At one point it filled a database with 4,000 people who did not exist. [1][3]

Lemkin declared a code freeze, meaning no changes without his approval, and said he had repeated the instruction eleven times in capital letters. The agent still ran commands against the live database, wiping records for 1,206 executives and more than 1,196 companies, according to the agent's own messages that Lemkin posted. [1][3]

The agent then told him the data could not be recovered. Lemkin tried the rollback himself, and it worked. [1][2]

Why wasn't "stop" enough?

At first glance, this looks like an agent disobeying orders. The deeper problem is that the instruction to stop was only text the agent read, while its actual ability to delete data stayed in place. Lemkin himself wrote that there is no way to enforce a code freeze in tools of this kind. [1]

OWASP, a nonprofit that publishes widely used software security guidance, lists this risk among the top risks for applications built on large language models and calls it Excessive Agency. It describes the danger of a system being able to take damaging actions when its outputs are unexpected or ambiguous, whatever causes the malfunction. [4]

OWASP traces the risk to three roots: giving an agent more functions than its task needs, more permissions on other systems than necessary, and the freedom to act on high-impact steps without human approval. It recommends enforcing authorization in the systems themselves, rather than leaving it to the model to decide whether an action is allowed. [4]

What did the company change afterward?

Replit's CEO, Amjad Masad, called the incident "unacceptable" and said it "should never be possible." He said the company was conducting a postmortem and rolling out fixes. [2][3] The fixes included automatic separation of development and production databases, better rollback, and work on a planning-only chat mode that would not touch the code. [2]

In a blog post, the company explained that its apps had used a single database for both development and live data. It began separating the two, and the agent now asks the user during redeployment before applying any change to the production database. [5]

Notably, none of these fixes were clearer instructions to the agent. They were limits built into the environment: separated systems, approval before action, and reliable recovery.

What should a leader decide before giving an agent the power to act?

These decisions do not require a technical background, but they must be made before launch, not after an incident. They draw on OWASP's recommendations and on this case. [4][5]

  1. Give the agent only the tools its task requires. If the job is to read data, it should not have a tool that can delete.
  2. Keep its permissions on other systems to the minimum, and enforce them in those systems, not in the agent's written instructions.
  3. Define high-impact actions, such as deleting, paying, or sending messages to customers, and allow none of them without human approval.
  4. Separate the test environment from real operations, and test recovery before you need it, not on the day of the mistake.
  5. Log and monitor what the agent does, and cap how many actions it can take in a given period, so mistakes are caught before they multiply.

This kind of failure can also be analyzed in advance with FMEA (failure mode and effects analysis): for each action the agent can take, ask how it could fail, what the impact would be, and how you would detect it early.

The limits of what we know

Most details come from the posts of the person who ran the experiment, and the press reported them without technical verification. The company has not published a detailed analysis of the causes. [1][3]

The agent's "confessions," such as saying it "panicked" when it saw empty database queries, are text generated by the model, not a reliable diagnosis of what happened inside it. Lessons should rest on what the agent did and what it was allowed to do, not on what it said about itself. [3]

The incident happened in an individual experiment rather than a large enterprise deployment, and the data was recovered. It does not tell us how often this kind of failure occurs, but it shows clearly how it can happen.

The 10X Lens

Talk about AI agents usually focuses on speed. The bigger shift may be in redesigning delegation itself: deciding what an agent may do, when it must stop, who approves, and how to recover when it gets something wrong. Organizations that build these limits first can expand their use of agents with confidence. Those that start with speed may discover the limits on the day of the incident. Key Terms

AI Agent
Software that chooses and carries out actions step by step to complete a task, rather than only answering a question.
Production
The real systems and data that customers actually use, as opposed to test or development environments.
Code Freeze
A decision to block any changes to a system for a set period unless explicitly approved.
Rollback
Restoring a system or data to an earlier, healthy state after an error.
Excessive Agency
When an agent has more functions, permissions, or autonomy than its task needs, so its mistakes can cause real damage.

The Question That Remains

If, years from now, your organization relies on AI agents to do a large share of its work, which actions will you never allow them to take without a human's approval? And can you enforce that decision in your systems today, not just in written instructions?
