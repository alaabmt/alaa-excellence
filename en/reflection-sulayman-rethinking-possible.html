10X Reflections from the Qur’an | Surat An-Naml

Sulayman: When Our Idea of Possible Changes

Is the goal impossible—or are we limited by the way we have always worked?

العربية

Methodological note: This contemporary reflection draws on the Qur’anic account of Sulayman and the Queen of Sheba's throne in An-Naml 27:38–40, in light of classical tafsir. It neither offers a new tafsir nor attributes modern management concepts to the Qur’an.

“[Solomon] said, ‘O assembly [of jinn], which of you will bring me her throne before they come to me in submission?’”

An-Naml 27:38 — Saheeh International

“That is impossible.” Sometimes the objection is reasonable: impossible with the current resources, procedures, or timetable. But “We cannot do it this way” is different from “It cannot be done.” The first invites us to reconsider our capabilities; the second can end the conversation prematurely.

The account of Sulayman invites reflection on two connected questions: How do we understand the boundaries of possibility, and how do we respond to blessings when extraordinary results arrive?

One Request, Two Very Different Answers

In An-Naml 27:39, a powerful jinn offers to bring the throne before Sulayman rises from his place. In verse 40, one described as having knowledge from the Scripture offers to bring it before his glance returns. The request is the same; the timeframes differ dramatically.

We must preserve the nature of the account: Sulayman was a prophet granted extraordinary dominion by Allah, and this was an extraordinary event by Allah's permission. It is not a repeatable management method, nor proof that any impossible outcome can be achieved by thinking differently.

Yet it raises a useful question: How often do we confuse the limits of our present capabilities with the limits of what is possible?

When Improving the Old Process Is Not Enough

Imagine a service that takes ten days. Teams remove approvals and simplify procedures, reducing the wait to eight, six, then four days. That is worthwhile progress.

But what if someone asks why the customer must submit information the organization already holds? Could the service, where lawful and appropriate, be delivered proactively once eligibility is established?

That would not merely accelerate the old process. It would reconsider whether parts of it should exist. This is the distinction between incremental improvement and reinvention.

In Tamayuz 10X, the question is not simply how to make existing work faster. It is: “If we started with the outcome people need, would we design the work in the same way?”

But the most important part of Sulayman's scene comes after the throne arrives.

“This Is from the Favor of My Lord” — Where Does Strength Come From?

“This is from the favor of my Lord to test me whether I will be grateful or ungrateful.”

An-Naml 27:40 — Saheeh International

When the throne stood before Sulayman, he did not attribute the extraordinary result to his kingdom or power. He said, “This is from the favor of my Lord.”

Consider the words “my Lord” (rabbī). Allah is the Creator, Owner, Sustainer, and Disposer of all affairs. Who gave us the minds with which we think, the health with which we work, and the patience to persevere? Who made learning possible, brought helpful people into our lives, and provided the resources we use? It is Allah, Lord of all worlds.

We see our effort but can overlook the blessings that made effort possible. This awareness also grounds human dignity: we are servants of Allah, not slaves to the whims of other people. We respect others, acknowledge their contributions, and listen to sincere criticism, but neither the approval of the arrogant nor the discouragement of others should define our resolve.

We take practical steps and rely upon Allah, recognizing that our abilities and resources come through His favor. Remembering “my Lord” keeps our strength from turning into arrogance and frees us from being ruled by others' opinions.

“Will I Be Grateful or Ungrateful?” — The Test of Blessing

Classical commentators, including al-Tabari and Ibn Kathir, explain the contrast here as gratitude for Allah's blessing versus denying or failing to acknowledge that blessing. In this context, the Arabic word akfur concerns ingratitude and denial of divine favor; it does not mean that every failure to express thanks is disbelief that takes someone outside Islam.

Our knowledge, strength, skills, resources, opportunities, and successes are all blessings from Allah. We plan, study, work, and make decisions, but our efforts do not make us independent of His favor and enabling grace.

We should not claim the credit for ourselves alone, or act as though our abilities and resources exist apart from Allah's blessings. Ingratitude can appear as arrogance, forgetfulness of the Giver, or treating an entrusted blessing as an entitlement to use without responsibility.

The deeper question is therefore not only “What did we achieve?” but “How did we respond to what Allah gave us the ability to achieve?”

Ambition Is Not Independence from Allah

Strategies, indicators, resources, and outcomes matter. Taking practical steps is necessary. But we take the means while recognizing that success and guidance come from Allah.

Ambition does not conflict with reliance upon Him, and planning does not remove the need to seek His help. We work carefully, learn continuously, and seek better methods while asking Allah for guidance, blessing, and beneficial outcomes.

When results come, we remember: “This is from the favor of my Lord.” We thank Allah, acknowledge everyone who contributed, and use the capabilities and resources entrusted to us for good.

The 10X Question

The Qur’anic verses do not present a modern management framework. But reflection on this account leaves us with two complementary questions:

Are we allowing today's methods to define the limits of our ambition, or seeking different capabilities and approaches that create greater value?

And if we achieve remarkable results, will we become more grateful to Allah—or so absorbed in our own capabilities that we forget their Giver?

In Tamayuz 10X, we pursue foresight, capability building, reinvention, and greater benefit to people and society. Yet knowledge, resources, opportunities, and the ability to effect change are blessings to be acknowledged and used responsibly.

True excellence is not only achieving exceptional results. It is becoming more grateful, more humble, and more responsible with Allah's blessings as those results grow.

Conclusion: Never Forget the Source of the Blessing

The scene begins with an extraordinary request and two very different offers. It reaches its deepest point when Sulayman sees the throne before him. His final emphasis is not speed or power, but Allah's favor and the test of gratitude:

“This is from the favor of my Lord to test me whether I will be grateful or ungrateful.”

An-Naml 27:40 — Saheeh International

Recognize the achievement without forgetting who enabled it. Celebrate without arrogance. Use the blessing to benefit others rather than letting it distract you from its Giver.

When Allah opens a door you never expected, will your first thought be the greatness of your achievement—or the favor of Allah that made it possible?
