Sulayman: When Our Idea of Possible Changes

Is the goal impossible—or are we limited by the way we have always worked?

العربية

Methodological note: This is a contemporary reflection inspired by the Qur’anic narrative and informed by meanings established in classical tafsir. It does not propose a new tafsir or attribute modern management concepts to the Qur’anic text.

“[Solomon] said, ‘O assembly [of jinn], which of you will bring me her throne before they come to me in submission?’”

An-Naml 27:38 — Saheeh International

“That is impossible.” How often does a meeting end with those words?

Sometimes the objection is perfectly reasonable. It may be impossible with today's tools, procedures, staffing, or deadlines. But “We cannot do it this way” is not the same statement as “It cannot be done.”

One Request, Two Very Different Answers

In Surat An-Naml, Sulayman asks who can bring the Queen of Sheba's throne before her arrival. One powerful jinn offers to bring it before Sulayman rises from his place (27:39). Then someone described as having knowledge from the Scripture offers to bring it before his glance returns (27:40).

The request is the same. The timeframes are radically different.

This is a Qur’anic account of Sulayman, a prophet granted extraordinary dominion by Allah, and of an extraordinary event occurring by Allah's permission. It is not a transferable management technique, nor a promise that every apparently impossible task can be achieved through creative thinking.

Yet it can make us examine an ordinary mistake: How often do we mistake the limits of our present capabilities for the limits of what is possible?

Sometimes a Better Process Is Not Enough

Imagine a public service that takes ten days. A team removes approvals, simplifies forms, and reduces the wait to eight days, then six, then four. That is meaningful progress.

Now imagine a different question: Why does the resident need to submit information the government already holds? What if, where appropriate and lawful, the service could be provided proactively when eligibility is established?

That is not simply the old process running faster. It is a reconsideration of whether parts of the process should exist at all.

10X ambition asks more than “How can we do today's work ten times faster?” It asks: “If we started with the outcome people need, would we design the work the same way?”

The Story Does Not End With Speed

“This is from the favor of my Lord to test me whether I will be grateful or ungrateful.”

An-Naml 27:40 — Saheeh International

When Sulayman sees the throne before him, he does not make the achievement a monument to himself. He recognizes Allah's favor—and recognizes the favor as a test.

That changes the question. It is no longer only “What can we achieve?” It is also “What will achievement do to us?”

Capability can grow while humility shrinks. Results can improve while gratitude fades. Success can become an excuse to overlook those who made it possible.

The greater the capability, the more important the character of the person entrusted with it.

Two Questions for Leaders

First: Have we reconsidered what is possible? Did we start with the outcome, or with our familiar method? Have we explored new knowledge, skills, partnerships, and operating models?

Second: What will success make of us? More grateful to Allah? More generous in acknowledging others? More committed to serving people? Or more absorbed in our own reputation?

An organization may reinvent its operating model while its leadership still needs to learn how to carry success.

The 10X Question

The verses are not an administrative framework, and human innovation must never be equated with the extraordinary event granted by Allah in Sulayman's story. But reflection leads to a contemporary challenge:

Are we allowing today's way of working to define our ambition, or are we redesigning the work around the value we want to create?

In Tamayuz 10X, foresight and ambition must be followed by capability building and reinvention—not merely incremental optimization.

And when something remarkable becomes possible, Sulayman's words remain the more demanding lesson: “This is from the favor of my Lord.”

Capability is a gift. Achievement carries responsibility. Success is a test.

What goal have you called impossible only because you keep approaching it in the old way? And if Allah opens a door you never expected, will your response be gratitude or self-admiration?
