Nuh: Build Before the Need Becomes Obvious

What capability will tomorrow require that you need to start building today?

العربية

Methodological note: This is a contemporary reflection inspired by the Qur’anic narrative and informed by meanings established in classical tafsir. It does not propose a new tafsir or attribute contemporary management concepts to the Qur’anic text.

“And he constructed the ship, and whenever an assembly of the eminent of his people passed by him, they ridiculed him.”

Hud 11:38 — Saheeh International

Some forms of preparation look unnecessary until the moment they become indispensable.

You invest time, money, attention, and effort into something the present does not yet seem to require. From the outside, the obvious question is: Why build this now?

The harder question comes from within: Can you continue preparing when today offers little visible proof that the preparation will matter?

Building Under a Different Kind of Certainty

The Qur’anic scene begins with a command:

“And construct the ship under Our observation and Our inspiration…”

Hud 11:37 — Saheeh International

Nuh is not forecasting in the way an executive, investor, or government forecasts. He is a prophet acting on revelation and divine command. That distinction matters. Our plans are fallible; revelation is not.

But the scene still confronts us with a human question: Must a need become visible before preparation begins?

The people passing Nuh could see the ship. What they could not see was the future in which the ship would matter.

That gap is familiar. Organizations postpone a capability because current operations can still function without it. Professionals postpone learning because their existing skills still work. Leaders postpone succession because the current team is still in place.

Then the future arrives, and the question changes. It is no longer, “Should we build this?” It becomes, “Why did we wait so long?”

A City Builds Ahead

Consider Dubai Metro as a separate contemporary example, not as an analogy to the prophetic event.

When the Metro opened in 2009, Dubai was still a heavily car-oriented city. The project attracted debate over cost, adoption, disruption, and whether the investment would prove worthwhile. Those were legitimate questions; large public investments should face scrutiny.

Fifteen years later, the scale looks different. Dubai’s Roads and Transport Authority reported roughly 2.4 billion riders since launch, average daily ridership of about 730,000, and growth from 20,000 riders on 10 September 2009 to 767,000 on 3 September 2024. The network itself expanded from 52 to 90 kilometres and from 10 to 53 stations.

The lesson is not that every ambitious project will succeed, or that criticism should be ignored. It is more disciplined: tomorrow’s required capacity cannot always be measured by today’s demand.

The Capability Lag

Many capabilities have a long lead time.

You cannot create experienced leaders the week you need them. You cannot establish trustworthy data governance the morning an AI system needs reliable data. You cannot build a culture of experimentation after disruption has already made experimentation urgent.

This creates a dangerous lag: by the time the need is obvious, the capability may still be years away.

So foresight is not only about seeing what might happen. It is about asking what would have to be true of us if that future arrived.

The 10X Question

The Qur’anic verses are not a management framework, and the ship in Nuh’s story is not an organizational metaphor. But after the reflection, a contemporary question emerges naturally:

If we have a view of the future we want to reach, what capabilities must begin taking shape now?

In Tamayuz 10X, foresight and ambition are followed by the work of identifying and building the capabilities the future requires. That might mean talent, technology, infrastructure, partnerships, institutional knowledge, or a different operating model.

The point is not to chase every trend. It is to make a harder distinction: What can wait, and what takes so long to build that waiting itself becomes the risk?

Some of what protects tomorrow has to be built while it still looks optional today.

So ask yourself: What is your “ship” — the capability you need to begin building before everyone can see why it will matter?
