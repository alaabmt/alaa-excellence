10X Reflections from the Qur’an | Surat Hud

Nuh: Build Before the Need Becomes Obvious

What capability will tomorrow require that you need to start building today?

العربية

Methodological note: This is a contemporary reflection inspired by the Qur’anic narrative and informed by meanings established in classical tafsir. It does not propose a new tafsir or attribute contemporary management concepts to the Qur’anic text.

“And he constructed the ship, and whenever an assembly of the eminent of his people passed by him, they ridiculed him.”

Hud 11:38 — Saheeh International

Some forms of preparation look unnecessary until the moment they become indispensable.

You invest time, money, attention, and effort into something the present does not yet seem to require. From the outside, the obvious question is: Why build this now?

The harder question comes from within: Can you continue preparing when today offers little visible proof that the preparation will matter?

Building Under a Different Kind of Certainty

The Qur’anic scene begins with a command:

“And construct the ship under Our observation and Our inspiration…”

Hud 11:37 — Saheeh International

Nuh is not forecasting in the way an executive, investor, or government forecasts. He is a prophet acting on revelation and divine command. That distinction matters. Our plans are fallible; revelation is not.

But the scene still confronts us with a human question: Must a need become visible before preparation begins?

The people passing Nuh could see the ship. What they could not see was the future in which the ship would matter.

That gap is familiar. Organizations postpone a capability because current operations can still function without it. Professionals postpone learning because their existing skills still work. Leaders postpone succession because the current team is still in place.

Then the future arrives, and the question changes. It is no longer, “Should we build this?” It becomes, “Why did we wait so long?”

When Leaders See What Doubters Cannot Yet See

Dubai Metro did not open to universal applause. In 2009, reporting by outlets including the Los Angeles Times and The Christian Science Monitor questioned costs, whether people would leave their cars, and the usefulness of a network that initially opened only part of its planned stations.

Those were questions worth asking. But Dubai's leadership was considering a larger picture: not only how many people needed a metro that day, but what kind of city Dubai intended to become—and what infrastructure that future would require.

The evidence did not take fifteen years to begin emerging. About 20,000 passengers used the Metro on 10 September 2009. Ridership then grew as the network and city expanded. By September 2024, the Roads and Transport Authority reported more than 2.4 billion cumulative journeys, with 767,000 passengers on a single day.

Criticism does not prove a vision is wrong. Conviction alone does not prove it is right. The distinction is whether leaders can listen, test their assumptions, act with purpose, and let results inform their next decisions.

Returning to Nuh's Story

“And he constructed the ship, and whenever an assembly of the eminent of his people passed by him, they ridiculed him.”

Hud 11:38 — Saheeh International

Notice the action: he continued building while others mocked him.

Nuh was a prophet acting on divine revelation, not a human leader relying on an uncertain forecast. We must never equate revelation with strategic confidence. Yet the scene invites us to examine our response to doubt: When you have a considered vision, do other people's inability to see it become a reason to abandon it?

Some criticism reveals risks you have missed. It deserves attention. Other objections reflect the limits of what observers can imagine today. Leadership requires the judgment to distinguish between them.

The 10X Question

Can we be clear enough about our strategic ambition to learn from criticism without becoming captive to it?

A leader does not need universal applause before beginning. A leader needs a reasoned direction, evidence, the capabilities to execute, honest measures of progress, and the willingness to correct course.

Do not let other people's doubt stop you building your future. But do not let your belief in your vision prevent you from examining it.

Between those two errors lies the discipline of leadership: know where you are going, keep building, and allow results to speak.
