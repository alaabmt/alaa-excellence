What Did the Garden Owners Leave Out of Their Plan?

When a plan gets every detail right but forgets the will of Allah and the people waiting at the gate

Methodological note: This is a contemporary reflection on verses from Surah Al-Qalam (68:17–33), read in light of the classical commentaries. It does not offer a new interpretation of the verses, and it does not attribute modern management concepts to the Qur’an.

Have you ever worked on a plan that looked solid from every side? The date was fixed, the team agreed, and no one doubted it could be done. Then the result was nothing like what anyone expected. The team went looking for the step where things went wrong, and no one asked what the plan had left out from the very start.

Surah Al-Qalam tells a short story about the owners of a garden who made a plan like that. Allah, glorified and exalted is He, tells it in the course of addressing the idolaters of Quraysh. Al-Tabari explains the meaning as: We tested them as We tested “the owners of the orchard.” [1]

﴿إِنَّا بَلَوْنَاهُمْ كَمَا بَلَوْنَا أَصْحَابَ الْجَنَّةِ إِذْ أَقْسَمُوا لَيَصْرِمُنَّهَا مُصْبِحِينَ﴾ ﴿وَلَا يَسْتَثْنُونَ﴾Meaning in English: “Indeed, We have tried them as We tried the companions of the garden, when they swore to cut its fruit in the [early] morning” “Without making exception.”Surah Al-Qalam, 68:17–18

The garden was full of fruit and ready for harvest, so its owners swore to pick it early the next morning. But the verses tell us what happened while they knew nothing of it: So there came upon it [i.e., the garden] an affliction from your Lord while they were asleep. And it became as though reaped. Al-Qalam, 68:19–20 In the morning they called to one another and set off for their garden, speaking in low voices and urging each other:

﴿أَن لَّا يَدْخُلَنَّهَا الْيَوْمَ عَلَيْكُم مِّسْكِينٌ﴾Meaning in English: “There will surely not enter it today upon you [any] poor person.”Surah Al-Qalam, 68:24

And they went early in determination, [assuming themselves] able. Al-Qalam, 68:25 When they saw the garden, they said, Indeed, we are lost. Then they understood what had happened: Rather, we have been deprived. Al-Qalam, 68:26–27

What did the commentators say about this scene?

Al-Tabari explains “without making exception” as meaning that they “did not say, ‘If Allah wills,’” and notes that the scholars of interpretation said much the same. [2] Al-Qurtubi reports this view, then adds another from Ikrimah: that they did “not set aside the right of the poor.” [3]

Ibn Kathir explains that they swore to pick the fruit at night “so that no poor person or beggar would know about them, so that all the fruit would be theirs and they would give none of it in charity.” [4] Al-Saadi says they whispered these words “for fear that someone would hear them and tell the poor.” [5]

Al-Tabari also reports from Qatadah and Ikrimah that the garden had belonged to their father, who used to give charity from it and feed the poor. When he died, his sons decided to keep the poor out. [2] This is a report from some of the early scholars about the background of the story. The verses themselves do not mention it.

On the word “able,” al-Tabari lists what the early scholars said about the phrase translated “in determination,” then gives his preferred reading: they went out “upon a matter they had intended and resolved on, and kept secret among themselves, able to do it in their own estimation.” [6] Al-Saadi puts it this way: “certain of their ability over it.” [7]

When they saw the garden, Ibn Kathir says, they thought they had taken the wrong road to it. [8] Then they were sure it was their garden, and said we have been deprived, meaning “we have no share and no portion.” [9] Al-Qurtubi mentions another reading of “we are lost”: “we have strayed from what is right in setting out with the intention of keeping out the poor.” [10]

When a person thinks their ability is their own

In that moment of confusion, one voice rises from among them:

﴿قَالَ أَوْسَطُهُمْ أَلَمْ أَقُل لَّكُمْ لَوْلَا تُسَبِّحُونَ﴾Meaning in English: The most moderate of them said, “Did I not say to you, ‘Why do you not exalt [Allāh]?’”Surah Al-Qalam, 68:28

Ibn Kathir reports from Ibn Abbas, Mujahid and others that “the most moderate of them” means “the most just and the best of them.” He reports from Mujahid, al-Suddi and Ibn Jurayj that “why do you not exalt Allah” means: why do you not make the exception? He then mentions another view: why do you not glorify Allah and thank Him for what He has given you? [11]

Al-Saadi’s comment on this verse carries a deep point of faith. To glorify Allah is to declare Him free of anything unworthy of Him, “and part of that is your belief that your ability is independent.” [12] Al-Qurtubi says the words “Did I not say to you” show that this man “had told them to make the exception, and they did not obey him.” [13]

We might reflect that the fault was not that they planned and made up their minds. Planning and resolve are part of taking the means. The fault was that the plan rested on a certainty of their own ability that left out the will of Allah, glorified and exalted is He, and that it shut the gate on the poor. The blessing in their hands was itself a test, just as the verses began: Indeed, We have tried them, meaning, We tested them. [4]

Did we take the wrong road, or set out with the wrong aim?

There is a detail in the scene worth pausing on, as a reflection rather than an interpretation. The first thing the owners said when they saw the garden was Indeed, we are lost. They thought they had taken the wrong road. That is often our first explanation when something unexpected happens: we look for a mistake in one of the steps. We blame the road, not the destination.

Then they reached a deeper understanding: Rather, we have been deprived. They had not taken the wrong road at all. They had arrived at their own garden. Anyone who has reviewed a project that failed may recognize the question in that shift. Do we only look for the step where we went wrong, or do we also ask about the aim the whole plan was built on?

Who is left outside the gate in our plans?

What follows are questions from modern working life, not an interpretation of the verses. Still, the words There will surely not enter it today upon you [any] poor person may lead us to ask of every plan we make: who will not be able to come through its gate?

A hypothetical example: a public service organization decides to move all appointment booking to a smartphone app, to cut queues and lower costs. The plan is carried out well. Waiting times fall, running costs drop, and the reports show good numbers. But a number of older people, and people without smartphones, quietly stop coming. Their absence shows up in no report, because the reports only count the people who came through the gate.

When the team notices that fewer people are coming from some neighborhoods, its first move is to look for a bug in the app. Yet a receptionist had warned from the start that some clients could not use it. Her warning never made it into the plan.

This team should not be compared with the owners of the garden. They agreed in secret to keep the poor out, while this team never meant to exclude anyone. Even so, the question the scene raises still stands: who has been left outside the gate without our noticing?

Excellence that goes beyond small improvements may start with that question. A small improvement fixes the bug and polishes the app. It is a bit like looking for the right road to the garden. A redesign goes back to the aim itself: who is this service for, and how does it reach everyone who needs it, through the app for those who can use it and another way for those who cannot? That may call for abilities the organization has not built yet, such as knowing who is not reaching it, not only who is, and hearing the first warning before it turns into a silent absence.

What do we do when a plan fails?

The scene does not end with the loss. After the reminder from the most moderate of them, They said, “Exalted is our Lord! Indeed, we were wrongdoers.” Al-Qalam, 68:29 Al-Tabari explains their wrong as lying “in our leaving out the exception in our oath, and our resolve to stop feeding the poor from the fruit of our garden.” [14]

Then they approached one another, blaming each other Al-Qalam, 68:30, and al-Qurtubi says each would tell the other, “No, it was you who advised us to do this.” [15] But blame was not the last thing they said. They turned to their Lord in hope:

﴿عَسَىٰ رَبُّنَا أَن يُبْدِلَنَا خَيْرًا مِّنْهَا إِنَّا إِلَىٰ رَبِّنَا رَاغِبُونَ﴾Meaning in English: “Perhaps our Lord will substitute for us [one] better than it. Indeed, we are toward our Lord desirous.”Surah Al-Qalam, 68:32

The commentators differ on what became of them after that. Ibn Kathir gives two views: that they hoped Allah would give them something better in this world, or that they sought its reward in the hereafter. He then adds, “and Allah knows best.” [16] Al-Saadi’s view is that if they were sincere in what they said, it seems Allah gave them something better in this world. [17] Al-Qurtubi reports that al-Hasan did not commit himself on their state, and reports from al-Qushayri that most scholars held that they “repented and became sincere.” [18]

There is a matter of faith to reflect on here: the way back to Allah stays open after a mistake, and admitting the wrong, glorifying Allah and hoping in Him do a person more good than being consumed by blame. Even so, the story closes with the words Such is the punishment [of this world]. And the punishment of the Hereafter is greater, if they only knew. Al-Qalam, 68:33 Al-Tabari explains that what happened to the garden is how Allah, glorified and exalted is He, deals in this world with those who defy His command and reject His messengers, and that the punishment of the hereafter is greater still. [19] The scene is a serious warning before it is a lesson in planning.

The 10X excellence question

Who do you want your service to reach in the years ahead?

Imagine your organization some years from now, with its service reaching everyone who needs it, not only those who find it easy to get there. What would that look like in daily work? Who is standing outside the gate today without appearing in your reports? And what do you need to start building now so that you hear the first warning before it turns into a silent absence?

A final reflection

The most moderate of them asked the others: Did I not say to you, ‘Why do you not exalt [Allāh]?’ It is a short sentence, and it reminds us that however carefully a plan is made, it still depends on the will of Allah, glorified and exalted is He. The blessings in our hands are a test, not a guarantee we hold through our own ability.

What we might carry from this scene is to say “if Allah wills” and mean it, and to ask before every plan: who will be left outside its gate? And when we get it wrong, to admit it and turn back to Allah, as they did: Indeed, we are toward our Lord desirous.
