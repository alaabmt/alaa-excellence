What Did the Garden Owners Leave Out of Their Plan?

When a plan gets every detail right but forgets the will of Allah and the people waiting at the gate

Methodological note: This is a contemporary reflection on verses from Surah Al-Qalam (68:17–33), read in light of the classical commentaries. It does not offer a new interpretation of the verses, and it does not attribute modern management ideas to the Qur’an.

The garden was full of fruit, and harvest time had come. Its owners agreed on a time that would not slip: early the next morning. Then they swore to it:

﴿إِنَّا بَلَوْنَاهُمْ كَمَا بَلَوْنَا أَصْحَابَ الْجَنَّةِ إِذْ أَقْسَمُوا لَيَصْرِمُنَّهَا مُصْبِحِينَ﴾ ﴿وَلَا يَسْتَثْنُونَ﴾Meaning in English: “Indeed, We have tried them as We tried the companions of the garden, when they swore to cut its fruit in the [early] morning” “Without making exception.”Surah Al-Qalam, 68:17–18

They went to sleep with the plan complete. The time was set, the road was known, and the fruit was waiting. But the night held something the plan had not counted on:

﴿فَطَافَ عَلَيْهَا طَائِفٌ مِّن رَّبِّكَ وَهُمْ نَائِمُونَ﴾ ﴿فَأَصْبَحَتْ كَالصَّرِيمِ﴾Meaning in English: “So there came upon it [i.e., the garden] an affliction from your Lord while they were asleep.” “And it became as though reaped.”Surah Al-Qalam, 68:19–20

Then dawn came. They called to one another: Go early to your crop if you would cut the fruit. Al-Qalam, 68:22 And they left. No one raised his voice on the way. They spoke in whispers:

﴿فَانطَلَقُوا وَهُمْ يَتَخَافَتُونَ﴾ ﴿أَن لَّا يَدْخُلَنَّهَا الْيَوْمَ عَلَيْكُم مِّسْكِينٌ﴾Meaning in English: “So they set out, while lowering their voices,” “[Saying], ‘There will surely not enter it today upon you [any] poor person.’”Surah Al-Qalam, 68:23–24

They walked with confident steps, sure that the matter was in their hands: And they went early in determination, [assuming themselves] able. Al-Qalam, 68:25 Then they arrived, and in front of them lay black ground with no fruit at all. The first thing they said was Indeed, we are lost. Then they looked again and recognized the place: Rather, we have been deprived. Al-Qalam, 68:26–27

In that moment of shock, one voice rose from among them:

﴿قَالَ أَوْسَطُهُمْ أَلَمْ أَقُل لَّكُمْ لَوْلَا تُسَبِّحُونَ﴾Meaning in English: The most moderate of them said, “Did I not say to you, ‘Why do you not exalt [Allāh]?’”Surah Al-Qalam, 68:28

This time the answer was glorification and confession: They said, “Exalted is our Lord! Indeed, we were wrongdoers.” Al-Qalam, 68:29

The blessing was a test before it was a harvest

The story opens with a word that carries its whole meaning: We have tried them. The fruitful garden was not a gift they could take for granted. It was a test of what they would do with what they had been given.

The words without making exception have two explanations. Al-Tabari says they “did not say, ‘If Allah wills’” [1], while al-Qurtubi reports from Ikrimah that they did “not set aside the right of the poor” [2]. The scene as a whole holds both meanings. The poor are named outright in what they told each other, and glorifying Allah is at the heart of the question asked by the most moderate among them.

We might reflect that the scene does not blame them for planning or for fixing a time. Resolve and careful planning are part of taking the means. What the scene shows is what happens when a person thinks tomorrow is in their hands alone, and so shuts the door of a blessing on everyone else.

They set out whispering, sure of their own ability

Notice that the verses do not only tell us what they said on the way. They tell us how they said it: lowering their voices. Al-Saadi explains that they whispered these words “for fear that someone would hear them and tell the poor” [3].

We might reflect that words we lower our voices for, so that the people they affect will not hear, carry a question in that very whisper. It is worth stopping at that question before we act.

The next verse describes how they went with one word: able. Al-Tabari’s preferred reading is that they went out “upon a matter they had intended and resolved on, and kept secret among themselves, able to do it in their own estimation” [4]. The ability existed in their own view of themselves. Notice, too, that the verse ends on this word, and the very next verse tells us what they saw when they arrived. Only one verse stands between their certainty and the truth.

Had they lost the road, or lost the fruit?

The first thing they said when they saw the garden was Indeed, we are lost. They thought they had taken the wrong road [5]. Then they knew it was their own garden, and the sentence changed: Rather, we have been deprived.

Notice that the first sentence looks for the mistake outside themselves, on the road. The second begins with Rather, turning away from the first guess, and then looks at the speakers themselves: we.

Anyone who has reviewed a project that failed knows this shift. Our first question is usually about the step where we went wrong. The more honest question is about the aim the whole plan was built on.

One of them had warned them before

The words Did I not say to you point back to an earlier time. He is reminding them of something he said before they set out. Al-Qurtubi says the phrase shows that this man “had told them to make the exception, and they did not obey him” [6]. Al-Saadi explains the most moderate of them as “the most just of them”, and explains glorifying Allah as declaring Him free of anything unworthy of Him, “and part of that is your belief that your ability is independent” [7].

Here lies the deepest point of faith in the scene. Their fault was not that they made up their minds. It was that they made up their minds as if their own ability were enough. Saying “if Allah wills” is not a phrase added to the end of a sentence. It is an admission that tomorrow belongs to Allah, glorified and exalted is He, and that everything in our hands is His blessing.

His words did not help them at the time, because no one listened. We might reflect on how many honest warnings are spoken at the start of the road and only remembered after the loss.

The story does not end with the loss

Notice that their answer used the very word he had called them to. He asked, Why do you not exalt [Allāh]? They said, Exalted is our Lord! Then they confessed: Indeed, we were wrongdoers.

There was a moment of blame: Then they approached one another, blaming each other. Al-Qalam, 68:30 But blame was not their last word. They turned to their Lord in hope:

﴿عَسَىٰ رَبُّنَا أَن يُبْدِلَنَا خَيْرًا مِّنْهَا إِنَّا إِلَىٰ رَبِّنَا رَاغِبُونَ﴾Meaning in English: “Perhaps our Lord will substitute for us [one] better than it. Indeed, we are toward our Lord desirous.”Surah Al-Qalam, 68:32

This verse leaves a door open after a mistake: admitting the wrong, then hoping in Allah instead of despairing of Him. Even so, the story closes with a serious warning:

﴿كَذَٰلِكَ الْعَذَابُ ۖ وَلَعَذَابُ الْآخِرَةِ أَكْبَرُ ۚ لَوْ كَانُوا يَعْلَمُونَ﴾Meaning in English: “Such is the punishment [of this world]. And the punishment of the Hereafter is greater, if they only knew.”Surah Al-Qalam, 68:33

This is the punishment of this world for those who defy Allah’s command, hold back what He has given them, and deny the poor their right, and the punishment of the hereafter is greater [8]. The scene is a warning before it is a lesson in planning, and Allah’s punishment in it must not be read as a management technique.

Who is left outside the gate in our plans?

Here we move to our own work, as a reflection and not an interpretation. The words There will surely not enter it today upon you [any] poor person may lead us to ask of every plan we make: who will not come through its gate?

Take a hypothetical example. A public service organization decides to move all appointment booking to a smartphone app, to cut queues and lower costs. The plan is carried out well, and the reports show good numbers. But some older people, and people without smartphones, quietly stop coming. Their absence appears in no report, because the reports only count the people who came through the gate.

A receptionist had warned from the start that some clients could not use the app, but her warning never made it into the plan. When the numbers fell, the team’s first move was to look for a bug in the app.

This team should not be compared with the owners of the garden. They agreed in secret to keep the poor out, while this team never meant to exclude anyone. Even so, the question the scene raises still stands: who has been left outside the gate without our noticing?

What questions do the eight 10X pillars open?

We are not saying that the verses offer a modern management model. But they raise questions that meet three of the eight pillars of 10X excellence, and one method for preventing failure.

Value is measured by who the plan reaches

The pillar of redefining value and strategic direction asks us not only how much we harvested, but who the good reached and who was left without it. A plan that hits its numbers but leaves the people who need it outside the gate calls us to rethink what we mean by success.

Leaders need to hear the warning before the loss

Under the pillar of leadership, transformation culture and future governance, the most moderate of them reminds us that every team has a fair voice that may see what the general excitement misses. Leaders who want real change build a culture where that voice is heard and answered before the work begins.

Reports do not count those left outside the gate

Under the pillar of maximizing organizational and technical intelligence, the most important thing to know may be what the dashboards do not show. Data tells us who came in. It rarely tells us who stopped coming. Organizational intelligence starts when we look for those who are absent as carefully as for those who are present.

Preventive Engineering (FMEA) asks about failure before it happens

We add one more lens, with care. What happened to the garden was a decree and a punishment from Allah, and we do not analyze it as an engineering problem. But Preventive Engineering, which uses Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA) to study possible failures and their effects before they happen, helps us question our own plans. It looks at each risk through Severity, Occurrence and Detection (S/O/D): how badly it would hurt people, how likely it is, and whether we would catch it before it turned into a silent absence. The receptionist’s note in the example was exactly this kind of early warning.

Questions to take into our next plan

  1. When we say “if Allah wills”, do we mean that tomorrow is not in our hands alone?
  2. Who will not come through the gate of this plan, and have we asked them?
  3. What are we saying in low voices because we would rather the people it affects did not hear?
  4. Who is the fair voice on our team, and when did we last listen to them before we started?

The 10X excellence question

What will your plans look like in a few years if no one is left outside the gate?

Imagine your organization a few years from now. Its plans are written with the people who have a stake in them in the room, and the first warning is heard before the work begins, not after the loss. What has changed in the way you plan? And who will you start bringing into the count today?

A final reflection

The story began with an oath without making exception, then whispers on the road, lowering their voices, then certainty, [assuming themselves] able. Then came the shock, Indeed, we are lost, and the truth, Rather, we have been deprived. Then a reminder of an earlier warning, Did I not say to you, then glorification and confession, Exalted is our Lord!, and then hope, Perhaps our Lord will substitute for us [one] better than it.

What we might carry from this scene is to plan knowing that tomorrow belongs to Allah, to leave room in our plans for whoever is standing at the gate, and to hear the warning before we find ourselves saying too late, Indeed, we were wrongdoers. And if we do get it wrong, the way back to Allah is open: Indeed, we are toward our Lord desirous.
