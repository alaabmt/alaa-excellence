When “Urgent” Slowed the Lab: How Automation Became a Learning System

At King Faisal Specialist Hospital & Research Centre in Riyadh, the automation story did not end when the equipment went live. Years into implementation, the data showed that some “urgent” rules were slowing flow. That is where the more important story begins.

The paradox: In August 2016, the published median turnaround time for Troponin‑T was 64.9 minutes. The team reviewed the workflow, reclassified 11 assay kits from STAT — urgent-priority processing — to routine, and removed unnecessary historical auto-rerun rules. By March 2017, the median was 52.4 minutes. In that configuration, some “urgent” rules made the automated system slower, not faster. [1]

This is not an argument against urgent testing, nor a universal laboratory rule. It is a story about a deeper operating principle: when the system changes, a rule designed for the old system can become a bottleneck in the new one.

Before the paradox: a laboratory facing a growth question

In 2012, the Department of Pathology and Laboratory Medicine performed 13.5 million tests, with the study reporting roughly 7% year-on-year workload growth. The challenge was not simply to buy faster analyzers. It was to build a capacity that could absorb rising demand with more predictable turnaround time and lower resource intensity. [1]

The transformation plan began in 2010 with Lean principles and business-process re-engineering. After more than four years of planning and preparation, Total Laboratory Automation (TLA) targeted hematology, chemistry and serology, representing 73% of department tests. Implementation began in October 2012, went partially live in 2013, and was completed in 2016. KFSHRC’s own historical record lists TLA implementation in 2012. [1][2]

What actually changed? More than an automation track

If this were only an equipment project

Manual handling would be replaced by an automated line while contracts, workflow rules, skill mix and decision rights remained largely untouched.

What the published case documents

Connected analyzers + pre/post-analytical processing + middleware and hospital information-system integration + contract consolidation + skill-mix redesign + continuous measurement and review.

The system linked chemistry, hematology, coagulation and related processes with middleware connected to Cerner Millennium. Logic for auto-verification, delta checks and reflex testing was built so staff could focus more attention on abnormal results and complex work. [1]

Governance: who saw the problem and could change it?

The paper says leadership teams analysed current and historical performance, created a strategic plan with named owners for project streams, and ran daily meetings for key tasks and challenges, weekly updates, and monthly steering reviews. A learning system therefore depended not only on data, but on people who could interpret it and change operating rules. [1]

The financial result was linked to consolidating five contracts into one, six fewer full-time equivalents required, and a changed skill mix. These are results reported by the implementation team, not independently audited savings. [1]

The study also reports roughly 4,600 blood tubes processed daily through TLA. But the design is a before/after implementation study with several concurrent operational changes; it cannot isolate automation alone as the cause of every outcome. [1]

The moment automation became learning

More than four years after go-live, the team reviewed the effect of STAT assays, auto-dilutions and reruns on turnaround time. It moved 11 kits from STAT to routine and removed unnecessary historical auto-rerun rules. For Troponin‑T, the published median fell from 64.9 to 52.4 minutes; at the study’s 11 a.m. peak, the median fell from 141 to 78 minutes. [1]

The 10X move: the question was no longer “Does the automation work?” but “What have we learned from operating it, and which inherited rules should we redesign now?”

From future to capability: a 10X reading

The public sources do not show that KFSHRC formally used Backcasting. We can, however, use it transparently as an analytical lens:

Desired future: a laboratory that absorbs rising diagnostic demand with fast, predictable results without scaling resources at the same rate.
← Capabilities: integrated automation, connected information, value-based skill allocation, rapid governance and continuous measurement.
← Build now: automation track, middleware/HIS integration, contracts, verification rules and decision ownership.
← Different work: monitor performance and rewrite rules after go-live.
← Impact: lower published TAT and reported direct/staffing cost improvements, while handling growing volume.

The 10X Eight: what does the evidence support?

Value and direction

The published objective combined efficiency, predictability, productivity and capacity for growth.

Leadership and governance

Named workstream owners and daily/weekly/monthly decision cadence.

Capability building

Skill-mix redesign and staff time redirected toward higher medical value.

Operating-model reinvention

Flow, equipment, software, contracts and rules redesigned as one system.

Innovation and learning

Optimization continued after go-live through rule changes and remeasurement.

Institutional/technical intelligence

Data, verification logic and information integration became part of operations.

Sustainable results/value

Published time/cost measures and TLA still listed in KFSHRC’s current laboratory scope. [3]

We do not label “future foresight” as a documented formal method. The growth ambition is clear in the published plan, but no specific foresight methodology is evidenced.

What do we not know?

The sources used do not publish a distinct pilot protocol and duration, an independent remeasurement of KFSHRC’s figures, or enough detail on the experience of every staff or patient group. A later independent academic decision-aid review cites the KFSHRC case in the wider automation literature, but does not replicate its outcome measurements. [4]

Three transferable lessons

  1. Do not automate a bottleneck before understanding it. Map rules and decisions, not just specimen movement.
  2. Design who reads the data and who can change the system. A dashboard without decision rights does not create learning.
  3. Treat go-live as the start of learning. Rules that made sense in the old system may become the new constraint.

The 10X Question

If tomorrow’s laboratory must absorb multiples of today’s demand without multiplying time, cost and resources at the same rate, what capabilities, data, skills and governance must we build now — and how will we detect when one of today’s rules becomes tomorrow’s bottleneck?
