From Meters That Read Consumption to a Network That Detects Problems Early: How Dubai Built Smarter Water Management

A smart meter does not create a smart network on its own. Value starts when data helps detect faults early, points operating teams to what deserves action, and reduces losses before they grow. Dubai’s water network shows how digital infrastructure gradually becomes a proactive operating capability.

OrganizationDubai Electricity and Water Authority (DEWA)
SystemHydro Insight, for analyzing smart meter data
Data scopeMore than 1.1 million meters, every 15 minutes
The central ideaData shows its value when it changes how fast faults are found and how decisions are made

The challenge: losses begin before anyone sees them

Large water networks run around the clock. A leak, a metering fault or an abnormal change in consumption can go on for some time before it shows up in a bill, a complaint or an obvious failure. The later a problem is detected, the wider its impact and the higher the cost of dealing with it.

That is why it is not enough for a utility to know how much water was used at the end of the billing cycle. More efficient management needs to know whether something abnormal is happening now, where it is happening, how serious it is, and what should be dealt with first.

Water network losses: the share of water the network loses during transmission and distribution, for reasons that include leaks and metering problems. A lower figure means that more of the water entering the network reaches its intended use.

The smart infrastructure came before the AI

The Dubai Electricity and Water Authority, known as DEWA, did not start with a stand-alone AI application. Over several years it built a system of smart meters, flow and pressure devices, remote control, and network monitoring and analysis systems.[1][2]

One element of this system is SCADA (supervisory control and data acquisition), which lets operators follow the state of the network from a control center, collect operating data such as pressure and flow, and connect that data to monitoring and control actions.

The network also uses RTUs (remote terminal units): field devices that send data from parts of the network to central control systems and help carry out some operating commands remotely.[2]

These layers are what make advanced analysis useful. Without regular measurement, reliable data and connected systems, AI stays cut off from the real work.

The 10X MoveThe shift was not only replacing old meters with digital ones. It was putting network data to work continuously, to detect faults and speed up the response before a problem spreads.

Hydro Insight: when a meter reading becomes an operating signal

DEWA launched Hydro Insight in mid-2024. Every 15 minutes, the system analyzes data from more than 1.1 million smart water meters in Dubai, using artificial intelligence (AI) and data science to automatically detect different types of anomalies in the meters.[1]

AI here does not mean the system “thinks” in place of the operating teams. Its main job is to scan a huge volume of readings quickly, spot the patterns that deserve attention, and feed those signals into diagnosis and repair.

According to DEWA, the system cut data processing time by 87.5%, and the time needed to detect meter anomalies fell from about 30 days to one hour.[1] Global Water Awards reports the same result in its description of the project, which won the Smart Water Project category in 2024.[3]

What counts as an anomaly? A reading or usage pattern that is clearly different from what the system expects, such as a faulty meter or an unusual change that needs checking. A signal on its own does not confirm a leak; it means the case is worth investigating.

What the evidence shows: no single number tells the whole story

15 minutesInterval between analysis cycles
1 hourTime to detect some meter faults, down from about 30 days
4.4%Reported water network losses in 2025

What matters most is putting these figures in context. DEWA data show that water network losses fell from 9.1% in 2014 to 4.6% in 2023, then to 4.5% in 2024 and 4.4% in 2025.[4] DEWA also states that losses were much higher in earlier decades.[1]

But Hydro Insight only started in mid-2024, so the whole historical decline cannot be credited to it. The long-term improvement came from many technologies and practices built up in the network over time, from pressure management and leak detection to smart metering, control and analysis.

Global Water Awards, an independent industry source, adds useful detail: the system was developed in-house to monitor more than one million meters, it cut the time to detect meter faults from 30 days to one hour, and the alert and early-detection services were linked to documented water savings over several years.[3]

Evidence limits: The figures show a clear improvement in network efficiency, and they show that Hydro Insight sped up data processing and fault detection. They do not show that the system alone caused the whole drop in water losses. The more accurate reading is that Hydro Insight added a faster analysis layer to a smart system that had been growing for years.

Does the research support this logic?

Yes, at the level of the general mechanism. A peer-reviewed systematic review published in 2024 examined the use of machine learning (ML) in smart water metering networks. Machine learning is a branch of AI that uses data to build models that recognize patterns and detect unusual cases. The review found that these methods are used to detect many types of anomalies in water networks, and that good data is a key factor in their success.[5]

This research does not prove Dubai’s own results, but it does show that the technical principle behind the system is consistent with an established line of research: using meter data and analytics to detect anomalies early.

The 10X Lens

07

Organizational and technical intelligence

The value is not in the number of meters, but in the organization’s ability to turn millions of readings into signals that help it understand and decide quickly. This is the move from owning data to using it as an operating capability.

05

Operating-model redesign

In traditional management, action starts after a problem appears. Here, data is monitored continuously to find what is changing before it becomes a failure or a bigger loss. The starting point of the process itself has changed.

08

Value and sustainable results

Reducing water losses protects an essential resource and cuts the cost of producing water that never reaches its intended use. Operational, financial and environmental impact come together in one result.

What Leaders and Organizations Can Use

For leaders

Instead of measuring digital transformation by the number of systems bought, measure it with operational questions: How much shorter is the time between a problem starting and being detected? How much shorter is the time between detection and action? How much loss can now be avoided?

For organizations

Start with the data your operations already produce, then identify the decisions it could improve. After that, build the alert and analysis rules and connect the results to workflows. AI sits on top of a clear measurement and connectivity infrastructure; it does not replace it.

Where the principle transfers: the idea is wider than the water sector

What transfers?

The logic of early warning can transfer to hospitals, factories, transport, supply chains and asset management: collect operating data, identify abnormal patterns, link each alert to a clear decision, then measure time and losses before and after the change.

What depends on context?

Sensor types, alert thresholds, model accuracy and measurement frequency differ from sector to sector. The water network’s technology should not be copied literally; what transfers is the logic of turning data from a record of the past into a tool for early action.

The Deeper Lesson

An organization does not become smarter by collecting more data. It becomes smarter when the gap between a signal appearing, being understood and leading to the right decision gets shorter.

In Dubai’s water network, measurement, monitoring, control and analysis were added in stages, until it became possible to check more than one million meters at frequent intervals and detect problems that used to take much longer to surface.

This is the idea worth transferring: Don’t start with AI. Start with the decision you want to make faster and more accurate, then build the data and systems that make it possible.
