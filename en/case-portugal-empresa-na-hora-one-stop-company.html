From 78 Days to Under an Hour: How Portugal Redesigned Company Registration

Portugal did not just speed up the old procedures. It brought the steps for creating a company into a single service, and later studies linked the lighter administrative burden to measurable gains in new firms and jobs.

Executive Summary

In 2005, starting a business in Portugal took 11 procedures, about 78 days and roughly €2,000, spread across several offices and documents. The entrepreneur, not the state, carried information from one agency to the next.[3]

That year, Portugal launched Empresa na Hora (“On the Spot Firm”), a one-stop service that brings the steps of company formation into a single point of service. With it, eligible types of companies can be set up in less than an hour once the requirements are met.[1][2][3]

The effect went beyond speed. Because the service was rolled out gradually across municipalities, researchers could compare areas that had it with areas that did not yet. They estimated that new firm entry rose by about 25% a year, and local employment by about 4.8% a year, in municipalities with the reform compared with others.[4] Later research also found that part of the extra entry came from small, marginal firms that were less likely to survive.[7]

The 10X point is the assumption the case breaks: that each agency should improve its own procedure. Portugal redesigned the journey around the customer’s outcome, a company formed and registered, and made government, not the entrepreneur, manage the links between agencies.

The Challenge

The problem was not one paper form. Creating a company was spread across multiple procedures, agencies and documents. A World Bank case study shows that in 2005 it took 11 procedures, 78 days and about €2,000.[3]

A one-stop shop means that the customer does not have to manage the links between government agencies. Instead, the service is re-engineered so that the connected steps are completed at a single point. In Empresa na Hora, this included pre-approved company names and articles of association, completing the commercial registration, and handing over the company’s basic details within the same service.[1][2]

The 10X Shift

From improving each procedure on its own to redesigning company formation around one result: the entrepreneur leaves a single service point with the company formed and registered, instead of carrying information and documents between agencies.[1][3]

What the Evidence Shows

78 days → < 1 hourTime to form a company

Eligible services moved from a process of about 78 days to one that can be completed in less than an hour.[2][3]

+25%New firm entry

Estimated annual increase in new firm entry in treated municipalities compared with others.[4]

+4.8%Local employment

Estimated annual increase in local employment in municipalities that adopted the reform.[4]

In a more recent independent assessment, the OECD reported that the 2005 reform is credited with about 4,500 new firms and 17,500 jobs in its first two years. This is an estimate of the reform’s effect, not an administrative count of every firm or job caused by the reform alone.[5]

After twenty years, Portugal’s Institute of Registries and Notary (IRN) reported that more than 369,000 companies had been created through the service, with an average service time of 41 minutes. These administrative figures describe use and speed; on their own they do not prove economic impact.[6]

That speed does not mean the whole journey is short. In August 2026, the Portuguese outlet ECO reported that getting an appointment for the service could take months because of backlogs at the commercial registries.[8]

Evidence limits: The causal studies make use of the service’s gradual rollout, but the reform did not make every firm more productive or longer-lived. Later research indicates that part of the extra entry came from small, marginal firms that were less likely to survive. Service time at the counter also does not necessarily include waiting for an appointment or all the obligations that follow formation.[4][7]

The 10X Lens

Value and strategic direction

Value no longer meant each agency completing its own procedure, but delivering the customer’s outcome: a company formed and registered.

Leadership, culture and governance

The reform required linking commercial registration to data and procedures beyond a single office, and a legislative change that allowed immediate formation.[1][3]

Operating-model redesign

Instead of the entrepreneur moving between procedures, the steps were combined into one pathway using pre-approved elements.

Innovation and experimentation

The service started in some municipalities and expanded gradually, which also made it possible to measure its effect across areas and over time.[4]

Value and sustainable results

The service has run for at least twenty years, and more than 369,000 companies had been created through it by 2025.[6]

What Leaders Can Use

Start from the outcome the customer wants, not from the current list of procedures. Then decide which steps can be combined, which data should move between agencies without the customer carrying it, and which decisions can be standardized or approved in advance.

Do not measure success only by the time of an internal step. Measure the whole journey, the cost, how many people complete the service, and what happens afterwards. Fast company formation is not enough if the wait before the service stays long or the new firms do not last.

Where the Principle Transfers

The principle can transfer to services where the customer journey is split across several agencies, such as licensing, registration and approvals. But moving the front end alone is not enough. The case shows that real simplification requires changing the rules, the data flows and the sequence of decisions, not placing the old procedures behind a new screen.

From Future to Impact

Desired future

Services organized around the customer’s need, not around agency boundaries.

Required capabilities

Shared data, common rules, clear authority and measurement of the whole journey.

What must be built now

A single pathway, pre-approved templates where appropriate, and connections between agencies.

New operating behavior

Government manages the links between its procedures instead of passing them to the customer.

Outcomes and impact

Less time and cost, and higher firm entry where the evidence supports it.

The 10X Question

If the future we want is one in which customers get the result of a service without needing to know how government is divided internally, what would that journey look like in practice? And what rules, data, decision rights and links between agencies must we build today to move from speeding up individual steps to removing waiting and repetition from the whole journey?
