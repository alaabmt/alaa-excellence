Idea of the Day

Before you get better, make sure you're measuring what matters.

Organizations don’t get better just because performance indicators are green. Real improvement starts when we make sure that what we measure represents the value we want to create, not just what is easy to count.

One of the quietest mistakes in management is to improve a number that does not accurately reflect the outcome we are looking for. The time of an action may decrease, and at the same time rework may increase. The number of beneficiaries may increase, while the quality of the experience decreases. The team may achieve the indicator goal, without the problem for which the indicator was developed improving.

A good indicator doesn’t just tell you what happened; it helps you know if you’re really getting close to the end.

So the previous question for any improvement project is not: How do we raise this indicator? It is: Does this indicator represent the success we want? The difference between the two questions is great. The first may lead to the improvement of the number, and the second leads to the improvement of the system.

A mature measurement balances more than one angle: the end result, the quality of the process you produced, the experience of the beneficiary, and any side effects that may appear as a result of the change. When we look at only one dimension, we may make a local success at the expense of the whole system.

Choose one of your important indicators today and ask: If this number improves, can I say with confidence that the service or the organization has become better?

In institutional excellence, measurement is neither a board for presentation nor an end to accountability; it is part of the way of thinking. The right indicator makes the problem more visible, reveals the impact of the decision, and prevents us from celebrating a cosmetic improvement.

Before you invest time and resources into improving performance, first make sure you have defined success correctly. Because what we measure directs attention, what we direct attention to makes behavior, and repetitive behavior makes organizational results.

Idea of the Day

Don’t start by improving the number. Start by making sure the number measures what’s worth improving.
