What Happened When Hospitals Tried to Protect Nurses From Interruptions During Medication Rounds?

Dr. Alaa Mohamed Ahmed

1 November 2026 · 8 min read

During a medication round, nurses are interrupted again and again: a colleague has a question, a doctor needs something, or a missing item has to be found. Most studies that looked at this found that interruptions are linked to more medication errors. So some hospitals tried simple fixes, the best known being a vest that says “Do not interrupt.” Yet a trial in four French hospitals, which randomly assigned whole units to the vest or usual practice, found no evidence that the vest reduced errors. A recent review found that the effect of such fixes on errors is still unclear.

This article draws on a randomized trial published in 2021, a field study in a Saudi hospital published in 2022, and three reviews published in 2025. All of them come from hospitals and pharmacies. Applying the idea to other organizations is the author's inference.

What do studies say about interruptions and errors?

A review published in 2025 collected 22 quantitative studies from nine countries on interruptions and medication administration errors among hospital nurses. Sixteen of them (73%) found a statistically significant link between interruptions and more errors. But the studies defined interruptions and errors in different ways, and they collected data differently: by direct observation, by asking nurses, or by reviewing records. [1]

In a 1,000-bed teaching hospital in Saudi Arabia, researchers observed 28 nurses during 15 medication rounds and then interviewed them. They recorded 182 interruptions. Most happened while medications were being given, in corridors and patient rooms. The most frequent sources were other nurses, doctors, and obstacles such as missing supplies. Much of what pulled nurses away from medications was dealing with these obstacles or with patients' needs. [2]

This is not only a nursing problem. A review of 51 studies in pharmacies found that pharmacists are interrupted anywhere from fewer than 5 to more than 20 times an hour. The most common sources were phone calls, questions from patients, and other staff passing on questions they could not answer. [3]

What happened when hospitals tried a “Do not interrupt” vest?

Four French hospitals tested a vest printed with “Do not interrupt,” worn by nurses during medication rounds. A poster at the unit entrance explained it to patients and families. Researchers randomly assigned 29 whole units (a cluster randomized trial): 14 used the vest and 15 kept their usual practice. The study covered 178 nurses and 1,346 patients across 383 medication rounds, and trained observers collected the data directly. [4]

The medication administration error rate did not differ significantly: an error happened in 7.09% of observed doses on units with the vest, compared with 6.23% on the other units. The study calls each dose an “opportunity for error.” The interruption rate was the share of doses interrupted at least once. Before the trial, it was 13.4% on the units that later wore the vest and 23.78% on the others. During the trial, it was 15.04% on vest units and 20.75% on the others: it rose slightly where the vest was worn and fell slightly where it was not. The researchers concluded that the vest had no impact on errors or on interruption rates.

The most common error was giving a drug in a form other than the one prescribed, and most errors had no effect on the patient's health. [4]

Why is preventing interruptions not enough?

A systematic review published in 2025 examined 14 studies of interventions aimed at interruptions during medication administration. Reviews of this kind follow a stated search plan and rate the quality of each study. Preventive measures, such as vests and awareness campaigns, may reduce interruptions from people outside the team, but nurses interrupting other nurses remained common. Training nurses to handle interruptions when they happen did little to reduce their number, but it improved how nurses managed them. The effect of all these measures on medication errors remained unclear. [5]

The author's reading of these studies is that a vest targets only one source of interruptions. Many interruptions come from within the team itself, or from missing supplies and failures of equipment or procedures that force a nurse to leave the medications and work around the problem. [2][5] Some interruptions are also necessary, such as alerting a nurse that a patient is getting worse. The aim, then, is to remove the interruptions that can be avoided, while keeping the ones that matter.

What can leaders do?

  1. Start by measuring: who interrupts, when, and why? In the studies, sources of interruption differed from place to place, and direct observation showed them most clearly. [2][3]
  2. Remove the causes of interruptions at the source: put supplies in place before the round starts, and send phone calls and questions to a colleague who is not giving medications. This is the author's inference from the sources of interruption the studies describe. [2][3]
  3. Agree on team rules for when it is acceptable to interrupt a colleague who is giving medications, and when it is not. Nurse-to-nurse interruptions stayed common even with vests and campaigns. [5]
  4. Train staff to handle the interruptions they cannot avoid: stop at a safe point, note where they were, and then return. [5]
  5. Measure the errors themselves, not only how many people wore the vest or how often interruptions happened. Interruptions can fall while errors stay the same. [4][5]
  6. Look for other error-sensitive tasks in your organization that are often interrupted, such as approving payments or routing urgent calls. This is the author's inference; these studies did not test it.

A sign or a vest alone is not enough: the only randomized trial among these five studies found no effect of the vest on errors. [4]

Limits of the evidence

The French trial collected its data in 2017, over a few months, and it tested only the vest and poster, with no change to how the work was organized. [4] The scoping review [1] and the systematic review [5] drew on studies that defined and measured interruptions and errors differently, so neither pooled the results into one statistical analysis. A link between interruptions and errors does not, on its own, prove that interruptions cause them. [1][5] The Saudi study was small and descriptive, and it did not measure errors systematically. [2] The pharmacy review, like the first review, is a scoping review, which does not assess the quality of each study. [1][3]

Of the five studies, only one took place in an Arab hospital, and none tested solutions to interruptions outside health care. Applying it to error-sensitive work in other sectors is a reasonable inference, but it has not been tested.

The 10X lens

Asking people not to interrupt someone doing error-sensitive work is not enough. Many interruptions are a symptom of a system that does not provide the right information or supplies at the right time and place. An organization that relies on a sign treats the symptom and leaves the cause in place. The real shift comes when every interruption is treated as information about a weakness in the system, so its cause can be removed, while people can still interrupt when it truly matters.

Key terms

Interruption
Breaking off a task to deal with something else, whether or not the person returns to it.
Medication administration error
A difference between what was prescribed for a patient and what the patient actually received, in the drug, dose, form, time, or route.
Opportunity for error
Each dose given to a patient, because each dose is a point where an error can happen. The error rate is the number of doses with an error divided by all doses.
Cluster randomized trial
A trial in which whole groups, such as hospital units, are randomly assigned to the intervention or usual practice, instead of individual people.
Systematic review
A review that collects studies on a topic using a stated search plan and rates the quality of each study.
Scoping review
A review that maps what has been published on a topic, without assessing the quality of each study or combining results into one number.

The question that remains

Three years from now, will your most error-sensitive work be protected by a sign or a vest, or by a way of working that removes the causes of interruption? Which of those tasks is interrupted most often today, and where do the interruptions come from?
