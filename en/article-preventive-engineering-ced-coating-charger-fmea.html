FMEA · Failure Mode and Effects Analysis · Manufacturing · Electrocoating · Process Quality

What did FMEA add to a statistically stable coating line for electric-vehicle charger enclosures, and was the effect measured?

Enclosures for electric-vehicle charging stations get a thin coat that protects them from rust. The coat must be between 15 and 30 microns thick. A micron is one thousandth of a millimeter. A team from Slovakia and the Czech Republic took 1,250 thickness readings on an industrial coating line over three months. The process looked stable, meaning its results repeated without sudden jumps. Yet its capability index (Cpk), which shows how comfortably the process fits inside the allowed range, was 0.81. The study treats 1.33 as the minimum for a capable process, and it estimates that about 6% to 7% of parts fall outside the window. The team then applied failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA). FMEA lists what could go wrong, rates each risk, and sets priorities. The study’s summary says proposed corrective actions reduced risk scores by 50% to 62.5%. Its own table calls these reductions expected, and the study reports no thickness measurements afterward.

What problem did the coating line face?

In cathodic electrodeposition (CED), a part is dipped in a coating bath, and an electric current deposits the layer on its surface. The study measured the dry coating on steel enclosures for a charging station, at control points fixed in advance on the front face, with 1,250 readings over three months. [1]

Mean thickness was 21.84 microns. The standard deviation, a measure of how widely the readings spread around the mean, was 3.14. The lowest reading was 13.40 and the highest 31.40, so both fall outside the window. No station mean crossed a limit on the control chart, a plot that tracks results against statistical limits. So the study called the process stable, with routine variation and no single special event behind it. [1]

The capability indices show a weaker result. Cp and Cpk compare the width of the specification window with the spread of the process over the short term. Cpk also accounts for how far the mean sits from the middle of the window. Pp and Ppk are their long-term counterparts (this is our explanation, not the study’s). The study requires 1.33 or more for Cp and Cpk, and 1.50 or more for Pp and Ppk. Cp was about 0.88 and Cpk about 0.81. Pp was about 0.80 and Ppk about 0.73. The study called the process “conditionally capable.” It estimated that about 6% to 7% of parts fall outside the 15-to-30 window, and that the drift leans toward the thinner side. [1]

A stable process repeats the same results. Those results can still miss the specification. In our view, this process is predictable but leaves too little margin.

How did the team set priorities?

The team ran FMEA following the AIAG–VDA handbook (a joint FMEA handbook; AIAG is the Automotive Industry Action Group) across the whole workflow: material receipt and surface inspection, hanging the part, degreasing, electrodeposition, curing, packaging, assembly, and shipping. [1]

Each failure mode got three scores: severity, occurrence, and detection (S/O/D). Severity is how bad the effect is. Occurrence is how often it happens. Detection is how hard it is to catch. Multiplying them gives the risk priority number (RPN). The table below shows eight failure modes, with their scores and the reduction the study reports. [1]

The study’s summary names three failure modes as the highest: contamination, bath chemistry, and hanging. But the table puts inspection and documentation level with bath chemistry at the top, at 336, by our reading of the table. Contamination (270) and hanging (252) rank below them.

Was anything measured after the changes?

The study says targeted measures were carried out, such as training, work instructions, bath monitoring, calibration, and inspection. It gives no dates. [1]

The “RPN after reduction” column is not a re-scoring. By our arithmetic, each number equals the first RPN multiplied by one minus the expected reduction: 270 × (1 − 0.55) gives 122 after rounding, and 336 × (1 − 0.625) gives 126. The same holds in all eight rows. The study does not say where the percentages came from. So the published number is a forecast. [1]

The study reports no thickness, capability index, or share of parts outside the window after the measures. It cites a photograph of a representative defect (Figure 9) as support for the risk ranking. In our view, a photograph does not test a ranking. [1]

Which of FMEA’s nine stages does the study show?

FMEA can be described in nine stages, from setting the scope to ongoing review. The mapping below is ours, not the study’s.

What the study shows, mapped to the nine stages (the mapping is ours)
StageWhat the study shows
1. ScopeThe production chain from material receipt to shipping.
2. Components and tasksEight steps, from surface inspection to shipping.
3. Failure modes, effects, causesCauses such as human uncertainty, procedure lapses, and fixture position.
4. Current prevention and detectionThe study does not separate existing controls from proposed ones.
5. Risk evaluationThree scores (S/O/D), with no stated scale and no stated raters.
6. PrioritiesEight failure modes, from 200 to 336.
7. ActionsTargeted measures such as training, monitoring, and calibration.
8. VerificationAn expected percentage reduction, with no measurement after the change.
9. Ongoing reviewNot described.

In our reading, the study is clearest on scope and ranking (stages 1 and 6). It is thinnest on current controls, verification, and ongoing review (stages 4, 8, and 9). Stage 8 is the one that shows whether the process improved.

What do we not know?

This is a limited case study: one coating line, three months of measurement, and readings taken before the improvements only. The drop in scores is a forecast.

  • The summary says “proposed” actions reduced the scores, the conclusion says targeted actions reduced them, and the tables call it an expected reduction. We followed the table.
  • The conclusion says all thickness values stayed within 15 to 30 microns. The study’s own table shows a minimum of 13.40 and a maximum of 31.40.
  • Scores in the study’s text are lower than in the table. Hanging is rated severity 7, occurrence 3, detection 3 in the text, which is 63 by our arithmetic, and 7, 6, 6 in the table, which is 252. The study does not explain the gap. We used the table because it is the only place with priority numbers.
  • The study describes its measurement design in two ways: 50 stations with 25 readings each, or 50 measurements at 25 points. We could not tell what a “station” is.

An independent study explains that the AIAG handbook replaced the risk priority number with “action priority.” Action priority weighs severity first, then occurrence, then detection. The risk priority number weighs all three equally. [2] That paper comes from radiotherapy, but it bears on this case. The coating study cites the AIAG–VDA handbook, yet it ranks risks by risk priority number. It does not explain why it did not use action priority.

How can you start a small pilot of your own?

These steps are our suggestion.

  • Measure the characteristic you care about against its limits, and compute a capability index before any analysis.
  • Tie each failure mode to something you measured, and write down the scoring scale and who rates.
  • Give each action an owner and a date, and report an expected percentage as a forecast.
  • Measure again with the same tool after the change, re-score, and check that the capability index itself rose.

What does this case add to the 10X view?

The study combines two views: statistics show where the variation appears, and FMEA asks why. In our view, contamination, hanging errors, and bath chemistry can be controlled before coating or in the bath. The study links the top failure modes to the variation through a photograph, and does not test the link with numbers. In our view, a stable line that misses its capability target needs that link made explicit. The study does not say whether the measures raised the capability index.

What is your preventive engineering question?

In your statistically stable processes, what still falls outside the limits now and then? After an action, how will you know the capability index itself rose?
