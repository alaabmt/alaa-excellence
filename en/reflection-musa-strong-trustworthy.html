The Strong and Trustworthy: How Do We Really Know People?

From the well of Madyan to responsibility: how actions reveal capability and trustworthiness—and why excellence also requires fair conditions for work.

Method note: This is a contemporary reflection grounded in established tafsir. It does not present a new interpretation of the Qur’an or attribute modern management concepts to the Qur’anic text.

Musa reaches the well of Madyan after a severe disruption in his life. He has left Egypt in fear, travelled as a stranger, and arrived without position, security or an established livelihood.

At the well he notices two women standing apart from the crowd. He does not begin by assuming why they are there. He asks what is happening. They explain that they wait until the shepherds finish and that their father is an elderly man. Musa then waters for them and withdraws to the shade.

Nothing in the scene looks like a job interview. Musa does not know that his conduct will soon become part of another person’s judgment about his character.

Before “strong and trustworthy,” there was observed conduct

One of the women later returns and invites him to meet her father, who wishes to reward him for watering for them. When Musa arrives, the man first listens to his story. Only after that comes the recommendation:

“The best one you can hire is the strong and trustworthy.”Al-Qasas 28:26

Al-Tabari explains “strong” in relation to being capable of the work and “trustworthy” in relation to what is entrusted to the person. Al-Saadi likewise treats capability and trustworthiness as two important qualities in someone given responsibility. [2] [4]

Classical commentaries also transmit reports about what the woman may have observed in Musa’s strength and conduct. Because the Qur’anic text itself does not spell out the full evidentiary sequence, it is better to distinguish clearly between the text and those transmitted explanations. [2] [3]

How do we really know people?

Many decisions about people begin with words: a résumé, an interview, a presentation, a confident answer or a first impression. Yet self-description is not always the strongest evidence of character.

In the Madyan scene, Musa does not announce that he is capable or trustworthy. His action comes first.

This opens a wider question: do we know people mainly by what they say about themselves, or by how they act when a real need, real pressure and meaningful choice are present?

One event is not enough for every judgment. The story also contains context and listening. Musa asks before acting, and the women explain their circumstances. Later, their father listens to Musa’s story before moving toward trust and responsibility. As a contemporary reflection, this suggests four useful disciplines:

  • Observe behaviour: what did the person do when the task became real?
  • Understand context: what constraints shaped the behaviour you saw?
  • Listen to the story: what experience sits behind the person’s choices?
  • Build trust on evidence: not on instant admiration, and not on permanent suspicion.

Capability alone is not enough

In contemporary work, “strength” can be reflected on as task-relevant capability: knowledge, skill, judgment and the ability to execute. That is an application of the broader idea of being capable of the responsibility, not a claim that the verse defines modern competency models.

Capability alone, however, is insufficient. A highly capable person can use access, authority or knowledge in ways that damage those who trusted them. Trustworthiness concerns what a person does with the capability they possess.

The reverse also matters. Good intentions do not automatically create the competence a demanding task requires. The pairing is powerful because it holds together two questions: Can this person do the work, and can this person be trusted with what the work gives them?

What about performance under difficult circumstances?

Musa is not in comfortable circumstances when he helps at the well. He is a stranger and in need. Yet his hardship does not prevent him from acting well when an opportunity to help is within his reach.

A contemporary lesson must be stated carefully. Difficult circumstances do not make responsibility meaningless, but neither should this story be used to glorify exhaustion or require people to tolerate unfair or harmful conditions.

The responsibility of the worker is not the whole story.

“I do not wish to make it difficult for you”: the leader also has a responsibility

After Musa is described as strong and trustworthy, the father does not say that strength justifies unlimited burden. In proposing the agreement, he says that completing ten years would be voluntary beyond the required term and adds that he does not wish to make things difficult for Musa.

Al-Saadi highlights ease and good treatment in his reading of the verse, while al-Tabari notes that completing ten was not part of the binding minimum of the agreement. [5] [6]

This creates an important balance for contemporary leadership:

A person should not use difficult circumstances as an automatic excuse for neglecting responsibility, and a leader should not use someone’s trustworthiness as an excuse to overload them.

Organizations sometimes celebrate the person who can “handle anything” and then reward that reliability by giving them even more to carry. Trustworthiness is not a free resource to consume. Strength does not mean the absence of limits.

Trust does not eliminate a clear agreement

The relationship then moves into explicit terms. The duration is stated. The optional extension is stated. The parties acknowledge what is between them.

That matters. Trustworthy people do not need ambiguity in order to prove their integrity. Clear expectations, responsibilities and boundaries can protect trust rather than weaken it.

Many workplace failures begin not with bad intent but with unspoken assumptions: “I thought you knew,” “I expected more,” or “that was understood.” Clarity reduces the space in which different expectations quietly grow.

The Tamayuz 10X lens: people do not perform in a vacuum

The Madyan passage is not a modern human-resources framework. Yet the reflection naturally intersects with several Tamayuz 10X pillars without forcing all eight onto the text.

Leadership and governance: trust needs evidence and clarity

Good leadership does not select people only because they present themselves well, nor does it build governance on suspicion of everyone. A stronger approach combines trust with evidence: observed conduct, graduated responsibility, clear expectations and fair review.

Capability and empowerment: do not only demand strength—build it

If the work requires knowledge, skill, tools, information or authority, the organization has a responsibility to build what can be built. Asking for higher performance while keeping the same constraints is not capability building.

Reinventing the organization: do not turn assessment into an impression contest

Selection, promotion and assignment of responsibility can be redesigned to rely more on demonstrated behaviour, practical tasks and evidence across situations—and less on the interview or personal pitch alone.

Sustainable results: performance is a shared responsibility

When results decline, the question is not always “Who failed?” It may be: is the problem capability, trustworthiness, unclear responsibility, or a system that makes good performance harder than it needs to be? Sustainable excellence holds both the individual and the environment accountable.

Four practical questions for an organization

  1. When selecting: what behavioural evidence have we seen of the person’s capability?
  2. When promoting: how has this person used authority, access and trust in the past?
  3. When performance is weak: is the problem only the individual, or are tools, workload, decisions and process blocking the work?
  4. When asking for more: are we demanding greater strength, or creating conditions that make existing capability more effective?

The story is larger than recruitment

If verse 26 is reduced to “hire the strong and trustworthy,” we preserve an important idea but lose the wider scene.

The passage contains a question before judgment, conduct before recommendation, listening before trust, concern about hardship after trust, and clarity in the agreement that follows.

These are not management steps that we should claim the Qur’an is prescribing as a corporate model. They are features of the story that open a deeply relevant contemporary question: how do we build trust without naivety, and demand performance without exploitation?

The Tamayuz 10X question

When you give someone responsibility, what evidence supports your trust? And when you ask them to be capable and trustworthy, what have you provided so that trustworthiness does not become exhaustion?

A final reflection

Before the verse describes Musa as strong and trustworthy, the story has already shown action, context, listening and judgment. And after the recommendation, responsibility does not rest on the worker alone: there is concern about hardship and there is a clear agreement.

Perhaps one lesson for our lives is that knowing people requires more than what they say about themselves, that trust should be deeper than impression, and that excellence should not be demanded from individuals in isolation from the environment in which they work.

Capability is the responsibility of the person who carries it. Trustworthiness is the responsibility of the person entrusted. Creating fair conditions is the responsibility of leadership.

When capability and trustworthiness meet leadership that does not seek unnecessary hardship and a system with clear expectations, trust is more likely to become something that can carry real responsibility.
