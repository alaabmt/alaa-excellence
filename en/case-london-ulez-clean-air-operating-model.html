How London Put a Price on Driving the Most Polluting Vehicles

The leap was not a road charge. It was a system that connected emissions standards to data, transition support, enforcement and measurement.

Prepared and analysed by: Dr Alaa Mohamed Ahmed · 25 October 2026 · 16 min read

Executive summary

London did not ban every older car, and it did not try to solve air pollution with public messaging alone. It tied the use of vehicles that miss defined emissions standards to a daily charge, then built an operating system around that rule: number-plate detection, vehicle-record matching, payment, exemptions, challenges and enforcement. The Ultra Low Emission Zone (ULEZ) grew from central London in 2019 to inner London in 2021 and all London boroughs in August 2023.

Compliance among vehicles subject to the scheme reached 96.7% in September 2024, up from 91.6% in June 2023. Transport for London (TfL) estimated that roadside nitrogen dioxide in 2024 was 27% lower than it would have been without ULEZ and its expansions. That is an institutional counterfactual model, not a directly observed before-and-after fall. An independent 2025 study found a clear effect from the 2019 central zone, but no statistically detectable additional effect from the 2023 expansion. The two findings answer different questions and define both the evidence and its limits.

The transformation was not the charge alone. London converted a public-health harm that was largely invisible in an individual trip decision into a measurable, enforceable operating rule, then connected it to transition support, monitoring and staged scale. Its limits are equally important: the cost burden on some residents, administrative friction in the scrappage scheme, continued congestion and pollution sources beyond the policy’s reach.

Numbers that answer different questions

  • £12.50: the daily charge for a subject vehicle that does not meet the standard.
  • 96.7%: compliance among all subject vehicles in September 2024, according to TfL.
  • 97.4%: compliance among cars in outer London that month; this has a different denominator.
  • More than £194 million: grants paid through the scrappage scheme.
  • More than 53,000 vehicles: older vehicles removed through the scheme, according to the operator’s evaluation.
  • 27%: TfL/GLA’s modelled estimate for lower roadside nitrogen dioxide in 2024 against a no-ULEZ scenario; it is not a direct pre/post measurement.
  • 19.6% and 8.2%: an independent estimate of lower nitrogen dioxide in the first three months of the 2019 zone at traffic and urban-background sites respectively.

Air pollution was a health problem. The road-use decision could not see the vehicle.

Urban pollution changes by street, hour and source. A broad goal such as “cleaner air” therefore could not change daily operations on its own. London had to make the emissions difference between two vehicles actionable when the road was used.

Before ULEZ, road access was generally detached from the emissions performance of each vehicle. Part of the public-health harm from road emissions did not appear fully in the trip cost seen by a driver. The institutional problem was not a lack of pollution data. It was the separation between that knowledge and an executable rule.

London set a practical future condition: the highest-emitting vehicles would no longer circulate routinely on the same terms, without imposing a general ban on driving. Working backwards from that outcome exposed the necessary conditions—an explicit standard, defined vehicle classes, geographic and time boundaries, reliable identification, usable records, options for affected people, due process and outcome measurement.

The new rule: emissions performance enters the driving decision

ULEZ operates 24 hours a day, every day of the year except Christmas Day. When a vehicle is in scope and misses the emissions standard, its user must pay £12.50 for the day it is driven inside the zone.[1]

The system does not make that determination by visual judgement, nor is it a physical gate that stops a vehicle. TfL uses Automatic Number Plate Recognition (ANPR) cameras and checks detected plates against Driver and Vehicle Licensing Agency (DVLA) records covering vehicle type, age, Euro standard and emissions. That matching establishes whether the vehicle meets the standard or its use creates a payment obligation; payment, exemptions, challenges and enforcement follow.[2]

The operating chain produced the change:

  1. a verifiable emissions standard;
  2. vehicle detection within the area;
  3. matching against an official record;
  4. a clear status—compliant, chargeable or exempt under defined conditions;
  5. payment, challenges and enforcement;
  6. support to change vehicle or travel mode;
  7. continuing measurement of compliance and air quality;
  8. staged expansion and operational adjustment.

Remove one link and the policy degrades into a sign or an inconsistently applied fee. Cameras without records cannot identify emissions performance. A standard without detection is hard to enforce. A charge without exemptions and challenges weakens procedural fairness. Expansion without transition support concentrates the burden on people with the fewest easy alternatives.

From the centre to the whole city

Central London ULEZ began on 8 April 2019. It expanded to inner London in October 2021 and to every London borough on 29 August 2023, excluding the M25.[3][6]

The sequence matters because behavioural effects can begin before the formal start of each phase. Once households and businesses know a rule is coming, they can replace vehicles, reroute or change mode in advance. Compliance rose before the 2023 expansion, leaving less measurable headroom after launch.

The phases were accompanied by legal scheme orders, consultations, impact assessments and monitoring reports. The scrappage programme also changed while it was running: some eligibility conditions broadened, some grant values rose and a vehicle-donation option was added. These changes do not show that every decision was optimal. They do show that metropolitan scale required a learning mechanism, not a fixed announcement.[3][5]

Transition support did not remove the burden

London operated a citywide vehicle-scrappage programme from 30 January 2023 to 8 September 2024. TfL’s evaluation says it distributed more than £194 million and recorded the removal of more than 53,000 older vehicles. That is an administrative output, not a causal estimate of how many vehicles would otherwise have remained or how much pollution each removal prevented. The programme also offered grants, public-transport options and vehicle donation.[5]

The size of the fund does not prove that transition was painless. Independent reporting in May 2024 found that 60% of more than 123,000 applications had been rejected at that point, amid complaints about delays and administrative errors. Not every rejection was an error; applications can be incomplete, ineligible or duplicated. Still, the figure exposes the difference between allocating assistance and delivering it smoothly.[11]

Any city copying the model should treat support as part of the operating design. Application-processing capacity, decision speed, clear eligibility, a workable challenge route and realistic transport alternatives matter as much as the headline fund.

What actually changed?

1. Compliance: a clear operating result

Compliance among all vehicles subject to ULEZ rose from 91.6% in June 2023, before the London-wide expansion, to 96.7% in September 2024. For cars in outer London alone, TfL reported 97.4% compliance—around 1.820 million compliant cars out of 1.868 million detected on an average day.[2][4]

Those percentages cannot be merged. One covers all subject vehicles; the other covers cars in a defined area. Camera detections are not a count of unique residents, and the indicator does not reveal whether compliance came from replacing the vehicle, changing route or switching mode. It does show that non-compliant vehicles became a small share of what the system detected.

2. Air quality: an official model and an independent test

TfL and the Greater London Authority estimated that roadside nitrogen dioxide across London in 2024 was 27% lower than it would have been without ULEZ and its expansions. This is a modelled answer to a counterfactual question. It is not a sensor reading showing a 27% fall between two dates.[4]

A peer-reviewed independent study published in 2025 used a different test. It estimated that the 2019 central ULEZ reduced nitrogen dioxide by 19.6% at central-London traffic sites and 8.2% at urban-background sites during its first three months. It also estimated a 28.8% reduction in nitrogen oxides at traffic sites. The same study found no statistically detectable incremental nitrogen-dioxide effect from the 2023 expansion.[7]

“Not statistically detectable” is not evidence of a zero effect. The additional effect may have been small, may have begun before the start date as people prepared, may have spilled beyond the boundary, or may have been difficult to separate from other changes. These explanations, however, do not turn a non-significant result into a proven success. Equally, the official 27% estimate cannot be used to attribute the whole modelled difference to the 2023 expansion alone. The disciplined conclusion is narrower: the first central phase had an independently measurable effect; much of the later transition may have occurred before the formal expansion or become intertwined with other factors.

3. Health: a promising trajectory, not a single-cause certificate

The CHILL study, published in 2026, followed 3,414 children during 2018–2022, covering the central phase and the sequence of interventions before the citywide expansion. At recruitment they were aged six to nine: 1,664 lived in central London and 1,750 in Luton. Researchers measured lung function annually for five years.[8][9]

Nitrogen-dioxide exposure among London children fell by more than twice the amount and at more than twice the rate seen in Luton. Growth in forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1)—the air a person can forcefully exhale in one second—was about 10 millilitres per year greater in London, with lung function becoming comparable between the groups by the end.

The comparator design strengthens the evidence, but it was not randomized. Follow-up overlapped the COVID-19 pandemic and suffered substantial attrition. The results support a meaningful association between the intervention period, lower exposure and an improved lung-growth trajectory. They do not prove that ULEZ alone restored every child’s lungs.

A second independent 2026 study found improved trends in adult emergency hospital admissions after the sequence of the earlier T-charge and central ULEZ. Adjusted reductions remained statistically significant for cardiovascular admissions at 9.3% and all-cause admissions at 5.1%; the 2.7% respiratory reduction was not significant. Because the central ULEZ period was short, the study could not separate its effect from the earlier charge.[10]

What did not change—or has not been shown?

  • ULEZ changes the emissions profile of the fleet more directly than the number of trips; it does not establish that congestion was solved.
  • Other pollution sources remain, including heating, construction, rail and non-exhaust particles.
  • Financial support did not make transition easy for every household or small business.
  • Higher compliance is not, on its own, proof of a health improvement of the same magnitude.
  • A non-detectable additional effect in 2023 is not proof of no effect, but it prevents the claim that every expansion automatically creates a measurable leap.
  • The reviewed record does not document a systematic TfL workforce-development or empowerment programme. “Capabilities and empowerment” therefore remains a gap in the 10X reading, even though operating the system plainly required legal, technical and analytical capability.

How the 10X pillars worked

Foresight and ambition

The design began with a tangible future condition: reduce exposure to road-traffic emissions by ending routine use of the highest-emitting vehicles on the same terms. The record shows long-horizon ambition and phased scale, but it does not prove use of a formal foresight method.

Redefine value and strategic direction

Road value was no longer framed as access alone. Air quality and health entered the access rule, making the vehicle’s emissions performance part of the driving decision.

Leadership, culture and governance

Political leadership and institutional continuity sustained a contested, multi-stage expansion. Scheme orders, consultation, exemptions, challenges and monitoring converted ambition into governable practice. Leadership did not equal consensus: affordability and fairness remained contested.

Reinvent the institution and operating model

The intervention was not a camera or a charge in isolation. It was a connected system of standards, data, detection, payment, exemptions, challenges, enforcement, support and measurement. That connection made the rule operable at city scale.

Innovation and experimentation

London used staged implementation and adjusted support and eligibility during operation. This was learning through scale, not a laboratory experiment, and it does not guarantee that every additional phase produces the same marginal effect.

Institutional and technical intelligence

The daily decision became possible when number-plate detection met vehicle records. The learning loop improved when TfL published compliance data and institutional estimates could be tested and bounded by independent research.

Sustainable value and results

The strongest evidence concerns compliance, fleet change and the independent effect of the first central phase. The incremental effect of the 2023 expansion, comprehensive distributive fairness and congestion relief remain unresolved rather than achieved outcomes.

What transfers?

  1. Turn the goal into a decision rule. “Cleaner air” needs a defined asset or behaviour, a standard and a point of execution.
  2. Design the full chain. Standards, data, payment, exemptions, challenges, enforcement and support are one system.
  3. Measure behaviour before the final outcome. Compliance moves faster than population health, but cannot replace pollution and health measures.
  4. Expect anticipation. If people respond before launch, evaluation must capture that shift rather than look only for a post-start jump.
  5. Keep evidence types separate. Administrative indicators, counterfactual models, causal studies and health outcomes answer different questions.
  6. Make fairness an operating capability. The speed and usability of support can matter as much as the money allocated.
  7. Let independent evidence set the boundary. A strong institution learns when the measured result is smaller or less certain than its preferred account.

The 10X question

If your city wants to reduce a harm that is invisible in a user’s daily decision, what standard, data, transition support and challenge mechanism would turn that harm into an enforceable rule without shifting the full cost to those with the fewest options?

This is an independent analytical case, not an endorsement or publication by Transport for London or the Mayor of London.
