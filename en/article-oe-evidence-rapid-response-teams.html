What Happened When Hospitals Created Rapid Response Teams?

Dr. Alaa Mohammad Ahmed

19 November 2026 · 7 min read

Before a patient's heart stops on a hospital ward, there may be warning signs that no one acts on in time. Many hospitals created rapid response teams that ward staff can call when a patient gets worse. In a review of 29 studies, most of them before-and-after studies, these teams were linked to fewer deaths than in periods or hospitals without them. But two randomized trials, in Australia and Belgium, found no clear difference in cardiac arrests, unexpected deaths, or unplanned intensive care admissions between care with and without the new system. What might explain the gap?

Back to articles

This article draws on five studies published from 2005 to 2024: two randomized trials, one in 23 hospitals in Australia (2005) and one in Belgian hospitals (2018), and three reviews of earlier studies (2015, 2024, and 2024). All five are about hospitals. Any point about other kinds of organizations is the author's inference.

What is a rapid response team?

Patients who have a cardiac arrest or die on ordinary hospital wards, rather than in intensive care, have often received delayed or inadequate care beforehand. So some hospitals created teams that staff can call when a patient shows specific warning signs of getting worse. The team comes to the bedside to assess and treat the patient early. [1]

The names and set-up of these teams vary between hospitals: some include doctors, and some are led by nurses. [3][4][5]

What do the reviews say?

A systematic review gathers the published research on one question and assesses its quality. A systematic review published in 2015 pooled 29 studies. Only 5 of them compared hospitals with and without teams or tracked results over several periods. The rest were before-and-after studies: they compared two periods in the same hospital, with no comparison hospitals. [3]

In adult patients, the teams were linked to about 13% fewer hospital deaths and about 35% fewer cardiac arrests than in periods or hospitals without a team. In other words, for every 100 adult deaths without a team, there were about 87 among a similar number of patients with one. Results for children were similar. These are relative reductions, measured against the rate without a team, not drops in percentage points. The researchers noted that results varied widely between studies. [3]

Another review, published in 2024, gathered 61 studies, including 4 randomized trials and 47 before-and-after studies. It found the results inconsistent and said that differences between hospitals and types of team made direct comparison difficult, although most studies reported better patient outcomes. [4]

What happened in the two randomized trials?

In a randomized trial, chance decides which hospitals or patients go into each group, so that the comparison is fair. In Australia, 23 hospitals were assigned by chance: 12 created response teams and 11 continued as usual. Researchers followed the results for six months. [1]

Staff in hospitals with the new teams called for emergency help more often: 8.7 calls per 1,000 admissions, compared with 3.1 in hospitals without them. But the main outcome, which combined cardiac arrest, unexpected death, and unplanned admission to intensive care, was similar in both groups: 5.86 per 1,000 admissions without teams and 5.31 with them. The difference was not statistically significant, so it may have been due to chance. [1]

In the hospitals with teams, some patients met the calling criteria and were later moved to intensive care. The team was called for only 30% of them. Taking both groups together, cardiac arrests and unexpected deaths were lower during the trial than before it. [1]

In Belgium, a system for observing patients, communicating concerns, and responding was introduced in stages, in an order set by chance. Data from 28 wards in 7 hospitals, covering about 70,000 admissions, were analyzed. There was no significant difference in unexpected deaths, cardiac arrests, or unplanned intensive care admissions. The researchers said unexpected deaths and cardiac arrests were less common at the start than they had expected, which made any difference harder to detect. [2]

Why is the team not always called?

A third review, also published in 2024, examined 25 U.S. studies, all of nurse-led teams. It named three areas that affect whether the team is called: how the hospital is organized, the working culture among clinicians, and what nurses believe and know. [5]

Three things helped most: nurses believing the team was useful, nurses knowing when to call it, and hospital-wide policies and practices. What got in the way most was negative views of the team and uncertainty about when to call it. Support from hospital leaders and doctors helped when it was present and became a barrier when it was missing. [5]

What can leaders do?

These steps are the author's inference from the studies. No study tested their combined effect.

  1. Write down the calling criteria clearly, and train staff until each person knows when to call the team. [5]
  2. Track missed calls, meaning times when a patient met the criteria but no one called the team, as well as the calls that were made. [1]
  3. Show that management and doctors support the team, and address staff concerns about calling it. [5]
  4. Do not stop at creating the team; in the Australian trial, hospitals with teams made more calls than hospitals without them, but patient outcomes were similar. [1]
  5. Compare units that have started using the team with units that have not started yet, not just results before and after. [3][4]
  6. Look for other units in your organization that need a clear way to ask for help early.

Limits of the evidence

Most studies in the first two reviews were before-and-after studies, which cannot show that the teams caused the improvement. [3][4] The Australian trial followed results for only six months. [1] The Belgian trial included 14 hospitals, but only 7 were in the final analysis, and unexpected deaths and cardiac arrests were rarer than the researchers expected. [2]

The third review covered only U.S. studies of nurse-led teams. [5] The published abstracts of the five studies do not mention any Arab hospital, and none tested the idea outside hospitals. The idea that this approach could help in other sectors is the author's inference; these studies did not test it.

The 10X lens

In the author's reading of these studies, creating a team may not be enough on its own. The team is more likely to help when staff know when to call it and call it without hesitation. If staff call the team only when it is already late, its effect on patients may not show. The author also believes the same idea may help other work where people need to ask for help early, such as equipment operation and customer service.

Key terms

Rapid response team
A team that staff call when a patient shows specific warning signs of getting worse, to assess and treat the patient early.
Randomized trial
A trial in which hospitals or patients are assigned to groups by chance, so that the comparison is fair.
Before-and-after study
A study that compares results in the same place before and after a change, with no group where the change did not happen.
Systematic review
A study that gathers the published research on one question, assesses its quality, and summarizes the results.
Statistically significant
A difference that a statistical test shows is unlikely to be due to chance alone.
Relative reduction
How much a number fell, measured against the original number. A drop in deaths from 100 to 87 is a 13% relative reduction.

The question that remains

Three years from now, will your response team still be a phone number that people hesitate to call? Or will every staff member know when to ask for help, and ask without fear? And who will check that the team is called when it should be?
