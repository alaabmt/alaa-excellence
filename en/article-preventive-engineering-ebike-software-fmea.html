Can Simulation Reveal Effects of Software Faults That an E-Bike Engineer Did Not Expect?

In a European green-mobility project, an engineer built a model of the software that controls an e-bike motor. Nobody had analyzed the safety of that design. Researchers at the University of Bergamo in Italy injected 13 realistic faults into the model and compared what the engineer expected with what simulation showed.

The expectation held for eight faults. For five, simulation showed something different, and one case exposed a bug in the model itself. But this is one model and one expert, who is a co-author, and the study is an early version that has not been peer reviewed.

What problem did the engineers face?

An electric bike relies on software that controls its motor and speed. The study’s model of that software was built in Simulink, a modeling tool. It belongs to a European project on green mobility that includes companies such as Brembo and Pirelli, and it took about 100 hours to build. But the engineers had not analyzed the safety of the design. [1]

Safety matters here. The study says most European countries set 25 kilometers per hour as the maximum speed of an e-bike. A software fault could push the bike past that limit, or brake it without any command from the rider. [1]

The study is a preprint, meaning an early version posted online. Researchers from the University of Bergamo put it on arXiv in September 2025. The version we read says the replication package will be released “upon acceptance,” so it was probably not yet peer reviewed. [1]

How did the researchers analyze the faults?

Failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA) asks, before any harm happens: how could each part fail, and what would follow? The researchers used a form supported by simulation. A fault is injected into the software model, and a simulation shows its effect. They used the Simulink Fault Analyzer, a tool from MathWorks. [1]

Two researchers identified 15 faults, such as a broken speed sensor, a noisy sensor, a stuck value, and an error in the controller that regulates speed, called a PID controller. The designer of the software scored each fault from 1 to 5 for plausibility, frequency, and criticality. Two faults were dropped because they were not plausible, frequent, or critical. That left 13 realistic faults. [1]

Then the designer was asked what effect each fault would have, and the answer was compared with the simulation. The 1-to-5 scores were used to choose faults. The study does not use the Severity, Occurrence, Detection (S/O/D) scores or the risk priority number. [1]

What did the study find?

The expert confirmed that the models of 7 of the 13 faults were accurate. For the other 6, minor inaccuracies were fixed. The designer’s expectation was right for 8 faults (61.6%), and simulation differed for 5 (38.4%). The table shows five faults from the study’s Tables V and VI. [1]

In F7, the simulation stopped because of a flaw in the model of the three-phase inverter, the part that turns direct current into alternating current for the motor. The model had not allowed for an invalid value from the sensor. In F15, the bike reached about 40 kilometers per hour, above the legal cap, and braked at about 2 meters per second squared without a rider command. [1]

For F2, the expert suggested a mitigation: a backup sensor takes over when noise passes a threshold. In simulation, the bike then followed the requested speed. [1]

Which of FMEA’s nine stages does the study show?

Our series describes FMEA in nine stages. The mapping below is ours, not the study’s.

What the study shows, mapped to the nine stages (the mapping is ours)
StageWhat the study shows
1. ScopeThe model of the software that controls an e-bike motor.
2. Components and tasksModel parts: motor, sensors, battery, inverter, and controller.
3. Failure modes, effects, causes15 faults, 13 of them realistic, with effects shown by simulation.
4. Current prevention and detectionNone; nobody had analyzed the design’s safety before.
5. Risk evaluationScores from 1 to 5 to choose faults, not to rank risk.
6. PrioritiesNot shown.
7. ActionsOne mitigation is shown: a backup sensor for F2.
8. VerificationSimulation of that one mitigation only.
9. Ongoing reviewThe expert reports adding new faults to his continuing assessment.

The study compares an expert’s prediction with an experimental result. But it does not rank the risks, and it does not measure the effect of mitigating all of them.

What do we not know?

The result rests on one model and one expert, so it is a limited trial, and the study is a preprint. [1]

  • The expert whose expectations were compared is one of the paper’s authors, not an independent party.
  • The researchers chose and injected the faults. Another team might have chosen differently.
  • The study does not score fault severity in a way that ranks them, and it reports no results after any design change.
  • The mitigation shown covers one fault of 13, so we do not know its effect on the others.
  • The study estimates about six months of work to learn the domain, so the cost is not small.
  • The researchers discussed their lessons with experts from the company that makes the tool, so read its claimed benefits with care.
  • The study says the model and interview transcripts are available through a link, and the replication package comes upon acceptance. We did not open these materials.

In an independent study, Shebl and colleagues tested two hospital teams. They found that other health professionals identified failures that both FMEA teams had missed. [2] This fits the idea that one expert’s expectation does not cover every fault. But it comes from another field, and it does not show that simulation covers them.

How can you start a small pilot of your own?

These steps are our suggestion, not the study’s.

  • Pick one software system that affects safety. Before any test, write what you expect to happen when each part fails.
  • Record your expectations in detail, because what is not written cannot be compared later.
  • Run the test or simulation, compare it with the expectation, and note each difference and its cause: the part or the model?
  • If possible, have someone independent of the fault-picker give the expectations.
  • After you mitigate a fault, run the same simulation again and compare before and after.

What does this case add to the 10X view?

The assumption weakened here is that the expert knows what will happen. For 5 of 13 faults, the expectation did not match the simulation, and in one case the flaw was in the model itself. We do not know whether that rate holds beyond this case. It suggests writing down an expectation before the test and comparing the two.

What is your preventive engineering question?

In your most important system, what do you expect to happen when each part fails? Did you write that expectation down before you tested it?
