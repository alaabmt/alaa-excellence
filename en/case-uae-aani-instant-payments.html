Aani: What Changes When a Phone Number Is All You Need to Send Money?

How the UAE turned instant payments into shared national infrastructure—and grew from an initial launch to a multi-provider network.

Dr. Alaa Mohammad Ahmed · 28 September 2026 · 8 min read

Executive snapshot

Conventional interbank payments can leave customers navigating account identifiers, beneficiary details and channels that do not all provide the same around-the-clock experience. For a merchant or small business, the interval between sending money and confirming receipt creates unnecessary friction. The UAE addressed this as an infrastructure problem, not just an opportunity to redesign a banking app. Aani became a national instant-payment platform under the Central Bank's Financial Infrastructure Transformation programme. [1][2]

The Central Bank established Al Etihad Payments to operate shared infrastructure. Aani launched on 16 October 2023 with eight licensed institutions and was made available through participating providers' channels and its own app. Integration and merchant acceptance then expanded. [2][3]

In April 2026, the operator reported more than 12.5 million registrations, 74 connected licensed financial institutions and approximately 774,000 participating merchants. It reported a three-second average transaction completion time. These are operator-published measures of reach and performance, not an independent estimate of cost savings or financial inclusion. The deeper lesson is the move from improving transfers inside each bank to creating a shared national capability. [5]

A project can wait even when the money is ready

Imagine a small investor agreeing with a supplier on equipment for a new business. The payment is sent over a weekend, but the supplier needs confirmation of receipt before dispatching the order. A payment screenshot helps, yet settlement or reconciliation on the chosen channel takes longer than the commercial decision. Capital is available and the goods are ready; time becomes the bottleneck.

This is an illustrative scenario, not a documented UAE incident or a claim about any particular Arab country. Instant domestic payments do not solve foreign-exchange controls, international remittances or bank compliance checks. They do, however, remove one source of delay when both parties are enrolled in a supported domestic network.

The constraint between the customer and the banking network

A bank can make its own app faster without improving the entire journey between customers of different institutions. Re-entering beneficiary details and navigating legacy transfer workflows create friction between deciding to pay and confirming the money has arrived. The critical question was not how to build another app. It was how to make the payment network available when people need it. [2][4]

The underlying capability is interoperability: different institutions work to shared standards and connections so payments can move across organisational boundaries rather than remain in closed networks.

From national ambition to everyday service

February 2023

Define the desired future. The Central Bank launched its Financial Infrastructure Transformation (FIT) programme, with instant payments among the initiatives intended to modernise the financial system. This documents a future-oriented strategy; it does not establish the use of a particular formal foresight method. [1]

October 2023

Create an operator and launch the first network. Al Etihad Payments, a Central Bank subsidiary, began operating Aani with eight participating licensed financial institutions and access through their apps or Aani's own application. [2]

2024

Build the network, not just the product. The operator focused on onboarding institutions, working with merchant acquirers and running outreach and partner workshops. It described the original participants as a pilot group, but did not publish a full testing protocol, baseline or detailed iteration results. [3][8]

February 2025

Broaden participation. The National reported figures attributed to the operator: approximately 1.5 million enrolled users, 57 licensed institutions and more than 80,000 enabled merchants. Reporting these figures does not constitute an independent audit. [4]

April 2026

Scale adoption. Al Etihad Payments reported over 12.5 million registered users, 74 connected institutions and approximately 774,000 merchants, with transaction count six times higher year-on-year during 2025. [5]

What changed in the payment operating model?

Before the redesign

Bank identifiers were part of the customer's workload

Entering or storing beneficiary details, following provider-specific steps and waiting for completion through the selected transfer channel.

With a shared national capability

A familiar identifier becomes a way to pay

A mobile number, email address or QR code can identify a recipient and connect the payment to accounts at participating institutions. The service provides immediate transaction feedback. [2][6]

The platform does not remove banks or regulatory safeguards. It gives them common infrastructure to deliver payments through multiple channels. For merchants, this enables account-to-account transactions, moving funds between participating accounts without assuming that every shop supports the service or that fees are always zero. [5][7]

The operator also reported around 25,000 daily transfers using mobile numbers alone and a sixfold annual increase in transaction count during 2025. The mobile-number figure is not necessarily the total number of payments through every Aani channel. The public announcement does not fully explain whether its registered-user measure removes duplicate enrolments across providers. Registrations should therefore not automatically be interpreted as unique active people. [5]

The progression from approximately 1.5 million reported registrations in February 2025 to over 12.5 million in April 2026 indicates expansion on the operator's published definitions. It does not isolate the platform's causal contribution to business investment, merchant margins or overall digital inclusion. [4][5]

The Tamayuz 10X eight-pillar lens

1. Foresight and ambition

A connected, more accessible financial system became part of a national infrastructure programme. Public sources establish the policy direction, not a specific scenario-planning methodology. [1]

2. Redefining value

Value shifted from executing a bank transfer to sending and receiving money when needed, using a familiar identifier rather than exchanging detailed account information. [2][6]

3. Leadership and governance

The Central Bank set the direction and established a specialised national operator, while banks and payment providers retained customer-facing roles within shared infrastructure. [1][2]

4. Scaling capabilities and empowerment

Institutions needed to connect their channels, enrol customers and explain the service. The operator reported partner workshops, industry dialogue and awareness campaigns. [3]

5. Reinventing the operating model

Shared rails—not another single-bank app—became the differentiator. Banks and wallets could preserve their own experiences while reaching participants beyond their closed networks. [2][4]

6. Innovation and experimentation

A small initial group preceded broader rollout. The sequence is documented, but the detailed pilot protocol, duration and changes arising from tests are not publicly established in the cited sources. [2][3]

7. Institutional and technical intelligence

Simple payment identifiers, participant connectivity, immediate status feedback and QR acceptance help information flow across the network. This is a systems-design capability; the evidence does not require claiming artificial intelligence was used. [2][6]

8. Sustainable value and results

Reach, participating institutions, merchant coverage and processing speed are measured. Independent evidence on net savings, distributional effects and long-term economic outcomes remains limited. [5]

What it means for an investor or merchant

Where the buyer, supplier and merchant are enrolled with participating institutions, real-time confirmation may shorten the delay between agreeing a payment and releasing an order. It can also simplify collecting smaller payments or settling a sale. These are plausible operating implications of the service, not published evidence that Aani alone reduced project start-up times or increased merchant profits. [2][5]

Important boundaries remain. The published transfer cap is AED 50,000 per transaction; participating providers may apply other limits or charges. Cross-border transfers were described as future services in the operator's April 2026 announcement—not an established Aani capability at that date. [5][6][7]

Five lessons other institutions can apply

  1. Start with the future experience: what would a service look like if its users did not have to wait for office hours at every boundary?
  2. Locate the shared constraint: is the delay in the interface, or in data exchange, approval and settlement between independent organisations?
  3. Design governance before the app: clarify the operator, partner obligations, joining rules, data safeguards and dispute handling.
  4. Test with a manageable network: validate connectivity and usability with initial partners before expanding. This is a transferable recommendation—not a claim about unpublished Aani test details.
  5. Measure outcomes beyond enrolment: look at cash-collection times, total merchant costs, fraud losses and access for underserved groups.

Evidence limits

The sources document the national programme, launch date, shared infrastructure and expansion sequence well. Performance indicators, however, largely originate from Al Etihad Payments. The sources reviewed do not provide an independent audit of merchant-level savings, a causal estimate of investment gains or a detailed early pilot protocol. Registration figures also need clear definitions distinguishing unique users from multiple accounts or enrolments. The hypothetical investor scenario is not evidence about conditions in another country. [3][4][5]

The Tamayuz 10X question

If we want an economy in which payment follows completed work immediately, what shared systems, skills and governance must we build now so that success no longer stops at the boundary of each institution?
