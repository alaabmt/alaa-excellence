What Did FMEA Reveal About the Risks to a Three-Year Child Brain-Imaging Study in Dhaka?

In Dhaka, a team measured brain activity in young children with a wearable scanner for three years. Each child needed a well-fitting headcap. Visits ran up to five a day. Dust, humidity, and power cuts threatened the equipment. Any failure could cost data from children in a long study.

After the study ended, six experts analyzed 47 challenges using failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA). The top risks were headcap fit, errors by the staff who took measurements, and equipment affected by the local environment. These are retrospective expert ratings from one site.

What problem did the team face?

The Bangladesh Malnutrition Trial followed 234 one-year-old children for three years. It also tested 75 three-year-olds once, in the Mirpur neighborhood of Dhaka. One measure was brain activity, recorded with functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS). A headcap fitted with sensors holds the device on the child’s head. [1]

The work was pressured. Visits ran up to five a day. The neighborhood is hot, humid, and dusty, with some power outages. After years of use in those conditions, the device needed repair by its manufacturer before the study began. If a child missed a testing window, the sample shrank and comparisons across years weakened. [1]

The difficulties were known, but earlier studies described them as stories and did not measure how severe they were or how much they threatened the data. So the team applied failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA), after the study ended. [1]

How did the team analyze the challenges?

The team had six experts. Two came from the Dhaka team, and three from the Boston team that checked data quality. A sixth came from outside the project. A seventh person helped with translation between the sites. The experts identified 47 challenges across seven procedures, from recruitment and session setup to headcap placement, data collection, and post-session work. [1]

Each expert scored every challenge on three scales, known together as S/O/D. Severity is how serious the effect is. Occurrence is how often it happens. Detection is how hard it is to notice. Each runs from 1 to 10, and a high Detection score means a challenge slips by unnoticed. The product is the risk priority number (RPN). [1]

A challenge was high risk if its RPN fell in the top quarter. It was high severity if its Severity fell in the top quarter. The team discussed scores on which experts disagreed widely. Then the researchers asked four researchers from studies in India, The Gambia, and Ecuador whether they had met the same challenges. [1]

What did the study find?

The table summarizes the numbers the study gives in its text. It does not list scores for each challenge, because those appear in a figure we did not reproduce. [1]

The highest score was 167, for the challenge that not all head landmarks could be aligned. Headcap preparation was the riskiest procedure: four of its five challenges were high risk. Among errors by the staff taking measurements, five of nine were high risk. The five high-severity challenges that were not high risk had a Detection score of 2 or less. The team noticed and handled them, such as a swollen laptop battery. [1]

Occurrence scores fell over the years in every group. In some groups the fall was statistically significant: child behavior, errors by the staff taking measurements, and the headcap. The study credits children’s growth and staff experience. These scores are retrospective expert ratings, not a count of incidents. [1]

The measures the study lists include training on site and online, routine equipment checks, spare parts before the project begins because of customs delays, building families’ trust by demonstrating the device on staff, and checking headcap fit before the study starts. [1]

In our reading, the study is strong on covering every procedure and comparing with other sites. But it does not measure the effect of any measure after it was applied.

What do we not know?

The result rests on one project at one site, so it is a limited trial. The analysis was done after the project ended, and the study admits the scores are subjective and may change with the team. [1]

  • The experts took part in or supported the project. They were not independent raters.
  • The Occurrence scores by year are retrospective expert ratings, and we do not know the actual number of incidents.
  • The poll covered four researchers from three rural sites. In our view, it cannot show how common the challenges are.
  • The study warns against generalizing. Some problems relate to one device, the Dhaka environment, and the short hair of the children.
  • We did not see the figure that lists the scores for each challenge. So we give no numbers for individual challenges except those in the text.
  • We did not open the supplement that holds the table of suggested measures.

In an independent study, Shebl and colleagues followed two hospital teams that each ran an FMEA. Other health professionals identified failures the teams had missed. [2] That comes from another field. But it supports the study’s caution that team makeup changes what shows up.

How can you start a small pilot of your own?

These steps are our suggestion, not the study’s.

  • Pick a long project that depends on a device or a repeated procedure, and list its procedures in order.
  • Assemble a mixed team: people who do the work, people who check the results, and someone who knows the local context.
  • Have each person score the three scales before reading anyone else’s scores, then discuss large differences.
  • Review anything with high Severity, even if it is easy to detect. A team cannot be sure it will always notice.
  • Re-score at each stage of the project, and record what changed and why.

What does this case add to the 10X view?

The numbers show that the biggest risks were not sudden breakdowns. They were small, repeated issues: headcap fit, measurement errors, and equipment affected by the environment. A sudden failure that people notice was severe but ranked lower. Our inference is that ranking by all three scores keeps a team from focusing on what is seen at the expense of what repeats unseen. The study does not show how changing priorities affected data quality.

What is your preventive engineering question?

In your long-running project, which challenge keeps recurring quietly without anyone noticing? Who on your team sees what others do not?
