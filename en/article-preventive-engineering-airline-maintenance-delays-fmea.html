What Did a Libyan Airline Team Forecast for Aircraft Maintenance Delays Using FMEA, and Was the Effect Measured?

Airbus A320 jets at a Libyan airline depart late because of maintenance. The airline logged 1,899 such delays between 2007 and 2022, costing about 293,000 minutes. A team of seven, including two university professors and five technical managers, rated 20 causes. It used failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA), a structured way to find what could go wrong, rate each risk, and set priorities. The team voted for three of five proposed improvement projects. The study forecasts that risk scores will fall by about 59%. When it was published in November 2025, the projects were scheduled for 2026, so nothing had been measured.

What problem did the airline face?

AFRIQIYAH Airways is based in Tripoli and flies only Airbus jets, including seven A320 aircraft. Maintenance records for those seven jets show about 1,899 departure delays blamed on maintenance between 2007 and 2022, totaling 292,887 minutes. [1]

The researchers picked the two jets with the most delay. Landing gear and wheels caused the most lost time: 16,886 minutes on one jet and 14,411 on the other. [1]

The study combines FMEA with DMAIC, a five-step improvement cycle: define, measure, analyze, improve, and control. It reaches the improve step only as a forecast, and the control step has not been done. [1]

How did the team rank the causes?

The team had seven members: two outside university professors and five technical managers from the airline. They used repeated anonymous questionnaires, brainstorming sessions, and a meeting to merge their lists, and they checked each cause against the literature. They ended with 20 causes grouped under people, machines, methods, materials, infrastructure, and environment. [1]

For each cause, the team gave three ratings: severity, occurrence, and detection (S/O/D). Severity is how bad the effect is. Occurrence is how often the cause happens. Detection is how hard the cause is to catch before it fails, and a high score means harder. Each rating ran from 1 to 5. Multiplying the three gives the risk priority number (RPN), so the highest possible RPN was 125. Scores ranged from 27 to 100. [1]

Five causes reached 100: rough landing technique, aging and worn parts, spare-parts delays, wheel and tire quality, and runway condition with debris on it. [1]

What did the team plan, and what does it expect?

The team proposed five projects: pilot training on landing technique, condition-based and predictive maintenance (servicing a part when sensor data shows wear, not on a fixed calendar), quality assurance for critical spare parts, runway maintenance, and supplier diversification with inventory management. A reduced budget meant only three could go ahead. [1]

The seven members chose three projects by weighted vote: three points for a first choice, two for a second, and one for a third. Condition-based maintenance scored 14 points. Supplier diversification scored 10 in the study’s table (our recount gives 12), and spare-parts quality assurance scored 9. [1]

The team then re-rated occurrence and detection for every cause, assuming all three projects were carried out, and kept severity unchanged. The table shows its forecast for the five highest causes. [1]

The team forecast that the total score across the 20 causes would fall from 1,206 to 492, about 59%. Six causes stayed unchanged because no project addresses them, among them pilot handling, communication between teams, and weather. An occurrence rating of 1 for three of these causes means the team expects them to be very rare, which is an estimate that assumes the projects are carried out. [1]

Two rows need a caution. Landing technique and runway condition are not linked to any chosen project in the study’s own project map, and the study’s forecast table starts the runway row at 60, not 100. The limits section explains.

Which of FMEA’s nine stages does the study show?

In our reading, the study reaches stage 7, and stages 8 and 9 are a plan, not a result.

What do we not know?

These numbers are a forecast. When the study was published in November 2025, the three projects were scheduled for 2026, and the monitoring phase had not been carried out. We found no later report. [1]

  • The “after” scores are a team’s estimate that assumes the projects are done. The study says its estimated reductions for each project were simulated in Excel. The main forecast uses the team’s consensus ratings. None of these are measured delay minutes.
  • The forecast table starts from numbers that differ from the first table for three causes (runway condition 60 instead of 100, runway maintenance delays 36 instead of 48, and weather 36 instead of 48), and its column adds up to 1,204, not 1,206. Using the first table, the total is 1,268 and the drop is about 61%. That is our arithmetic.
  • The forecast lowers causes that the study’s own project map does not link to the three chosen projects, among them landing technique, runway condition, and design weaknesses.
  • The study’s full data set covers more than 3,000 delays across three aircraft types. We could not see the supplementary tables.
  • Our recount of the votes gives supplier diversification 12 points, not 10, and the ranking does not change.
  • The text describes weights for the criteria, but the scores shown are simple products.
  • It is one airline and one fleet.

Shebl and colleagues, writing about health care, called multiplying these scores to rank priorities mathematically flawed. They also said FMEA teams need information beyond their members’ experience. [2] That is a different field, but it fits our caution about the forecast percentage.

How can you start a small pilot of your own?

These steps are our suggestion.

  • Pick a measurable problem, such as delay minutes, and calculate the baseline from your records for a stated period.
  • Form a mixed team that includes an outside expert, and agree on what each score means before rating.
  • Link each project to the causes it addresses in one table, and do not lower the score of a cause no project addresses.
  • Decide who owns the project and who approves a change, and the minimum data you record each month. Count each delay once, with the same start and end rule every month.
  • After the work, measure delay minutes the same way and re-rate the causes. Report the two numbers separately. Reopen the review when a cause returns, not for a single late flight, and record what you learned.

What does this case add to the 10X view?

The study used delay records to find where to look, and expert ratings to rank causes. The 59% figure remains a team’s estimate. A ranked list of causes tells a team where to act. It does not show that acting worked. Our inference is that the first question to ask about any “after” number is whether it was measured or estimated.

What is your preventive engineering question?

In your organization, which cause of delay keeps returning for years? Do you measure the effect of an action after you carry it out, or only predict it?
