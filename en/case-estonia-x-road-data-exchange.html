10X Case · Digital Government

A State Without Queues? How Estonia Connected Its Databases Without Centralizing Them

In Estonia, drivers do not need to carry their license, because a police officer can query the transport database directly. Behind that small scene sits a bigger decision: let government systems talk to each other without handing their data to a single center.

Executive Summary

When every agency keeps its data in its own system, citizens become the couriers between them. They carry certificates and copies and fill in the same details at every office. The usual fix, pooling everything in one central database, creates a large single point of failure and raises privacy concerns.

Estonia took a third path: a data exchange layer called X-tee, known in English as X-Road until 2018. Data stays with the agency that owns it, and systems exchange it directly on request, with every message signed, encrypted, and logged. A central server works like a phone book that tells systems how to find each other; it does not store the data. [1][3]

The government's e-Estonia portal says this exchange saves more than 820 years of working time each year for the state and citizens, and that the figure comes from only 5% of queries. That number is government-reported, and the source does not publish its method. [2]

The 2017 ID-card crisis showed the other side. A flaw in a globally manufactured chip affected about 800,000 cards, more than half the population, and the certificates of 760,000 cards were suspended. Estonia fixed the cards remotely without replacing them, but the delay between notification and suspension drew criticism. [4][5][6]

The 10X lesson is that Estonia did not just speed up government offices. It removed an assumption they were built on: that citizens are responsible for moving their own data between agencies. That success also turns digital identity into critical infrastructure, which needs continuity planning as much as technology.

The Challenge: Citizens as Couriers

Every ministry and agency runs its own system, and each one needs data held by others. If those systems cannot talk to each other, citizens end up carrying paperwork, or each agency collects its own copy of the same data, and the copies multiply and drift apart. [2]

Estonia's Information System Authority (RIA, from the Estonian Riigi Infosüsteemi Amet), which runs the exchange, gives an everyday example of the alternative: a driver need not carry a license, because a police officer can check driving rights in the Transport Administration database through X-tee, based on the person's ID document. [1]

The 10X Move

The decisive choice was both architectural and administrative: no central state database. Each dataset stays with its owner, the service owner decides who may use it, and data travels directly from one party to another. The center collects only records that an exchange took place, never its content. In this way Estonia combined the benefits of connection with the principle that data stays under its owner's control. [1][2]

How the Exchange Works: Who Owns, Who Decides

  1. Members: Public bodies and private companies can join. Each member describes the data it shares, and others can use it by agreement. [1]
  2. Access rights: Set by the service owner alone, at the level of the organization and the information system. [1]
  3. Security: Authentication, multi-level authorization, logging, and data that is signed and encrypted when sent, then authenticated and logged when received. [1][3]
  4. The center: A central server acts as a phone book, sharing the addresses and validity of members' security servers and collecting statistics, while the data itself never passes through it. [1]
  5. "Invisible" services: For example, a newborn is registered in the hospital system, and the data flows on to the population register and to benefit and health insurance systems. [2]
  6. Across borders: Estonia and Finland federated their exchange systems in February 2018, so members of both can use each other's services as if they belonged to one system. [3]

Evidence of Impact and Its Limits

The e-Estonia portal says the exchange connects 99% of government services, that more than 900 organizations and companies use it daily, and that it saves more than 820 years of working time a year. It adds that these savings come from only 5% of queries, because the other 95% are hard to measure directly. [2]

In 2017, RIA said the state offered about 1,500 government services online, and that only marriage, divorce, and real-estate transactions were unavailable digitally, while the private sector offered about 5,000 digital services. [4]

All savings and adoption figures come from Estonian government bodies, and the "820 years" article is undated and does not explain its calculation. The sources used here include no independent evaluation that measures the exchange's effect on cost or citizen satisfaction, or compares it with countries that chose central databases.

The Price and the Learning: The 2017 ID-Card Crisis

On 30 August 2017, an international team of researchers alerted RIA to a flaw in a chip made by Infineon and used in Estonian ID cards issued since October 2014. In theory, anyone with enough computing power could derive the private key and impersonate the cardholder or sign in their name. [4][5]

The flaw affected about 800,000 cards, more than half of Estonia's 1.3 million people. A software update was released on 25 October, updating began on 31 October amid technical problems, and the certificates of 760,000 cards were suspended from 3 November. A month later, about 327,000 users had updated their cards, and mobile-ID users had risen by 26,000 to 160,000. [4]

RIA says Estonia stood out for being able to update cards remotely and to suspend certificates instead of revoking them. It also acknowledged that international and vendor notification channels failed to warn it, and that governments cannot fully fix problems in technologies they merely buy. [5] The independent think tank ICDS criticized the gap between the August notification of Estonia's response team and the November suspension, which left a long window for possible abuse. [6]

The 10X Lens

Redefining Value

Value means the state "disappears" from daily life, so citizens never carry paperwork or re-enter data the state already holds. [2]

Reinventing the Operating Model

Connection without centralization: data stays with its owner and moves only on request. [1]

Future Governance

Service owners set access rights, every exchange is signed and logged, and the center never sees the content. [1]

Institutional Intelligence

Each agency can build smarter services on other agencies' data without building duplicate databases. [1][2]

From Future to Impact

Desired Future → A state that is present when needed and invisible in daily life.

Required Capabilities → Trusted digital identity, a secure exchange layer, clear rules on data ownership and access, and fallbacks when identity fails.

What Was Built → X-tee, the digital ID card, mobile-ID, remote card updating, and the link with Finland.

New Operating Behavior → Agencies exchange data instead of citizens carrying it, and new services are built on other agencies' data.

Outcomes and Impact → Most public services online and reported time savings, with a clear lesson: the more a state relies on digital identity, the more its security and continuity become a matter of national security.

Insider Critique and Evidence Limits

An engineer at RIA would likely object to reducing the success to technology; the hardest part was agreeing who owns which data and who may access it. That matches what Hertie School students heard in Tallinn: "You cannot build a house starting with windows," and history, context, and trust cannot simply be copied. [7]

The same insider might also reject calling the 2017 crisis a failure, since remote updating spared the state from replacing hundreds of thousands of cards. That is fair, but the independent criticism of the response time stands, and the sources do not measure the actual harm or how many people lost access to services. [4][6]

What can be said with confidence: the state became able to deliver services that draw on data from many agencies without citizens carrying it, and without pooling it in one center. What cannot be said with confidence: how large the real savings are, or that the model transfers unchanged to a larger country or one with less trust in its institutions.

Putting It to Work

Ask where your clients carry data that your organization or a partner already holds. Start by connecting two systems that keep requesting the same documents, and let the data owner set access rights. Sign and log every exchange. Then plan what happens when your identity or verification method fails, before you need that plan.

Transferability

The principle applies to governments and large organizations with many systems, and the exchange technology has been adopted in other countries. But Estonia's small size, its heavy reliance on digital identity, and citizens' trust in institutions are specific to this case. The key lesson is that moving the technology without the rules on data ownership and governance does not move the model. [3][4][7]

The 10X Question

If our services ran ten years from now without clients carrying a single document, which data would our systems need to exchange today? Who would own the decision to grant access? And what would we do on the day the trust mechanism everything depends on fails?
