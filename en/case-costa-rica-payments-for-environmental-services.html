How Costa Rica Paid to Keep Forests Standing

The leap was not simply planting more trees. It was making forest value contractible, financeable and open to evaluation.

Executive summary

Costa Rica's forests were never worthless. They regulated water, protected biodiversity, stored carbon and sustained landscapes that underpin tourism. Yet those benefits were public, while the income from clearing trees or converting land to pasture was private and immediate. By 1995, forest cover had fallen from more than half the country in 1950 to roughly one-quarter. The problem was not simply weak environmental awareness. The incentive system made clearing economically rational.

The shift came in the 1990s as part of a wider policy package that included a ban on converting mature forest, protected areas, and changes in agriculture and the economy. At its centre, Forestry Law 7575 changed what the state was prepared to buy. It recognised four services provided by forests: greenhouse-gas mitigation, biodiversity protection, water protection and natural scenic beauty. It tasked the National Forest Financing Fund (FONAFIFO) with managing a programme that pays landholders for contractual commitments to conserve, restore or sustainably manage land.

The product was no longer a green grant. It was an operating loop: dedicated finance, a contract, a land-use commitment, monitoring, evaluation and redesign. The largest domestic funding source became 3.5% of the single fuel tax, supplemented by water charges, public budgets, international finance and results-based payments.

Cumulative programme coverage reached about 1.3 million hectares from 1997, and the OECD reports that payments exceeded USD 600 million over 24 years. The area figure is not a map of unique land or additional forest; contract periods and renewals may overlap. An Inter-American Development Bank evaluation offers a more precise view of impact: it estimated that first-year deforestation fell by an average of about 0.21 hectares for an enrolled farm or parcel in its sample, but found no statistically significant effect in later years. Other studies find positive effects that vary by region, programme cohort and baseline threat.

This case therefore does not argue that payments alone restored Costa Rica's forests. The stronger conclusion is that the country made forest value purchasable, manageable and learnable—then used evidence to expose where money changed behaviour and where it paid for an outcome likely to happen anyway. The next challenge is equally clear: target additionality more effectively, measure the services actually purchased, widen access for women, some Indigenous communities and smallholders, and diversify finance before the fuel-tax base declines.

Forests created value for everyone, while landholders bore the cost of keeping them

Under the old model, a landholder absorbed the opportunity cost of leaving forest standing, while the benefits of water, carbon and biodiversity were spread across society. Preferential cattle credit, land rules that rewarded “improvement” through clearing, and road expansion helped make conversion profitable.

That was the institutional failure: nature produced value, but the decision system did not record it in the landholder's account. A ban could change the law, but it did not necessarily create an income stream for conservation, and enforcement across extensive private land remained difficult.

Costa Rica worked backwards from a different future: standing forest should be able to finance part of its own survival. That required answering four operating questions. What public service does the forest provide? Who is authorised to buy it? Where will durable funding come from? And how will a promise become a contract that can be monitored?

From a forestry incentive to purchasing public value

Forestry Law 7575 identified four categories of value: greenhouse-gas mitigation; biodiversity protection; water protection for urban, rural and hydropower use; and natural scenic beauty for tourism and science.

This was more than a change in language. It shifted the rationale from subsidising an activity to recognising that forests deliver public services. The state could contract with landholders for forest protection, reforestation, regeneration of degraded land or agroforestry, depending on the modality and terms.

Legal recognition, however, did not mean that every service was measured separately. For years, payments bundled multiple services, even though two hectares may differ greatly in water value, biodiversity or deforestation risk. In 2024, the programme began differentiating some payments through service stacking. That is a design change, not yet proof of better outcomes.

How did the institutional loop work?

1. Foresight and ambition

The ambition was not limited to isolated protected areas. It brought private land into a national conservation and restoration mechanism. That forced a long-term question: how can standing forest retain value for its owner as agricultural, property and financial pressures change?

New funding layers—including payments after verified emissions reductions—connect the forest to a future economy that buys environmental performance. Yet financial resilience remains unfinished. Dependence on fuel-tax revenue ties forest protection to consumption of a resource that should decline through decarbonisation.

2. Redefining value and strategic direction

The pivotal move was to convert ecosystem services from unpriced benefits into value recognised by the state. That does not turn nature into a complete commodity. It does place part of its benefit inside the land-use decision.

The transferable lesson is not “pay people and everything changes.” First define the value on which society or the institution depends but which the financial system ignores. Then define the unit of contract, the buyer, the verifiable result and the behaviour that payment is expected to change.

3. Leadership, culture and governance

Governance combined a law defining services, a public fund managing finance and contracts, and technical procedures for selection, payment and monitoring. FONAFIFO coordinates regulations, operating manuals, technical assessment, payment and programme statistics.

Earmarking 3.5% of the fuel tax gave the programme more continuity than an occasional annual grant. Water-use charges, private agreements, international finance, credit and results-based payments supplemented it.

Continuity was not immunity. Falling fuel consumption and fiscal transfers exposed the fragility of the main source. The governance lesson is that earmarking can protect a programme at launch, but may become a constraint unless the financing model evolves with the economy.

Why pillar four is not claimed independently

There are regional offices, technical processes, contracts in Indigenous territories and efforts to reduce access barriers. The evidence does not establish broad, equitable capability building or empowerment across the programme. It instead identifies gaps.

In the participant or contract distribution cited by the OECD, women represented 15% and Indigenous associations 2%, compared with 46% for legal entities and 37% for men. These are administratively different categories, not equivalent population units; they cannot be read as a direct distribution of social benefit. Research also shows that formal title and transaction costs can exclude some smallholders.

A contract is not, by itself, proof of empowerment. Genuine empowerment requires the practical ability to access the mechanism, negotiate and retain value.

5. Reinventing the institution and operating model

The larger innovation was not the payment rate. It was an integrated institutional buyer. FONAFIFO aggregates money, publishes rules, receives applications, contracts and pays, and follows compliance. It operates within a wider system in which protection, enforcement, national monitoring and verification are distributed; forest recovery and emissions results cannot be attributed to FONAFIFO alone.

The loop turned strategy into repeatable work that could be scaled. It also produced administrative data on contracts, area and beneficiary categories. Criteria could evolve without rebuilding the programme from scratch.

Yet the operating model must answer the additionality question: did public money buy a change that would not otherwise have happened, or did it pay a landholder who would have retained the forest anyway? The weaker the answer, the lower the realised value per unit of finance.

6. Innovation and experimentation

The programme evolved through different contract types, priority criteria and funding sources. It tested protection, reforestation, regeneration and agroforestry, and in 2024 moved from a common payment for bundled services toward more differentiated service stacking.

Experimentation entered the rules rather than remaining a side project. But a redesigned contract is not a successful experiment until evidence shows that it improves additionality, biodiversity or equitable access.

7. Institutional and technical intelligence

Contract registers, geospatial data, satellite imagery and independent evaluations allowed the system to separate three questions that are often confused: How many hectares entered the programme? What happened to forest cover? How much of that change was caused by the programme?

The answers are not interchangeable. Contracted hectares measure activity. Trees still standing are an observed outcome. “Forest retained because of payment” requires a counterfactual comparison.

This is why the IDB's 2022 evaluation matters. Using a quasi-experimental design based on staggered enrolment, it estimated an average reduction in first-year deforestation of about 0.21 hectares for an enrolled farm or parcel in the study sample, with no statistically significant effect in later years. Because baseline deforestation in the sample was already low, the relative first-year effect was large. It should not be extrapolated across all contracts, years or ecosystem services.

Other evidence adds context. One farm-level study in a strongly implemented region estimated that participating farms increased forest cover by about 11% to 17% of mean contracted area over eight years. Earlier national studies, however, often found limited additional avoided deforestation because many enrolled forests were likely to remain standing without payment. The findings are not simply contradictory. They show that impact depends on threat, place, timing and targeting.

8. Sustainable value and results

The OECD describes cumulative programme coverage of about 1.3 million hectares from 1997, including more than 560,000 hectares added to coverage in 2011–2021, and payments exceeding USD 600 million over 24 years. These are measures of operating reach, not unique land area or a causal estimate of additional forest.

A clearer externally verified outcome is Costa Rica's USD 16.4 million payment for 3.28 million tonnes of emissions reductions achieved in 2018 and 2019. The agreement could unlock up to USD 60 million for up to 12 million tonnes by the end of 2025. The distinction is essential: the first amount and volume were achieved; the other two were contractual ceilings.

Those emissions reductions belong to a broader programme to reduce deforestation and forest degradation, including protected areas, fire management and landscape restoration. They cannot be credited to ecosystem-service payments alone.

What was achieved—and what remains unresolved?

Costa Rica's national forest cover recovered, but in a multi-causal setting: the clearing ban, protected areas, reduced pressure from some agricultural activities, tourism, economic change and rural migration all operated alongside payments.

In northern Costa Rica, one study observed mature-forest conversion falling from 2.2% to 1.2% a year after the 1996 ban, while much crop expansion shifted onto previously cleared land. It could not isolate the ban's effect from payments and other changes.

The institutional result is therefore clearer than any single ecological claim. Costa Rica built a national system that finances contracts, monitors them, exposes them to evaluation and changes design. How much additional forest each dollar purchases remains variable and calls for sharper targeting.

The costs hidden by the success story

The programme may favour larger landholders with clearer documents and greater ability to navigate applications. It may not reach people protecting customary or collectively held land that the property system does not recognise in the same way. A quasi-experimental study of participants also found no detectable effect on wealth or self-reported wellbeing.

There is a financial paradox. As the country reduces fossil-fuel consumption, the source financing conservation erodes. Diversifying into water charges, corporate purchases, climate finance and budgets linked to specific outcomes is therefore part of the operating model's sustainability, not an optional add-on.

Finally, biodiversity, water and scenic beauty remain harder to measure than contracts, forest cover or carbon. If the state pays for four services, it must move toward seeing what it bought from each one rather than using hectares as a permanent proxy for every result.

Evidence boundaries

This case separates four layers:

  • Declared objective: purchase ecosystem services and encourage conservation, restoration and sustainable management.
  • Design capacity: law, a public fund, finance sources, contracts and monitoring procedures.
  • Implemented activity: about 1.3 million hectares of cumulative programme coverage and more than USD 600 million paid during the period reported by the OECD.
  • Realised result: heterogeneous effects on avoided deforestation, plus a verified payment for 3.28 million tonnes of emissions reductions in 2018–2019 within a broader programme.

The evidence does not show that payments alone restored national forest cover, that every contracted hectare was an additional hectare saved, or that all participants gained socially to the same degree.

What can institutions transfer?

First, identify the public value on which the institution depends but for which its decision system does not pay. Second, assign one accountable buyer to integrate finance, contracts, payment and verification. Third, never confuse contract count with outcome, or observed outcome with causal impact. Fourth, direct resources toward cases the programme can change, not merely those easiest to process. Fifth, design access for people who do not have the same documentation, expertise or liquidity. Finally, do not tie the programme's future to a funding source that shrinks when the wider strategy succeeds.

The 10X question

What public value does our institution depend on today but fail to reward in the decisions of those who produce or protect it? If we make that value visible and contractible, how will we prove that we purchased additional impact—not activity that would have happened anyway?
