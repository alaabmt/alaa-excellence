What Happened to a Carpet Mill’s Defects After It Applied FMEA, and Which of Its Numbers Can We Trust?

At a carpet mill, visible defects made whole carpets unacceptable to customers, and heavy rejects were hard to dispose of. A team of five employees rated 25 defect types using failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA). The method asks what can go wrong, how bad it would be, how often it happens, and how easily it is caught. The mill acted on the top three: it converted three looms, trained staff, and tested incoming yarn. Defective output was 5.07% before and 0.49% three months later, according to the study. But it covers one mill, and some of its numbers do not agree.

What problem did the mill face?

The mill makes machine-woven carpets. Carpet defects are visible, so customers reject them, and heavy defective pieces are hard to dispose of. Before the project, defective output was 5.07% of total production. The study gives no period for this figure. [1]

The mill recorded its most frequent faults for six months. It then formed a team of five experienced employees: an assistant production manager, a machinist, a garment master (a senior sewing worker), the quality control manager, and a textile engineer. The team listed 25 defect types, based on quality records and the staff’s experience. [1]

The researchers say few textile studies compare the situation after the actions with the situation before. So this team re-scored the risks after it acted. [1]

How did the team set priorities?

For each defect type, or failure mode, the team gave three ratings: severity, occurrence, and detection (S/O/D). Severity is how serious the effect is. Occurrence is how often it happens. Detection is how hard it is to catch before the customer sees it. Each runs from 1 to 10, and a high Detection score means the defect slips by. Multiplying the three gives the risk priority number (RPN), a score used to rank defects. [1]

The team agreed that any defect scoring above 100 deserved action. Three of the 25 crossed that line: scrape (360), corner burning (224), and abrage (112). The table below shows what each defect is, why it happened, and what the mill did. [1]

The three defects above the threshold and the actions taken (as reported in the study)

The researchers read the causes as loom setup and staff awareness. [1]

What changed after the actions?

After three months of monitoring, the team re-scored the risks and compared defect rates. The table shows what the study reports. [1]

Defective output as a share of total production fell from 5.07% to 0.49%. Occurrence fell for all three defects (for scrape, from 8 to 5). Detection improved for scrape and corner burning but stayed at 2 for abrage. Severity did not change for any of them. The study does not explain how the actions improved Detection. [1]

Some numbers do not agree. First, the corner-burning scores after the actions multiply to 105 (7 × 5 × 3), not 90. The drop would then be about 53%, not 59.82%. That is our arithmetic from the study’s figures. Second, the percentages in the abstract (75%, 59.82%, and 28.57%) are drops in the risk score, not in the number of defects. Third, the study credits the fall to scrape, yet only three looms were converted and scrape’s share of defective output barely moved (43.90% to 40.23%). It does not reconcile the two. [1]

Our own arithmetic points the same way. The defect types the mill did not target made up about 31% of defective output before and about 43% after. In terms of production, that is about 1.6% falling to about 0.2%, a drop of roughly 87%. The three targeted types fell by about 90% or more. Untouched defects fell almost as much as targeted ones, so the actions may not explain the whole change. Differences in how the two figures were counted may also play a part.

In our reading, the study reaches stage 8, which the authors say few textile studies do. Its measurement is incomplete, and stage 9 is missing.

What do we not know?

This is a single-mill study: one team of five, and three months of monitoring. [1]

  • The study does not name the mill, say where it is, or give the number of looms, the production volume, or the period behind the 5.07% figure.
  • There is no comparison group and no statistical test, so we cannot tell which action, if any, caused the improvement.
  • The scores after the actions are subjective ratings. The study does not say who gave them or how. Each score is a judgment on a 1-to-10 scale, and multiplying such scales is a shaky basis for exact percentages.
  • The study judges abrage and corner burning by their share of defective output, so abrage looks small (about 8%; our calculation gives 7%). Because total defective output fell so much, the shares understate the change.

Shebl and colleagues, writing about health care, called multiplying these scales mathematically flawed. They also said FMEA teams need information beyond their members’ experience. [2] That is a different field, but it fits our caution about percentage drops in the risk score.

How can you start a small pilot of your own?

These steps are our suggestion.

  • Record defects for several months, and calculate the defect rate with a stated period and volume.
  • Form a team of people who know the work, and add someone from outside the department.
  • Agree on the threshold before scoring, on who owns and who approves a change, and on the minimum data you record for each defect.
  • Apply an action to part of the line first, and keep a comparison part if you can.
  • Re-score after the action, measure the defect rate with the same period and volume, and report the two numbers separately.
  • Open a new review when a defect returns or its score rises. Link each signal to a specific row, and note what you learned for the next round.

What does this case add to the 10X view?

The case challenges one common assumption: that a lower risk score proves an improvement. Here the team did measure afterward, which is the rare part, and the measurements do not fully agree with the scores. Our inference is that the number to ask for first is the defect rate, with its period and volume stated.

What is your preventive engineering question?

On your production line, which defect returns every week until it feels like part of the job? Could you measure its rate before and after any change, in the same way?
