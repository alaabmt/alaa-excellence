Did FMEA Reduce Errors on the Front Page of a Chinese Hospital’s Electronic Medical Records?

At Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital in China, every inpatient has a one-page summary at the front of the electronic medical record. It holds the patient’s details, diagnosis, operations, and charges. Hospital performance is scored from it, and health insurance payments are calculated from it, so an error on this page reaches both the score and the bill.

Ten experts analyzed the page’s errors with Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA), then changed the review system and the training. Their estimated average Risk Priority Number fell from 181.2 to 95.0, and the page’s overall accuracy rose from 93.44% to 95.48%. But the study has no comparison group, and the experts who scored the risks before also scored them after.

What problem did the hospital face?

Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital is one of the largest public hospitals in its province and discharges more than 100,000 patients a year. Between January 2020 and May 2022, the study period, it discharged 250,774 patients. That meant the same number of front pages had to be filled in and coded. [1]

The front page is a condensed summary of the Electronic Medical Record (EMR). It covers patient information, diagnosis, operations, and expenses. Large Chinese hospitals submit it to regulators to assess their performance. In 2019, the State Council also issued a pilot for payment by Diagnosis-Related Group (DRG), a system that sorts cases into groups and pays by group. As a result, coders had to edit the page twice: once for performance assessment and once for insurance payment. [1]

The study says the quality of this page in China is unsatisfactory, partly because of weaknesses in the system itself. It names three effects of an error: wrong source data, a wrong diagnostic group, and an effect on the patient’s reimbursement. [1]

How was the method applied?

The hospital formed a panel of ten experts: four physicians, two coders, two administrators, one quality-control staff member, and one information engineer. Each expert first mapped how the page is completed, alone. Then the group merged the maps. The result was two main processes and six subprocesses, including physician entry, physician review, coder proofreading, and review by the records management office. [1]

The experts then listed 13 failure modes, meaning the ways each step could go wrong. The team also asked 115 physicians and coders about causes, and they summarized 15 main causes. [1]

Each failure mode was scored on Severity, Occurrence (the study calls it Frequency), and Detection. These three scores are known as S/O/D. Each runs from 1 to 10, and a higher Detection score means the error is harder to catch. Multiplying the three gives the Risk Priority Number (RPN), which ranges from 0 to 1,000. The team weighted each expert’s scores by title, experience, and familiarity with FMEA. [1]

Finally, the team applied the Pareto principle: a minority of causes produces most of the effects. Seven causes accounted for 80% of the consequences. [1]

What did the hospital change?

The actions went in two directions. First, the system. Quality-control rules were added between May and September 2021 and built into the physicians’ system between July and October. The insurance system was merged with the front page on one screen between July and December. The hospital also built an “intelligent” front page that checks entries as they are made. Second, management: training seminars, a quality and information manual, regular communication with physicians, and faster feedback on review results. [1]

Before the changes, the highest RPN was 296.3, for wrong coding, and the lowest was 50.6, for data transfer. Afterward, wrong coding fell to 103.5 and late feedback to 105.9, while wrong entry fell only from 257.7 to 215.8. All 13 rows fell, and the average went from 181.2 to 95.0, a drop of 48% by our calculation. But these are expert estimates, not field measurements. [1]

What do we not know?

The result rests on one hospital with no comparison group, so it is a limited trial. [1]

  • The experts who scored the RPN before also scored it after. The study admits the scoring is subjective and was not validated by another method.
  • The text we read does not give the dates of each page group in Table 6, or whether the “after” group includes the months when changes were still being introduced during 2021.
  • The effect of each action cannot be separated, because rules, system merging, and training came together. Other factors may also have affected accuracy.
  • The study did not measure the satisfaction of physicians and coders. It assumes satisfaction improved because the RPN fell.
  • The authors note that the system they used may differ from systems at other hospitals.

How can you start a small pilot of your own?

These steps are our suggestion, not the study’s.

  • Pick one form or record whose errors affect money or decisions. Map who fills it in and who reviews it.
  • Before any change, measure the real error rate on a sample, and record the dates.
  • Score S/O/D for each point of error with a mixed team, not one opinion.
  • Fix the few causes that produce most errors first, and build the rule into the system itself where you can.
  • Afterward, measure the same sample the same way, and add an unchanged comparison group if possible.

What does this case add to the 10X view?

The RPN fell more for coding and quality review than for data entry. That fits a shift from late review to a rule inside the system, although the study does not prove the cause. The measured gain is small, about two percentage points in overall accuracy, but it covers tens of thousands of pages.

What is your preventive engineering question?

In a form or record your team reviews, how many errors are caught only after they happen? Which rule could stop them at the moment of entry?
