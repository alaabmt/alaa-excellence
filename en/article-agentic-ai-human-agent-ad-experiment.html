What Happened When People Worked With an AI Agent Instead of a Human Partner? A 2,234-Person Experiment

Dr. Alaa Mohammad Ahmed

29 October 2026 · 7 min read

When an employee works with an AI agent instead of a human colleague, does the work get done faster, and does it get better? Researchers randomly assigned 2,234 American participants to write ads either in two-person teams or paired with an agent. Those paired with the agent submitted about half again as many ads, yet the ads performed about the same in real campaigns, and their copy was more alike. What can a leader conclude from this preprint, and what can't they?

What did the researchers test, and why does it matter to a leader?

Many leaders are weighing whether to give teams AI agents: programs driven by a large language model that act in a workspace on their own, sending messages, editing text, choosing images, and generating new ones. Beyond speed, leaders need to know what happens to the quality of the work and to how people collaborate.

Harang Ju of Johns Hopkins University and Sinan Aral of MIT ran the experiment. They recruited American adults through Prolific, a service that pays volunteers to take part in studies, and gave each one task: write digital ads for a think tank's annual report in 40 minutes, for $9. Each participant was randomly assigned a human partner or a GPT-4o agent and learned which only after the task. The experiment ran in October 2024. The final sample was 2,234 participants, 1,258 paired with an agent and 976 with a person, who together produced 11,024 ads. [1]

The agent could do anything a person could on the platform except submit the final ad. Every ten seconds it was prompted to act or wait: send a message, edit text, pick an image, or generate one. The study was preregistered and approved by MIT's ethics board, and the paper says MIT Sloan's Initiative on the Digital Economy supported it. [1]

What did the experiment find?

Participants who worked with an agent submitted about 50% more ads than those who worked with a person. The team as a whole produced fewer ads, because it had one human instead of two. Confirmed by an independent study [1]

Then 1,168 American raters scored the ads on a seven-point scale. Copy quality rose by about 0.32 points with the agent, image quality fell by about 0.11 points, and the estimated likelihood of a click did not differ. The copy was also more alike from ad to ad, which means less variety. Confirmed by an independent study [1]

Roughly 2,000 sampled ads then ran as real campaigns on X from January 21 to February 9, 2025, drawing 4,932,373 impressions and 7,546 clicks. The two kinds of ads showed no statistically significant difference in click-through rate, viewing time, or cost per click. Better copy, whoever wrote it, went with a click-through rate about 3.5% higher per point, against an average of 0.14%. Limited experiment [1]

Working habits changed too. Participants paired with the agent sent 56% more messages, made 73% fewer direct text edits, and handed the agent 58% of the copywriting, against an average of 50% in two-person teams. Their messages read more like instructions than conversation. Confirmed by an independent study Among people working with an agent, task-focused communication went with more output and higher quality, and social chat with the reverse. These relationships were calculated after the experiment and do not establish cause. Limited experiment [1]

Who decided and who executed in this experiment?

In decision support, the system suggests and the person decides. In automation, the system carries out steps a person defined. In autonomous decision, the system chooses what to do without delegation. Here the agent did much of the writing and design under a person's direction: automation under human supervision. It never decided to publish, because only the participant submitted the final ad. The study did not test an agent with authority to publish or spend, so it says nothing about the safety of that arrangement. [1]

What should a leader decide after this paper?

These decisions are analysis drawn from the results, not recommendations stated in the paper, except where credited to the researchers.

  1. Give the agent the work where it did better, such as copy; keep people on the work where quality slipped, such as choosing images; and retest whenever the model changes. [1]
  2. Measure quality independently of volume: output per person rose by about half, yet the live campaign did not improve. [1]
  3. Hold a human brainstorm before the agent starts executing where variety matters. The researchers suggest this; the experiment did not test it. Forecast [1]
  4. Train people to give the agent clear instructions. That went with better results, but the study does not show it caused them. Limited experiment [1]
  5. Pilot with a small team and a comparison group working without an agent, which the study itself lacked. [1]

The limits of what we know

  • The paper is an unreviewed preprint, and figures changed between the third version (February 2026) and the fourth (October 2026): extra messages went from 62% to 56%, and fewer direct edits from 62% to 73%. [1] [2]
  • No group worked without an agent, so we do not know what the agent added compared with one person working alone. [1]
  • Participants were online volunteers, not employees, doing one 40-minute task with a 2024 model, GPT-4o. Models have changed since. [1]
  • Raters were members of the public on the same platform, and the campaign measured clicks and viewing time, not sales. [1]
  • The paper says its code and data are available on request; I did not request them or rerun the analysis. It also acknowledges that the findings may not apply to high-stakes decisions. [1]

The 10X lens

The useful question is not how many more ads the agent produced but which work has an independent quality measure. Three things are enough to start: a list of tasks the agent will take over, a quality score from someone outside the team, and a comparison group working without an agent. Key terms

AI agent
A program driven by a language model that acts inside a workspace on its own, sending messages and editing files.
Preprint
A study posted publicly before independent reviewers have examined it.
Randomized experiment
An experiment that assigns participants to conditions by lottery, so the groups end up alike in everything except the condition being tested.
Click-through rate
The number of people who clicked an ad divided by the number who saw it.
Delegation
Leaving part of the work to the agent instead of doing it yourself.

The Question That Remains

If your organization's teams work with AI agents every day a few years from now, what must you build today to know where an agent lifts the quality of the work and where it lowers it, and to protect the variety of ideas? And how will you measure that, with a comparison group working without an agent, before you expand its use?
