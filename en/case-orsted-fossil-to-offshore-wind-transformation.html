Leaving the Core Before the Future Leaves You: How Ørsted Rebuilt Itself Around Offshore Wind

In 2008, 85% of the company's heat and power came from fossil fuels. The more useful questions are how the company built a capability that did not yet exist, and what it paid along the way.

Executive Summary

When DONG Energy was formed in Denmark in 2006, it was one of Europe's most coal-intensive companies and accounted for about a third of Denmark's carbon emissions. In 2008, fossil fuels still supplied 85% of its heat and power, and renewables just 15%. Its core business faced tightening European climate targets and public opposition, while offshore wind, the most promising alternative, was not yet a commercially mature industry. [1]

The company committed to reversing that ratio within 40 years. It dropped new coal projects and, in 2009, placed a single order for 500 turbines to create a supply chain for offshore wind. When falling gas prices hit revenues in 2012, it chose to expand renewables and wind down its fossil and utility businesses. In 2017 it sold its oil and gas production business and renamed itself Ørsted. In effect, the company changed what it was in business to do: it stopped selling fossil-fuel energy and started building and running offshore wind at industrial scale. [1]

By the company's own figures, renewables made up 99% of its energy generation in 2025, and emissions from its generation and operations had fallen 98% since 2006. These are company-reported numbers. The years 2023 to 2025 also showed the limits of the new model: DKK 28.4 billion in impairments on US projects, followed by a capital raise of roughly DKK 60 billion. [1][3][4]

The 10X lesson is that Ørsted did not try to improve its existing business. It decided that business would not carry its future, and it built an entirely new industrial capability before the market forced it to. The case also shows that getting the direction right does not guarantee success in every market or every project.

The Challenge: A Large Company on the Wrong Side of the Future

DONG Energy was created in 2006 by merging Denmark's state oil and gas company with five other Danish energy companies. It inherited coal plants and an oil and gas business. It also inherited something that looked marginal at the time: Vindeby, the world's first offshore wind farm, an 11-turbine project built in 1991. [1]

The company describes the pressures plainly: governments and the EU were setting carbon and renewable energy targets, the renewables sector was growing, and its carbon-intensive operations faced public opposition. Filip Engel, the company's Vice President of Sustainability, has said the shift was driven by top management under external pressure, including EU climate targets and public protests. [1][10]

That is a documented orientation toward the future. It is not evidence of a formal foresight or scenario-planning method, and none of the sources used here describe one.

The 10X Move

The decisive step was to move the company's center of gravity rather than bolt clean energy onto a fossil portfolio. Offshore wind, meaning turbines installed at sea where winds are stronger and steadier, would become the core business, and the old business would be dismantled step by step. The main barrier was cost: offshore wind lacked infrastructure and economies of scale. So the company bet on scale itself, with larger sites, larger turbines, orders big enough to build a supply chain, and cost reduction at every stage from manufacturing to operations and maintenance. [1]

How the Way of Working Changed: A Short Timeline

  1. 2008: 85% of heat and power is fossil-based. The company commits to reversing the ratio within 40 years, drops new coal projects and closes some existing ones. It says the decision was not universally popular inside what was then a leading fossil-fuel company. [1]
  2. 2009: An order for 500 Siemens turbines secures a supply chain for several offshore wind farms. [1]
  3. 2012: Falling gas prices hit revenues. The company decides to expand renewables and dismantle its fossil-fuel and utility businesses. [1]
  4. 2015: Global installed offshore wind capacity is about 8,000 MW, roughly a third of it built by the company, according to an article by one of its vice presidents. The article names the volume of capacity under construction as one of the main drivers of lower costs. [2]
  5. 2016: The company says the cost per MWh of new offshore wind fell below that of new coal- and gas-fired plants for the first time. The same year it went public through an initial public offering (IPO) built on an offshore wind investment story. [1]
  6. 2017: It sells its oil and gas production business, renames itself Ørsted, and sets its first emissions target validated by the Science Based Targets initiative (SBTi), a body that checks whether corporate targets are consistent with climate science. [1]
  7. 2024–2025: The last coal-fired plant, in Esbjerg, closes. The company reports meeting its Scope 1–2 target with a 99% renewable share, and refocuses on offshore wind. [1]

Evidence of Impact and Its Limits

The strongest evidence of transformation is the change in what the company owns. It no longer produces oil and gas, it has closed its last coal plant, and offshore wind is now its core business. These facts can be checked against its official announcements and published history. [1]

The headline percentages need more care. Comparing 85% in 2008 with 99% in 2025 sets two differently worded measures side by side: one refers to "heat and power production", the other to "energy generation". A UN Global Compact case study cites 90% at an earlier stage. Part of the shift also came from converting existing plants to run on biomass, fuel made from plant material such as wood, rather than from wind alone. The company describes its biomass as sustainable, but its climate effect depends on sourcing and accounting choices that are not assessed here. [1][10]

The 98% and 99% figures are company-reported, and the sources used here contain no independent audit of them. The claim that offshore wind became cheaper than coal and gas in 2016 is also a company claim. The closest external signal in these sources comes from public tenders in 2015, though it is reported through the company's own article: subsidy for the Horns Rev 3 project in Denmark came in about 30% lower than in 2010 after adjusting for inflation, and costs in a UK tender round fell by more than 30% over five years. [2]

The Price and the Learning: When Capability Does Not Cover Every Risk

In October 2023, Ørsted stopped developing its Ocean Wind 1 and 2 projects in the United States and recognized impairments, or write-downs in the book value of assets, of DKK 28.4 billion, of which DKK 19.9 billion related to Ocean Wind 1. The company cited supplier delays, higher long-term US interest rates, and revised assumptions on tax credits and permit timing. [3]

In August 2025, it announced a plan to raise about DKK 60 billion through a rights issue, selling new shares to existing shareholders at a discount, backed by the Danish state as majority shareholder. Days later, the US Bureau of Ocean Energy Management (BOEM) ordered work to stop on Revolution Wind, a project that was 80% complete, and the share price fell about 16% in Monday trading. The company went on to raise about DKK 59.6 billion, and a federal court ruling allowed work to resume. [4][5][6]

In March 2026, Revolution Wind began delivering power to the New England grid. In August 2026, the company said the project was more than 95% complete and expected full commercial operation by year-end, while full operation of Sunrise Wind had slipped to the second half of 2027. In its first-half 2026 results, Ørsted recorded a further DKK 1.2 billion impairment on its US projects, citing higher interest rates. [7][8][9]

The lesson is that the company succeeded in changing what it does, but rapid expansion into a new market with a different political and financing environment exposed the limits of that capability. In 2025 the company began refocusing on offshore wind in its core markets and sold its European onshore business. [1]

The 10X Lens

Foresight and Ambition

The goal of reversing the 85/15 ratio gave a clear, measurable picture of a different future rather than a general slogan. [1]

Redefining Value and Direction

Selling oil and gas and changing the name signaled that value would no longer come from the business that founded the company. [1]

Leadership and Transformation Culture

A decision that was not universally welcomed inside the company, led by top management under clear external pressure. [1][10]

Multiplying Capability

The turbine order and larger sites turned offshore wind from scattered projects into an industry with a supply chain and economies of scale. [1][2]

From Future to Impact

Desired Future → An energy company whose survival does not depend on fossil fuels.

Required Capabilities → Developing large offshore sites, a turbine supply chain, lower construction and operating costs, and the financial capacity for very large projects.

What Was Built → Orders at scale, larger sites, larger turbines, a public listing, and the sale of legacy assets to fund the new direction.

New Operating Behavior → A shift from selling energy made from fuel to developing, building, and operating offshore wind farms.

Outcomes and Impact → An exit from coal, oil, and gas and, by the company's account, a deep cut in emissions, alongside losses and delays in a US market whose political risks the company could not control.

Insider Critique and Evidence Limits

Someone who led this transformation from the inside would likely challenge three points. First, the story reads like a single company decision, yet the company itself points to a wider Danish effort to build a national wind industry, and the state is its majority shareholder. The result should not be assumed to transfer to a company without that backing. Second, the sources used here do not explain how the company rebuilt its workforce skills, who made project decisions, or how incentives changed. That gap should not be filled with guesswork. Third, the headline percentages are company-reported and their definitions have shifted over the years. [1][4][10]

What can be said with confidence: the company became able to develop, build, and operate offshore wind farms at a scale that did not exist anywhere when it started, and that capability became its core business rather than a side activity. What cannot be said: that the model guarantees profitability or protects against interest-rate and political shocks.

Putting It to Work

Ask what makes your core business a poor fit for the future you expect. Then set a transformation target as a measurable ratio, not a slogan. Identify the new capability you need and the real barrier in front of it, as lack of scale was for offshore wind. When you expand into new markets, test political and financing risk as rigorously as you test the technology.

Transferability

The lesson travels to organizations whose core business faces rising regulatory or technological pressure, such as carbon-intensive industries or services that digital models may overtake. But the size of the investment, state support, and the maturity of Denmark's wind industry are specific to this case. What transfers best is the logic of the decision: commit to a clear transformation ratio, build at a scale that changes the cost curve, and sell what no longer belongs to the future.

The 10X Question

What future do we want our organization to be operating in ten years from now, and will today's core business still carry it? If not, what new capability must we start building now, what are we prepared to give up to fund it, and how will we test the risks before we scale?
