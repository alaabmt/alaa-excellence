10X Case · Industry and Leadership

Same Workers, Different Results: How GM's Worst Plant Became a Model of Quality

General Motors closed its Fremont, California, plant in 1982 after years of absenteeism, grievances, and open conflict. Two years later the plant reopened with almost the same workforce, and its results were transformed. The people stayed the same. The system they worked in did not.

Executive Summary

In the early 1980s, GM's Fremont plant was one of the least productive in the company. Unexcused absenteeism often topped 20%, more than 1,000 grievances had piled up, and a senior union official described labor relations as an "ongoing war." GM shut the plant in 1982. [1]

Fremont was a symptom of a wider problem. GM did not know how to build a small car at a profit, it trailed its Japanese rivals on quality and productivity, and many of its managers believed line workers had little to contribute to improving the work. [1][2]

In 1984, GM and Toyota reopened the plant as a joint venture called NUMMI (New United Motor Manufacturing, Inc.). The bet was a bold one. Toyota agreed to rehire the entire union leadership, and 99% of assembly workers and 75% of skilled trades workers came from the old GM plant. [1]

What changed was how the work was organized. Workers were placed in small teams, some trained at a Toyota plant in Japan, and anyone on the line could now stop it to flag a problem. In the old plant, the rule had been that the line never stops. [3]

NUMMI went on to reach productivity and quality levels comparable to Toyota's plants in Japan, according to an academic review that draws on MIT research. [1] Workers and researchers interviewed later recalled steep drops in absenteeism and grievances. [3]

Much of this evidence is secondhand, however. GM took more than two decades to apply Toyota's practices consistently across its own plants, and NUMMI itself closed in 2010 after GM went bankrupt and withdrew. [1][3]

The 10X lesson lies in what was redesigned. NUMMI did not rely on new machines or a new workforce. It rebuilt the relationship between managers and workers: instead of policing people assumed to be careless, the system trusted them and expected them to stop the line when something went wrong. That kind of change cannot be photographed and copied.

A Plant Better Known for Conflict Than for Cars

A former union leader recalled that Fremont's workforce was considered the worst in the American auto industry, more focused on grievances and strikes than on building cars. On a normal day, one worker in five failed to show up, and Mondays were worse. [3]

Everyone knew the cardinal rule: the line must never stop. If a worker missed part of a job, the car moved on unfinished, and hundreds of vehicles needing repairs piled up in the lots outside. [3] One NUMMI worker, quoted by researchers, described how employees deliberately slowed down to protect their jobs, while managers answered with threats of firing. [1]

The 10X Move

NUMMI kept the workers and changed the assumptions behind their work. The old system treated each worker as the doer of one narrow task, left improvement to experts, and relied on inspectors to catch defects at the end of the line. The new system made workers part of solving problems, gave them the right to stop the line, and put a team leader beside them to help the moment a problem appeared. [1][3]

How Work Changed Inside the Plant

  1. In 1982, GM closed the Fremont plant and laid off thousands of workers. The following year, it began planning with Toyota to reopen the plant as a joint venture. [3]
  2. GM wanted to learn how to build a small car profitably and to bring an idle plant and workforce back into production. [2]
  3. Toyota agreed to rehire the entire union leadership, so most workers came from the old plant and belonged to the UAW (United Auto Workers). [1]
  4. Some workers were sent to a Toyota plant in Japan, where each spent about two weeks alongside a counterpart doing the same job they would do in Fremont. [3]
  5. Workers were organized into small teams, and an andon cord was strung along the line. Pulling it stops the line and calls the team leader to the problem. [3]
  6. The first car came off the line in December 1984. The plant went on to build about 6,000 vehicles a week on average, running two shifts a day. [3]
  7. In 1987 and 1988, when NUMMI ran below 65% of capacity, Toyota kept its production workers. It sent them to training, brought some contracted maintenance work back in-house, and assigned surplus workers to teams that designed the production process for the next model. [1]

Evidence of Impact and Its Limits

A 2014 study by economists from Case Western Reserve University and Harvard reports that NUMMI reached productivity and quality levels comparable to Toyota's Japanese plants, citing research from MIT's International Motor Vehicle Program. [1] That research began in 1986 by comparing NUMMI with the old Fremont plant and two other plants, and later grew to cover dozens of plants worldwide. [4]

Industrial engineering professor Jeffrey Liker said NUMMI's defects per 100 vehicles were among the best in America and matched those of Corollas shipped from Japan from the start. The same broadcast cites an unnamed study suggesting the old system would have needed about 50% more workers to build the same car. [3]

The original MIT productivity data are not available in the open sources used here, so the quantitative claims come from reliable secondary sources. The radio interviews rely on memories more than twenty years old, and the "50% more workers" figure comes from an unnamed study, so it should not be treated as an established result.

Why the Lessons Did Not Spread

Liker recalled a senior GM executive ordering a plant manager to photograph every inch of NUMMI and make his own plant look exactly the same. The manager knew at once it was impossible: employee motivation and good relations between union and management cannot be copied, or even photographed. [1][3]

When GM installed physical features such as the andon cord in other plants, the culture stayed the same. In some factories, workers were yelled at for pulling the cord. [1] The Van Nuys plant tried to adopt the system without Toyota as a partner; its quality did not improve, and GM closed it in 1992. [3]

The researchers argue that Toyota's real advantage lay in long-term relationships of trust with workers and suppliers, arrangements too complex to write fully into a contract, and that GM's history and structure made such relationships hard to build. GM's own production system took hold across the company only as managers trained at NUMMI and at Saturn, another GM effort to reinvent design, manufacturing, and labor relations, spread through the firm. It came too late to save GM from bankruptcy. [1]

The 10X Lens

Redefining value

Quality was built in at every station instead of being inspected at the end of the line. [1][3]

Reinventing the operating model

Workers became partners in solving problems, not just doers of narrow tasks. [1]

Leadership and a culture of change

Management proved it was serious through costly decisions, such as keeping workers when output fell. [1]

Multiplying capabilities

Workers trained beside counterparts in Japan and later helped design the production process for the next model. [1][3]

From Future to Impact

Desired future → GM wanted to build a small car with high quality and a profit, and to learn Toyota's methods from the inside. [2][3]

Capabilities needed → That required teams that could solve their own problems, team leaders who stepped in right away, and mutual trust between management and the union.

What was built → Training in Japan, a team-based structure, the andon cord, and a no-layoff commitment kept in practice. [1][3]

New way of working → Any worker could stop the line, and fixing problems became a shared responsibility.

Results → The plant reached quality and productivity close to Toyota's, but the impact stayed local for years because the parent company did not build the same capability elsewhere. [1]

Insider Critique and Evidence Limits

A NUMMI manager would likely push back on the phrase "same workers" if it suggests nothing else changed. The workers stayed, but daily management, training, and decision-making were all different, and Toyota trainers played a direct role in building the training programs. [1][2]

That manager might also stress that trust was earned through decisions with a price tag, such as keeping workers in 1987 and 1988 and letting union leaders order supplies for their teams without going through management. [1]

What we can say with confidence: the new system transformed the plant's results while most of the workforce stayed, and copying the physical features alone failed elsewhere. What we cannot say with confidence: the exact size of the productivity gain, since the original data are not available here.

Putting It to Work

Before you copy a success from one site to another, ask about what the photos do not show. How are decisions made there? Who is allowed to stop the work? What happens to the person who reports a problem? Then start with one small decision that proves to the team that raising a problem is rewarded, and check whether anyone actually responds to employees' suggestions.

Transferability

The lesson applies to hospitals, government agencies, and service organizations, anywhere staff are expected to surface errors. NUMMI's conditions were unusual, though. The plant had been closed, workers needed their jobs back, and Toyota was a hands-on partner that trained and followed up. [1][3] Van Nuys shows that without such conditions, good intentions may not be enough. [3]

The 10X Question

If, years from now, we want an organization where every employee raises a problem the moment it appears, without fear, what would daily work look like? And what must we start building now in training, leadership, and trust, beyond the tools themselves, to make that possible?
