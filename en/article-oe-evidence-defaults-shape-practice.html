What Happens When the Right Action Becomes the Default?

Dr. Alaa Mohammad Ahmed

22 October 2026 · 8 min read

Eleven U.S. hospitals did not hire more palliative care staff. They changed one setting instead: for older patients with advanced chronic illness, a palliative care consult was now ordered automatically, and doctors could cancel it. The share of patients who got a consult rose from 16.6% to 43.9%. Yet other studies show that defaults do not work everywhere, and that they change what people do before they change outcomes.

Back to articles

This article draws on four randomized trials published between 2023 and 2026 and two systematic reviews published in 2023 and 2024. All of them come from health care. Applying the idea to other organizations is the author's inference.

What happened when the consult was ordered by default?

Palliative care focuses on relieving symptoms and helping patients and families make treatment decisions during serious illness. Researchers wanted to know whether changing one default in the electronic ordering system could increase palliative care consults. Their trial covered 34,239 patients aged 65 or older with advanced chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), dementia, or kidney failure, admitted to 11 U.S. hospitals between 2016 and 2018. [1]

Under usual care, a consult happened only if a doctor remembered to order it. In the intervention group, the consult was ordered automatically for every eligible patient, and the doctor could cancel it. Hospitals switched to the new setting one after another, on dates chosen at random. [1]

The main analysis included 24,065 patients who stayed at least 72 hours. With the default order, 43.9% received a consult, compared with 16.6% under usual care. Consults also came more than a day earlier: 3.4 days after admission on average, instead of 4.6. More patients left the hospital with a do-not-resuscitate order or went to hospice care. [1]

But the study's primary goal was not met. Length of hospital stay did not change, and in-hospital deaths were similar. [1]

Why do defaults change what people do?

A 2023 trial at an academic hospital in Kansas City randomly assigned 1,000 inpatients who smoked to one of two approaches. In the usual approach, treatment was offered only to people who said they were ready to quit. In the other, treatment started for everyone: nicotine replacement in the hospital, a prescription at discharge, and four counseling calls. Patients could turn down any part of it. [2]

In the automatic group, 60% used quit-smoking medication after discharge, compared with 34% in the usual group. And 89% completed at least one counseling call, compared with 37%. After one month, 22% had quit, compared with 16%. By six months, the two groups were almost the same, at 19% and 18%. [2]

Why does this happen? One likely explanation, offered here by the author rather than measured in these trials, is that accepting a default costs nothing, while leaving it takes a decision and some effort. Many people also read a default as a quiet recommendation from whoever designed the system. That means the person who builds a form or an order set is deciding, often without meaning to, what most users will do.

When do defaults fail?

At the University of Washington in Seattle, 118 surgical clinicians were randomly assigned to one of two versions of the electronic discharge order set after common operations, such as laparoscopic gallbladder and appendix removal. One version included a pre-filled opioid prescription with a suggested amount. Overall, the amount of opioid prescribed did not differ between the groups. The reason: only 21% of patients in the intervention group received the pre-filled prescription. For the rest, the prescription was typed in by hand. Even so, the pre-filled prescriptions were clearly smaller than the hand-written ones. [3]

In 12 intensive care units between 2021 and 2023, researchers tested a ventilator order that pre-set the lower breathing volume recommended in guidelines. Clinicians who chose a higher volume had to write a reason. Across 4,412 patients, adherence to the recommended setting did not rise significantly, and the effect varied from unit to unit. Staff interviews suggested why: clinician experience, the culture of teamwork between professions, and habits that units had formed during the COVID-19 pandemic. [4]

Systematic reviews show the same mixed picture. A 2023 review of hospital studies found that 19 of 24 interventions (79.2%) improved care delivery, and changing the default was the most common type. But only one study had a low risk of bias, and few looked at long-term effects. [5] A 2024 review of 54 randomized trials in primary care looked at behavioral prompts built into electronic health records (EHRs), including defaults. Most documentation measures improved (30 of 38), but far fewer measures of patient safety (4 of 12) or clinical outcomes (1 of 7) did. [6]

What can leaders do?

These steps come from the findings of the six studies. Where a step is the author's inference, the text says so.

  1. Map the defaults in your processes. Ask what happens automatically in forms, order sets, and booking templates when nobody chooses anything. These settings shape what many employees do every day, even if no one planned it that way.
  2. Make the recommended action the default when the evidence for it is strong, but keep opting out easy and record the reason. Both the palliative care and the smoking trials kept people's right to say no. [1][2]
  3. Put the default where people actually work. If staff can skip it by typing their own entry, many will, as the surgeons did. [3]
  4. Measure two things separately: how often the default is used, and whether the outcome you care about changes. The action can change while the outcome stays the same, as with length of stay. [1][3]
  5. Before you switch a default on, check that the team receiving the extra work can handle it. This is the author's inference: when requests double without more capacity, the wait may simply move somewhere else.
  6. Review the effect unit by unit and over enough time, not just as an overall average. Effects varied between ICUs, and long-term effects are rarely measured. [4][5]

A default cannot replace the quality of the service it leads to. Opt-out care doubled engagement with smoking treatment, but the researchers suggest that lasting quit rates need stronger and longer treatment. [2]

What we don't know

The palliative care trial is large and randomized, but its data come from U.S. hospitals in 2016–2018, and it did not meet its primary goal. [1] The smoking trial took place in one hospital and used a Bayesian analysis, which reports results as probabilities. [2] The surgical trial was run in one academic center and covered 663 operations. [3] Both systematic reviews rated most included studies at moderate or higher risk of bias. [5][6]

None of these studies included organizations in the Arab region, and all of them come from health care. Applying the idea to government forms, customer service, or procurement is a reasonable inference, but these studies did not test it. Defaults also steer behavior without asking people directly, so they call for transparency and strong evidence that the default serves the people it affects.

The 10X lens

An organization that asks thousands of employees to remember the right action every day is betting on their memory and attention under pressure. The real shift comes when the right action is designed into the system once, so it becomes the easiest path, while people keep the right to step off it when they have a reason. The evidence is also a reminder that a default changes actions; it does not guarantee results. Results still depend on good service, enough capacity, and continuous measurement behind the default.

Key terms

Default option
What happens automatically when a person does not choose something else.
Opt-out
Everyone receives the service unless they decline it. The opposite, opt-in, means only people who ask for it receive it.
Nudge
A change in how choices are presented that makes one decision easier without blocking the alternatives.
Order set
A ready-made group of orders in the electronic health record for a specific condition, from which clinicians choose.
Palliative care
Care that relieves symptoms and helps patients and families make treatment decisions during serious illness.

The question that remains

Three years from now, do you want the right action in your organization to depend on each person's memory and attention, or to be the easiest path in every form and system? If you want the second, which defaults should you review today, and how will you know they changed the outcome and not just the action?
