How Brazil Moved Primary Care from Waiting Rooms into Communities

Brazil's problem was not simply a shortage of clinics. A health system often learns about people only after they arrive. The Family Health Strategy changed the unit of responsibility: multidisciplinary teams became accountable for defined communities, with community health workers linking homes, local risks, and clinical care.

Executive Snapshot

Access to health care remained uneven across Brazil, while the system had historically leaned toward hospital treatment after illness appeared. In a country that stretches from large cities to rural and remote communities, opening a facility is not enough if people cannot reach it and the care team does not know who lives in its area or which risks are accumulating.

Brazil began expanding the Family Health Strategy—Estratégia Saúde da Família (ESF)—in 1994. The model assigns multidisciplinary primary-care teams to a territory and registered population. A team includes, at minimum, a physician, nurse, nursing assistant or technician, and community health workers. Its work spans clinical care, home visits, prevention, follow-up, health surveillance, and referral coordination.[1][2]

The Ministry of Health reported 53,356 teams in operation in 2024, supported by about 271,000 community health workers.[3] Longitudinal studies associate wider coverage with lower infant mortality and lower mortality from conditions that timely care can address. Those findings do not establish that ESF alone caused the improvement. A large national study also found no reduction in avoidable hospital admissions attributable to the strategy, showing that wider primary-care reach does not automatically repair weak hospital integration or uneven implementation quality.[7][9][10]

The 10X significance lies in the shift from treating whoever arrives to maintaining responsibility for a known population. Local presence, stable teams, governance quality, and working referral pathways are not supporting details; they are part of the outcome-producing system.

The people a waiting room cannot see

A primary-care service built around incoming visits misses people kept away by distance, cost, limited information, or mistrust. It can also miss households in which risks accumulate until an avoidable problem becomes an emergency.

Brazil created the Sistema Único de Saúde (SUS), or Unified Health System, in 1990 to extend the right to health care. A legal right, however, does not by itself produce equitable access or continuous care. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development described Brazil's system as historically hospital-centered and identified ESF as a central mechanism for rebalancing care toward primary and preventive services.[4]

The operational question was therefore larger than the number of clinics: how could the system become responsible for people who might not seek help in time?

The decisive move: from visits to populations

ESF changed the starting point. Each team is assigned a registered population within a defined territory rather than simply receiving an unrelated stream of appointments. Current rules set a planning parameter of 2,000 to 3,000 registered people per team, depending on municipal population.[2]

Basic health units remain the clinical base. The redesign connects them to neighborhoods and homes; it does not replace facilities. Teams combine clinical care, vaccination, prevention, follow-up, and surveillance. Community health workers operate at the boundary between households and the clinic. They register families, update information, visit homes, identify needs and risks, and connect what is happening in the neighborhood to the team's decisions. The World Health Organization presents this role as a way to integrate public health and primary care by extending what a team can know and act on beyond clinic walls.[5]

Community health workers do not replace physicians or nurses. Their value is the additional institutional capability they create: knowing who lives in the area, who has dropped out of follow-up, where risks are emerging, and who may need a visit, referral, or preventive intervention.

How the model reached national scale

The rollout depends on an interlocking chain that combines federal direction with municipal adaptation. Decentralization creates room to fit local conditions, but it also makes municipal management a source of variation in results.

  • The federal government sets policy, financing rules, and accreditation standards.
  • Municipalities organize teams and basic health units.
  • Each team holds responsibility for a territory and registered population.
  • Community health workers carry information from households to the team and bring education and follow-up back into the community.
  • The team provides primary care and coordinates movement into specialist and hospital services.

For 2024, the Ministry of Health reported 53,356 active teams, including 4,750 added teams, alongside 271,000 community health workers and 67,600 endemic-disease control workers.[3] These figures demonstrate implementation scale. They do not establish uniform quality, continuity, or health outcomes.

A recent 30-year review gives slightly different 2024 totals and explains that Brazil changed its coverage indicator after 2020 and again in 2024.[6] Historical and current coverage percentages therefore cannot be merged into one clean trend without preserving each definition.

What the results actually show

Evidence repeatedly links wider ESF coverage to improvement in selected mortality outcomes.

A longitudinal study used data from Brazil's 27 states between 1990 and 2002. Over that period, average program coverage rose from zero to 36%, while infant mortality fell from 49.7 to 28.9 deaths per 1,000 live births. After adjusting for factors including water and sanitation, income, women's literacy, fertility, health workforce, and hospital beds, every 10-percentage-point increase in coverage was associated with a 4.5% decline in infant mortality.[7] The researchers described the program as an important contributor, not the only cause.

Another nationwide analysis covering 2000–2009 associated wider and longer-running coverage with lower mortality and hospitalization from heart and cerebrovascular conditions considered responsive to primary care.[8] A separate study of 1,622 municipalities estimated that moving from no coverage to full coverage was associated with a 6.8% reduction in amenable mortality, with stronger results where municipal health governance was better.[9]

The pattern does not hold across every measure. A study of 5,506 municipalities from 2000 to 2014 found that coverage rose from 14% to 64% and primary-care consultations increased, but it did not find that ESF reduced avoidable hospital admissions.[10] A systematic review likewise found recurring associations with lower child mortality but no clear pattern across studies of avoidable hospitalization.[11]

The defensible conclusion is that ESF contributed to wider access and selected health gains within a much broader transformation. The evidence does not justify saying that coverage alone reduced every form of hospital use or caused Brazil's national health improvement.

Why coverage is not enough

The 30-year review documents persistent gaps in equipment, staffing, and service scope across regions. Some facilities do not conduct annual community assessments. Administrative work can draw community health workers away from homes, and coordination with specialist and hospital networks remains weak in many areas.[6]

That helps explain the apparent contradiction. A municipality can enroll more residents and deliver more primary-care visits without preventing hospitalization when specialists, beds, or timely referral pathways remain unavailable. Coverage opens a door. Team stability, infrastructure, information, and network integration determine where that door leads.

The case through the 10X lens

Foresight and ambition

Brazil raised the ambition from serving people who presented for care to building continuous primary care for the whole population. The sources do not establish a formal foresight methodology, so the case makes no claim about scenario planning or institutional futures practice.

Redefining value and strategic direction

Value moved beyond visit volume toward knowledge of a population, prevention, continuity, and reduced inequality.

Leadership and future governance

Federal policy and financing operate through decentralized municipal delivery. Evidence that stronger local governance changes results makes governance part of the mechanism, not administrative background.

Capability multiplication

Teams and community health workers gave the system greater capacity to identify needs outside facilities and reach populations that might otherwise remain invisible.

Reinventing the operating model

This is the core transformation: the unit of responsibility shifted from an individual visit to a registered population in a defined place.

Institutional intelligence

Registration, home contact, and local knowledge turn community conditions into inputs for follow-up and prevention. This is institutional intelligence, not an artificial-intelligence or automated-decision claim.

Sustainable value and results

Three decades of operation, national scale, progressive reach, and repeated mortality associations support the pillar. Mixed hospitalization findings and uneven quality limit any universal claim.

The evidence reviewed does not provide a sufficiently specific national pilot, learning loop, and scale protocol to support a separate innovation-and-experimentation pillar. It is therefore excluded.

What another organization can use

First, assign each service team a known population or customer group, not merely an incoming queue. Responsibility for a defined group changes what a team looks for and how it allocates time.

Second, build a trusted boundary-spanning role between frontline professionals and people's daily lives. That role might be a community worker, relationship lead, or case coordinator. Its field time must be protected from administrative work that would consume the very presence the model depends on.

Third, connect the role to a team with decision rights and resources. Gathering local information without the ability to respond turns field presence into another reporting burden.

Fourth, measure reach and quality together. Enrollment and visit counts describe activity but do not establish continuity, resolved need, or equitable outcomes.

Fifth, design referral as part of the service. Primary care cannot prevent deterioration when access to specialists or hospitals fails at the point of escalation.

Transferability and its limits

The principle travels more easily than the exact form. Teams accountable for known populations, local presence, and continuity can inform health care, education, social services, insurance, and government services.

Copying the community-worker role without stable financing, clear authority, training, data protection, and referral capacity may add a new layer of work without changing results. A model that works in a small municipality may also perform differently in a dense city or a region with severe workforce shortages.

The 10X Question

If the future we want is one in which an organization does not wait for problems to arrive at its door, which population will each team be accountable for—and what data, local presence, skills, decision rights, and referral capacity must be built now?
