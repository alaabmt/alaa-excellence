When Cutting Costs Is Not Austerity: How Aravind Redesigned Cataract Surgery

Aravind Eye Care System is an Indian network specializing in eye care. It runs hospitals and centers for screening, treatment and training, and it manufactures ophthalmic supplies. It has built an operating model that can treat very large numbers of patients while keeping clinical quality high and providing a large share of its services free or at a subsidized cost.

Executive Snapshot

In the year ending March 2025, Aravind recorded more than 6 million outpatient visits and 734,322 surgical, laser or injection procedures. Of these, 257,052 were subsidized and 106,770 were provided free through screening camps.[1]

What is cataract surgery?

A cataract is a clouding of the eye’s natural lens that makes vision blurry or dim. It can worsen until it gets in the way of reading, driving, work and daily life. It is treated surgically: the clouded lens is removed and a clear artificial lens is implanted in the eye.

The central idea: What matters in this case is not only the surgical technique, but how Aravind redesigned the system that takes a patient from screening to treatment and uses the time of surgeons, staff and supplies far more efficiently.

The challenge: when the treatment is known but hard to reach

Cataract surgery is a well-established and effective procedure, but having a treatment does not solve the problem. Price, distance, a shortage of eye surgeons or limited capacity can keep patients from getting the operation.

In the traditional model, part of a specialist’s time can be spent on tasks that a trained team could do safely. When demand rises, that scarce expertise becomes the bottleneck.

The 10X MoveInstead of asking surgeons to work faster, Aravind redistributed the whole workload, so that doctors spend their time on tasks that genuinely need a doctor, while other tasks are carried out by trained teams following standardized protocols.

How Aravind redesigned the work

Aravind broke the patient journey down into specific tasks and assigned each one to the most suitable professional level. Much of the screening, measurement, preparation and support work is done by trained support staff, while ophthalmologists focus on diagnosis, medical decisions and surgery.

The design did not stop at the operating room. The network uses screening camps and vision centers in rural and semi-rural areas to bring care closer to people. According to Aravind, vision centers provide primary eye care and connect more complex cases to the hospital remotely or by referral.[2]

The network also created Aurolab, a unit that manufactures ophthalmic supplies and began by developing intraocular lenses at a more affordable price. This vertical integration made the cost of some inputs part of the design problem itself.[3]

What the evidence shows: volume did not come at the expense of quality

The strongest recent evidence comes from an analysis of 3.6 million cataract operations performed at Aravind hospitals between 2012 and 2023. It found that visual outcomes after surgery improved steadily over the period.[4]

In an earlier study of high-volume surgery, 94% of patients who completed follow-up reached corrected visual acuity of 6/18 or better, with an average surgical time of 3.75 minutes per case.[5]

On the economic side, a study in Health Affairs estimated the total cost per operation at about US$120, or US$195 per quality-adjusted life year (QALY), a measure that combines length of life with health-related quality of life.[6]

Evidence limits: These studies show that Aravind has combined high volume, quality and low cost, but they do not isolate the effect of each operational element. The results therefore cannot all be attributed to one way of dividing work or to manufacturing alone.

The Tamayuz 10X Lens

Value and strategic direction

Value is defined not by the number of operations or by revenue alone, but by the ability to restore sight with quality, reach and financial sustainability.

Capability multiplication and empowerment

Instead of treating the shortage of eye surgeons only as a recruitment problem, the network redistributed the work and built a specialized support workforce.

Operating-model redesign

Hospitals, vision centers, community outreach, training and manufacturing work as one system that tackles the limits on access, cost and capacity at the same time.

Value and sustainable results

The real test is not how much the system saves, but whether it can sustain access, quality and scale together.

What leaders can use

For leaders: protect the time of scarce expertise

Identify the work that genuinely needs the highest level of expertise, then check whether other tasks are using up that time unnecessarily.

For organizations: redraw the service journey

Sort each step into work that needs high specialization, work that can be standardized, work that can be delegated, and work that can move closer to the people served.

Where the Principle Transfers

Aravind cannot be copied literally into every health service or organization. Cataract surgery lends itself well to standardization and repetition, while other services need more tailoring.

The design principles do transfer: protect the time of scarce expertise, standardize what can be standardized, build the workforce around clear roles, reduce waiting, and bring the service closer to the people it serves.

In the health sector in particular, the delegation of tasks must comply with the licensing laws, scope of practice and safety standards of each country.
The 10X QuestionIf the demand for the service doubles tomorrow, will your organization double the resources as well, or redesign the way it works to get more done with the same resources?
