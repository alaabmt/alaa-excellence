Why Did Jacob Send His Sons to Search After They Had Given Up?

When a grieving heart complains to Allah alone, and hope becomes an order to search rather than a call to wait

Methodological note: This is a contemporary reflection on verses from Surah Yusuf (12:80–87), read in light of the classical commentaries. It does not offer a new interpretation of the verses, and it does not attribute modern management ideas to the Qur’an.

The brothers of Joseph came back from Egypt a second time without their youngest brother. He had been detained there, accused of theft, and the eldest of them had stayed behind because he could not bring himself to face their father. Before leaving, they had pleaded with the ruler of Egypt to release the boy, and found every door closed:

﴿فَلَمَّا اسْتَيْأَسُوا مِنْهُ خَلَصُوا نَجِيًّا﴾Meaning in English: “So when they had despaired of him, they secluded themselves in private consultation.”Surah Yusuf, 12:80

Then they stood before their father, Jacob, peace be upon him, with a second piece of news as hard as the first. He had lost Joseph years before. Now he was losing Joseph’s brother as well, and the son who had stayed in Egypt with him. This was his answer:

﴿قَالَ بَلْ سَوَّلَتْ لَكُمْ أَنفُسُكُمْ أَمْرًا ۖ فَصَبْرٌ جَمِيلٌ ۖ عَسَى اللَّهُ أَن يَأْتِيَنِي بِهِمْ جَمِيعًا ۚ إِنَّهُ هُوَ الْعَلِيمُ الْحَكِيمُ﴾Meaning in English: [Jacob] said, “Rather, your souls have enticed you to something, so patience is most fitting. Perhaps Allāh will bring them to me all together. Indeed, it is He who is the Knowing, the Wise.”Surah Yusuf, 12:83
﴿وَتَوَلَّىٰ عَنْهُمْ وَقَالَ يَا أَسَفَىٰ عَلَىٰ يُوسُفَ وَابْيَضَّتْ عَيْنَاهُ مِنَ الْحُزْنِ فَهُوَ كَظِيمٌ﴾Meaning in English: And he turned away from them and said, “Oh, my sorrow over Joseph,” and his eyes became white from grief, for he was [of that] a suppressor.Surah Yusuf, 12:84

His sons, worried for him, said: By Allāh, you will not cease remembering Joseph until you become fatally ill or become of those who perish. Yusuf, 12:85. He replied: I only complain of my suffering and my grief to Allāh, and I know from Allāh that which you do not know. Yusuf, 12:86. Then he told them something they did not expect:

﴿يَا بَنِيَّ اذْهَبُوا فَتَحَسَّسُوا مِن يُوسُفَ وَأَخِيهِ وَلَا تَيْأَسُوا مِن رَّوْحِ اللَّهِ ۖ إِنَّهُ لَا يَيْأَسُ مِن رَّوْحِ اللَّهِ إِلَّا الْقَوْمُ الْكَافِرُونَ﴾Meaning in English: “O my sons, go and find out about Joseph and his brother and despair not of relief from Allāh. Indeed, no one despairs of relief from Allāh except the disbelieving people.”Surah Yusuf, 12:87

The deeper the loss, the wider the hope

After a second blow, we might expect Jacob’s hope to shrink, so that he would ask Allah to return only the son he had just lost. Instead he said: Perhaps Allāh will bring them to me all together. Al-Saadi explains “all together” as Joseph, Benjamin and the eldest brother who had stayed in Egypt. He describes beautiful patience as patience “that carries no resentment, no panic, and no complaint to people,” and says Jacob turned to Allah for relief “when he saw that the matter had become severe” [1].

That is the meaning of faith at the heart of the scene. Hope in Allah is not measured by how much is left in our hands, but by our knowledge of the One we hope in. Jacob ended his words with two of Allah’s names: Indeed, it is He who is the Knowing, the Wise. He knows Jacob’s state and everything hidden from him, and in His wisdom He has set an end to every matter.

A grief that fills the heart and is taken to Allah alone

Beautiful patience did not mean that Jacob felt no grief. His eyes turned white from it, and the Qur’an describes him as one who suppressed it. Al-Tabari explains the word as someone filled with grief, “holding it in and not showing it” [2]. He explains I only complain of my suffering and my grief to Allāh as “I complain of my worry and my grief to none but Allah,” and he reports from Qatada that “no trial ever came down on him without his good opinion of Allah coming after it” [3].

The commentators differed over what Jacob meant by and I know from Allāh that which you do not know. Al-Qurtubi reports from Ibn Abbas that Jacob knew Joseph’s dream was true, and he records another view: “I know of Allah’s kindness to me what makes me think well of Him” [4]. On either reading, his hope was not an empty wish. It rested on knowledge of Allah. The scene seems to hold together two things that might look contradictory: a heart so full of grief that it affected his sight, and a tongue that complained only to Allah and never gave up hope.

The scene opens with despair and closes by forbidding it

Notice that the scene begins with the brothers: So when they had despaired of him, and ends with their father’s instruction: and despair not of relief from Allāh. Between the two, no hopeful news arrived and no one who was missing came home. What changed was where they were looking. The brothers had despaired of the ruler of Egypt. Jacob’s hope was in Allah.

We might also reflect that the brothers had given up on the very man who was Joseph, without knowing it. Al-Saadi explains they had despaired of him as despairing “of Joseph allowing them their brother” [5]. The door they thought was shut was the door through which relief would come.

Al-Saadi then draws the link between hope and action in his comment on the last verse: “Hope leads a servant to strive and work hard for what he hopes for, and despair leads him to heaviness and slowness.” He adds that those who despair of Allah’s relief do so because “in their disbelief they think His mercy far off” [6]. Jacob’s warning against despair was more than comfort for his sons. It was an order to move.

Why did he tell them to search instead of wait?

Jacob did not say “wait,” or “pray and leave it there.” He said: go and find out. The Arabic verb here is tahassasu, from the root for the senses. Al-Qurtubi defines it as “seeking something with the senses” [7]. Ibn Kathir says Jacob “urged his sons to go out in the land and seek news of Joseph and his brother,” and he distinguishes this word from a close relative, tajassus, often translated as spying: “tahassus is used for good, and tajassus for evil” [8]. The word carries a search that asks, looks and follows the trail, with good intent.

It is worth noticing, too, that he sent the very sons he had just reproached with Rather, your souls have enticed you to something. His reproach did not stop him from trusting them with the search, and he addressed them with the same tender words: O my sons.

What do we do when the wait for what we hope for goes on?

Here we turn to our own work, as reflection rather than interpretation, and with the difference kept in view. Jacob was a prophet. What he knew from Allah is not something any of us possesses, and the story ended through Allah’s decree and arrangement, not through a human plan. Still, the scene raises a question for anyone who has waited a long time for something they hope for.

Take a hypothetical example. A hospital team tried twice to cut waiting times in a crowded clinic, and both attempts failed. Their report concluded that the problem could not be solved with the current staff and budget. The director could have closed the file, or blamed the team. Instead, she asked them to go back and spend a full day in the clinic: to watch for themselves, listen to patients and staff, and follow up the small observations they had dismissed both times. She did not promise them success. She simply refused to let despair be the decision.

What questions do the eight 10X pillars open?

We are not saying that the verses offer a modern management model. But they raise questions that meet three of the eight pillars of 10X excellence.

Ambition does not have to shrink after every loss

On the pillar of foresight and shaping ambition, we might reflect that Jacob did not lower his hope after the second loss. He widened it, to the point of saying all together. His hope was in Allah, not in a plan of his own, but the scene still raises a question about our own ambition. Organizations often lower their sights after each setback until the goal becomes holding on to what is left. Are we still drawing the future we want, or drawing it to the size of our last loss?

A leader’s grief need not become the team’s despair

On the pillar of leadership, a culture of change and future-ready governance, the Qur’an describes Jacob as one who suppressed his grief. He carried it without unloading it on those around him, and he was the one who sent his sons back onto the road. A leader who admits how heavy a situation is, yet refuses to let despair become the team’s language, builds a culture that returns to work after failure instead of stopping there.

The search starts in the field, not in the report

On the pillar of maximizing organizational and technological intelligence, go and find out reminds us that knowledge is sought with the senses: by looking, asking and following the trail. An organization rich in data can still miss the small signal that never shows up in a spreadsheet, just as it can miss it by settling for “we tried and it didn’t work.”

Questions to carry into a long wait

  1. What have we given up on because we looked only at the means, and forgot that the whole matter belongs to Allah?
  2. To whom do we complain of what weighs on us, and does our complaining make things heavier for the people around us?
  3. Which file have we closed that deserved a second search?
  4. Do we trust people we have disagreed with to take part in the search, or shut them out because of the past?

The 10X excellence question

What could your organization do a few years from now if its team went back to searching whenever it thought the doors were closed?

Imagine your organization a few years from now. When a long project stalls, the team does not close the file. It goes back to the field to ask, look and follow the trail, and its leaders carry the weight of hard moments without passing despair on to the people around them. What will the organization be able to do then that it cannot do today? And which habit will you start building now?

A final reflection

The scene opened with brothers who had despaired, and a father who met the second blow with patience is most fitting, then hoped his Lord would return every one of the missing: Perhaps Allāh will bring them to me all together. He grieved until his eyes became white from grief, and complained to no one but his Lord: I only complain of my suffering and my grief to Allāh.

Then he did not stop at complaint. He said: O my sons, go and find out about Joseph and his brother. What we might carry from this scene is to grieve without despairing, to take what weighs on us to Allah, and then to go out and search and ask, with his words in our ears: and despair not of relief from Allāh.
