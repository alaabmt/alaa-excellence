What Happened When Hospitals Started Using a Surgical Safety Checklist?

Dr. Alaa Mohammad Ahmed

16 November 2026 · 7 min read

In an operating room, each team member may assume someone else has checked something important, such as the patient's name or the surgical site. The World Health Organization designed a short checklist that the team reviews together at three points during an operation. In eight hospitals, deaths fell by about half after the checklist was introduced. But when the Canadian province of Ontario encouraged all its hospitals to adopt it, death and complication rates showed no clear change from before adoption to after. What might explain the difference?

This article draws on five studies published from 2009 to 2025: a study in eight hospitals around the world (2009), a study in 101 hospitals in Ontario, Canada (2014), and three reviews of earlier studies (2021, 2022, and 2025). All five are about surgery. Any point about other kinds of organizations is the author's inference.

What happened in the first eight hospitals?

The Surgical Safety Checklist is a 19-item list that the World Health Organization designed to improve communication within the team and to make key steps consistent from one operation to the next. The team reviews it at three points: before anesthesia, before the first incision, and before the patient leaves the operating room. [1][5]

In 2007 and 2008, eight hospitals in eight cities tested it, including Amman in Jordan, Toronto in Canada, New Delhi in India, London in England, and Seattle in the United States. Researchers collected data on 3,733 patients before the checklist and 3,955 patients after it. All were aged 16 or older and had surgery other than heart surgery. [1]

The death rate fell from 1.5% to 0.8%, and the rate of complications during the hospital stay fell from 11% to 7%. Both drops were too large to be explained by chance alone. But this was a before-and-after study: it compared two periods in the same hospitals and did not compare them with hospitals that did not use the checklist. [1]

What happened when Ontario encouraged all its hospitals to use the checklist?

After these and similar results, the checklist spread quickly around the world. In Ontario, Canada, a policy encouraged every hospital to adopt it. Researchers compared surgical outcomes in 101 hospitals during the three months before adoption and the three months after. The comparison covered 109,341 operations before and 106,370 after. [2]

The researchers adjusted the rates for differences between patients in the two periods. The adjusted risk of death in the hospital or within 30 days of surgery was 0.71% before the checklist and 0.65% after it. The adjusted risk of complications was 3.86% before and 3.82% after. Neither difference was statistically significant, so either one could be due to chance. These figures should not be compared with those of the 2009 study, because the patients and the definition of complications differed. [2]

What might explain the different results?

A review published in 2021 pooled the findings of 20 systematic reviews of the checklist. A systematic review is a study that gathers the published research on one question and assesses its quality. It analyzed 24 before-and-after studies that together reported 18 clinical outcomes. Nine of them favored the checklist, including deaths, complications, and surgical site infections. Deep vein thrombosis was the only outcome assessed that did not. [3]

Another review, published in 2022, looked at 300 studies. Only 38% of them gave even some description of how the checklist was actually used, rather than just whether it was used. In those studies, the checklist's link to team communication, understanding of the case, and safety culture was clearer than its link to complications and deaths. Safety culture means how ready the team is to talk about errors and risks. [4]

A third review, published in 2025, pooled 13 studies with 17,867 participants. It estimated average compliance with the checklist at 73%, as each study defined compliance. Compliance was 76% for the first step, before anesthesia, but 61% for the step before the first incision and 62% for the step before the patient leaves the operating room. It estimated completion of every item on the list at 51%. [5]

Compliance and completeness are different measures. Compliance asks whether the checklist was used in an operation; completeness asks whether every item was done. So the 51% should not be compared with the 73%. [5]

The same review listed what helps compliance: earlier experience with the checklist, training, a positive work environment, management support, and regular monitoring with feedback to teams. It also listed barriers: too few staff, heavy workloads, no one owning the checklist, resistance to change, weak audits, and high staff turnover. [5]

What can leaders do?

These steps are the author's inference from the studies. No study tested their combined effect.

  1. Measure how the checklist is used, not just whether it was signed. [4][5]
  2. Focus on the steps with lower compliance, such as the step before the first incision and the step before the patient leaves the operating room. [5]
  3. Train teams, monitor compliance regularly and share the results with them, and show that management supports the checklist. [5]
  4. Name someone in each operating room who owns the checklist and follows up on it. [5]
  5. Do not rely on a policy that simply encourages the checklist; in Ontario, death and complication rates showed no clear change after adoption. [2]
  6. After the checklist starts, track team communication as well as complications and deaths. [4]

Limits of the evidence

Both the eight-hospital study and the Ontario study were before-and-after studies, and neither was randomized: no hospitals were assigned to go without the checklist for comparison. [1][2] The Ontario study used administrative health records, the records health agencies keep on admissions, discharges, and procedures, rather than data the researchers collected in operating rooms. It compared only three months before adoption with three months after. [2]

The 24 outcome studies in the 2021 review were all before-and-after studies. [3] The 2025 review included only 13 studies. [5]

One of the eight hospitals was in Amman, but the study abstract does not report results for each hospital separately. [1] None of the five studies tested the checklist outside surgery. The claim that it works in other sectors is a reasonable inference, but these studies did not test it.

The 10X lens

In the author's reading of these studies, having a checklist, or even signing it, may not be enough. The checklist is more likely to help when the team actually pauses, reads the items together, and anyone can raise a concern. If the checklist becomes a form filled in after the operation, it may stay in the file without any effect on patients. The author also believes the same idea may help other work with critical steps, such as maintenance and equipment operation, but these studies did not test that.

Key terms

Surgical Safety Checklist
A 19-item list designed by the World Health Organization, which the surgical team reviews at three points during an operation.
Before-and-after study
A study that compares results in the same place before and after a change, with no group where the change did not happen.
Systematic review
A study that gathers the published research on one question, assesses its quality, and summarizes the results.
Statistical significance
When a difference is too large to be explained by chance alone.
Compliance
The share of operations in which the checklist was used, fully or partly (definitions varied between studies).
Completeness
Whether every item on the checklist is done, not just some of them.

The question that remains

Three years from now, what will a checklist mean in your organization: a form that is signed and filed, or a real pause in which the team reads each item aloud and anyone can raise a concern? And who will make sure that the items teams usually skip are not skipped?
