What Happened When Hospitals Standardized Patient Handoffs Between Shifts?

Dr. Alaa Mohammad Ahmed

31 October 2026 · 8 min read

When a doctor's shift ends, they hand over what they know about each patient to the next doctor: how the patient is doing, which tests are pending, and what to do if things get worse. Miscommunication at this handoff is a leading source of medical errors. In 32 U.S. hospitals, resident doctors learned a standard way to do it, called I-PASS, with 18 months of coaching. Reported handoff-related adverse events fell by 47%. Yet other studies show mixed results, and results were more often positive when training came together with changes to how the work is done.

This article draws on a multi-hospital study published in 2023, a randomized trial published in 2026, and two systematic reviews published in 2025. All of them come from hospitals. Applying the idea to other organizations is the author's inference.

What changed in 32 hospitals?

The study covered 32 U.S. hospitals: 12 community hospitals and 20 academic hospitals. It included 2,735 resident physicians, meaning doctors working in hospitals while they train in a specialty, from internal medicine, pediatrics, and other fields. Another 760 senior physicians led the change in their own departments. [1]

Participants learned a standard handoff method called I-PASS. Each letter is a step: how sick the patient is, a short patient summary, a list of actions to take, what could happen and what to do if it does, and finally the receiver sums up what they heard in their own words. Coaching teams from outside the hospitals supported them for 18 months and reviewed performance measures with them every month. [1]

Handoff-related adverse events, as reported by residents in regular surveys, fell by 47%. Major events dropped from 1.7 to 0.9 per resident per year, and minor events from 17.5 to 9.3. [1]

The quality of the handoffs themselves also improved when researchers observed them directly. Verbal handoffs that included all five key elements rose from 20% to 66%, and written ones from 10% to 74%. Handoffs in which the receiver summed up what they heard rose from 31% to 83%. Improvement was similar in adult and pediatric care, and in community and academic hospitals. [1]

Why does standardizing the handoff help?

One likely explanation, offered here by the author rather than measured in the study, is that a standard method makes the key information come up every time and in the same order, so it does not depend on one person's memory or style. When the receiver sums up what they heard, misunderstandings surface before the other person leaves.

A 2025 systematic review from the Making Healthcare Safer IV project looked at ten years of evidence on 12 handoff tools used within hospital units. It found moderate-certainty evidence that I-PASS reduces medical errors and adverse events, based on ten studies. The older SBAR tool may improve safety, but with low certainty. The other tools had not been tested enough to judge. [2]

When is a standard handoff not enough?

In Australia, a randomized trial published in 2026 tested a nurse-led program on nine wards in three hospitals. The program combined three parts: a structured check of each patient at the start of the shift, a structured nurse-to-nurse handover at the bedside with the patient present, and nurses joining the doctors' ward rounds. Wards started the program one after another, on dates chosen at random. [3]

The trial included 13,753 admissions. Its primary outcome did not change. That outcome combined emergency team calls, unplanned ICU admissions, falls, and pressure injuries, which are skin wounds caused by long pressure on one spot. Falls and emergency calls did not change significantly either. But moderate and severe pressure injuries fell by 41%, an absolute difference of 0.8 percentage points, and nurses rated their wards' safety culture more positively. [3]

Another systematic review, also published in 2025, combined 42 studies on improving handoffs between hospital doctors. Only six were randomized trials. Adverse events improved in 3 of the 16 studies that measured them, medical errors in 3 of 7, and length of stay in 3 of 7. None of the four studies that measured deaths found an improvement. Positive results were more common when studies combined training with changes to how the work is done. [4]

What can leaders do?

These steps come from the findings of the four studies. Where a step is the author's inference, the text says so.

  1. Choose a tested handoff method instead of inventing a new one. The strongest evidence today is for I-PASS. The evidence for SBAR is weaker, and other tools have not been tested enough. [2]
  2. Ask the receiver to sum up what they heard, in their own words, before the other person leaves. It is the simplest way to catch a misunderstanding, and with training and follow-up it rose from 31% to 83% of handoffs. [1]
  3. Do not stop at training. Change how the work is done: a standard written template, a set time and place for the handoff, and someone who supports teams in the first months. [1][4]
  4. Measure handoff quality by direct observation: were all five elements covered, and did the receiver sum up? Do not stop at counting who attended training. [1]
  5. Track the outcomes that matter over enough time, and expect them to vary. Handoff practice can improve while the main outcome stays the same, as in the Australian trial. [3]
  6. Look for other handoffs in your organization: between shifts, between departments, and between operations and maintenance teams. This is the author's inference; these studies did not test it outside hospitals.

A tool alone is not enough. The study with the largest improvement came with coaching and monthly reviews over a year and a half. [1]

What we don't know

The 32-hospital study compared results before and after implementation, with no control group. Its adverse events were reported by the residents themselves in surveys, so changes in their willingness to report could affect the numbers. Some authors hold equity in a company that trains hospitals in I-PASS. They state that the study was designed before the company existed and that an independent statistical team, with no link to the company, ran the analysis. [1]

The Australian trial covered only three hospitals, and its primary outcome did not change. [3] Most studies in the two reviews took place in academic hospitals and involved doctors in training, and few were randomized trials. [2][4]

None of these studies included hospitals in the Arab region. Applying the idea to call centers, control rooms, or maintenance teams is a reasonable inference, but these studies did not test it.

The 10X lens

Every handoff between two people or two teams is a point where information can get lost, and more shifts mean more of these points. An organization that leaves handoffs to each employee's personal style is betting on their memory at the end of a long day. The real shift comes when the handoff becomes standard work that everyone knows and that is measured like any other process. The evidence is also a reminder that the tool does not work alone. What makes the difference is the training, follow-up, and measurement that come with it.

Key terms

Handoff (handover)
Passing information about a patient, and responsibility for them, from one person or team to another, for example at a shift change.
I-PASS
A standard handoff method with five steps: Illness severity, Patient summary, Action list, Situation awareness and contingency planning, and Synthesis by receiver.
SBAR
An older tool for structuring communication in four steps: Situation, Background, Assessment, and Recommendation.
Adverse event
Harm to a patient caused by their care, not by their illness.
Resident physician
A doctor who works in a hospital while training in a specialty.
Pressure injury
Damage to the skin and the tissue below it caused by long pressure on one spot, for example from staying in bed for a long time.

The question that remains

Three years from now, do you want safety in your organization to depend on the memory of the person handing over and the attention of the person taking over, or on one method that everyone knows and that is measured? If you want the second, which handoff between two teams or two shifts should you standardize first, and how will you know the results improved, not just the form?
