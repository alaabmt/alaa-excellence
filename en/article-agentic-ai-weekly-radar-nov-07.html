Who Owns Your AI Agents? Four Companies Announce Tools to Manage Them

Dr. Alaa Mohammad Ahmed

7 November 2026 · 5 min read

On November 4, 2026, four companies announced tools or funding for managing AI agents, and they say organizations struggle to know who owns each agent working in their accounts. What can a leader try this week?

Back to Agentic AI 10X

Figures are current as of November 7, 2026. The limits of the evidence are at the end of the article. [1]–[5]

Agents are at work in the organization. Who owns them?

Picture an organization where every team has added an AI agent that reads email, updates records, and sends requests. Months later the director asks: how many agents are running now? Who approved each one? What can each read and change? How do we stop one that goes wrong?

An AI agent is software driven by a large language model that plans and carries out steps with tools and permissions a person grants.

On November 4, 2026, four companies announced tools or funding aimed at exactly this question. SailPoint says 79% of organizations run agents in production and only about 2% use tools that give each agent an owner and defined permissions. [1] The table lists the four.

What did each company announce?

SailPoint records each agent in a map that links it to its human owner, credentials, entitlements, tools, and reachable data. Each agent gets a primary owner and a co-owner. Owners periodically confirm that the agent's permissions are still needed, and one action can turn an agent on, turn it off, or retire it. The new Protect features add a kill switch for agents running on employee devices; it is available in the top tier. [1]

Sysdig compares what an agent says it is doing with what a system-level sensor records. As the company puts it, the agent's logs tell you what it meant to do, and the sensor tells you what happened. The product can detect a risky action, hold it for a person, or block it before it completes. Its policies start in alert mode and later move to enforcement. [2][3]

Cloudflare added a Personal Assistants category to its analytics. A site owner can now choose, for each supported agent, to allow it, block it on pages with ads, or block it completely. In early access, its account protection tool shows which user each agent represents and how many accounts use it. [4]

Sentra raised $150 million in a third funding round led by Capital One Ventures, bringing its total to more than $260 million. It says its platform maps what people, AI agents, and service accounts (accounts that programs use, not people) can reach, enforces least privilege, and revokes access automatically. [5]

What can a leader try this week?

From these announcements we draw four steps a leader can start with:

  1. Ask for a list of the agents in one team: a named owner for each, and what each can read and change. Then count how many had no known owner before and after the exercise.
  2. Give agents read-only access by default. Allow writing, sending, or deleting only with a technical limit, a log, and human approval for steps that cannot be undone.
  3. Keep a monitoring record that is independent of the agent and its vendor. An agent's own log is the party under review testifying about itself, which is the idea behind Sysdig's comparison.
  4. Measure the pilot's result before expanding, instead of counting the tools you bought.

If you run a website or customer portal, first watch what traffic such agents send before you set a rule for each one.

The 10X Lens

Start with the controls that let you stop any agent: a register, a named owner for each, permission limits, an independent monitoring record, and a measured result before scaling. Adding agents faster than you can see and control them can add risk before it adds benefit. For more, see the Agentic AI 10X page.

Key Terms

AI Agent
Software that plans and carries out steps with tools and permissions a person grants.
Least Privilege
Giving an agent only the minimum access its task needs.
Runtime Monitoring
Watching what a program actually does while it runs, not only what its logs say.
Access Certification
A recurring review in which an owner confirms an agent's permissions are still needed.

The Question That Remains

If you were handed tomorrow a list of every agent working in your accounts and data, for how many could you name an owner? How many could you stop within a minute?

The limits of what we know

The four announcements come from companies that sell these solutions, or from press reports of them. We know of no independent evaluation of any product and no performance figures. SailPoint's 79% and 2% come from a report we could not open, and we found no description of its sample. SailPoint's demo environment is separate from the systems organizations actually run, and it published no detection rates or customer results.

Sysdig's page says early access begins with audit and deny controls, and it lists no price or general-availability date. Cloudflare says that knowing a visitor is an agent does not show that the person behind it is an existing customer; it names no supported agents and gives no volumes. Sentra's round shows investor interest, not that the product works, and its news reached us through a trade publication rather than the company's own release; no customer count has been published.

We left out other launches this week, such as Perplexity's Teammates, because we opened only secondary coverage of them. The four items show a market. We found no independent incident or study on this problem this week. [1]–[5]
