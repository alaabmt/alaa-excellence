Who Owns Your AI Agents? Four Launches on One Day Offer Answers

Dr. Alaa Mohammad Ahmed

7 November 2026 · 5 min read

SailPoint says 79% of organizations run AI agents in production, and only about 2% use identity tools built for them. Those are the vendor's own figures, not an independent measurement. On November 4, 2026, four companies announced tools or funding aimed at the same question: who owns each agent, what does it do, and who can stop it?

Back to Agentic AI 10X

Each key claim carries a label that names its source. Figures are current as of November 7, 2026. [1]–[5]

Why does this week matter to leaders?

An AI agent is software driven by a large language model that plans and carries out steps with tools and permissions a person grants. As agents multiply inside an organization, one simple question gets hard to answer: how many are running now, who owns each, what can each read and change, and how do we stop it?

SailPoint's figures come from a report we did not open, and we found no description of its sample. Company-reported [1] The vendor supplied the figure, so treat it as a sign of market interest, not a measurement. The same day, three other companies announced tools or funding for the same problem. What deserves a limited pilot?

SailPoint announced that Agentic Fabric is generally available. It records each agent in a map linking it to its human owner, credentials, entitlements, tools, and reachable data. Each agent gets a primary owner and a co-owner, runs through periodic certification campaigns, and can be activated, deactivated, or retired with one action. The new Protect features add a kill switch for agents running on employee devices. The kill switch is available only in the top tier. Company-reported [1]

SailPoint published no detection rates or customer results. Its demo environment is also separate from production. So a leader should start small: list the agents in one team, give each a named owner, and count how many were ownerless before and after.

Sysdig launched a product that compares what an agent says it is doing with what a system-level sensor records. As Sysdig puts it, the agent's logs tell you what it meant to do; the sensor tells you what happened. The product can detect a risky action, hold it for a person, or block it before it completes, and its policies start in alert mode before moving to enforcement. Press report of company announcement [2][3]

Sysdig's page says early access begins with audit and deny controls. It lists no price or general-availability date. Its supporting statistics come from its own reports with no stated method. The idea still works as a pilot: take one agent and compare its own record with what the system saw.

What is enough to watch?

On November 4, Cloudflare added a Personal Assistants category to its analytics, and a site owner can now choose, for each supported agent, to allow it, block it on pages with ads, or block it completely. In early access, its account-abuse tool shows which user each agent represents and how many accounts use it. Company-reported [4]

Cloudflare itself admits a limit: knowing that a visitor is an agent does not show that the person behind it is an existing customer, and closing that gap will take privacy-aware methods. The post names no supported agents and gives no volumes. A leader who runs a website or customer portal should first watch what traffic such agents send.

Sentra raised $150 million in a Series C led by Capital One Ventures, bringing its total to more than $260 million. It says its platform maps what people, AI agents, and service accounts can reach, enforces least privilege, and revokes access automatically. Press report of company announcement [5] The round shows where investors are looking; no customer count or result has been published to show the product works.

What to stop relying on

Do not accept an agent's own log as the only proof of what it did. That log is testimony from the party under review. Add a record that neither the agent nor its vendor controls. This is our reading of Sysdig's approach, not a source recommendation.

What should a leader decide this week?

These steps are our analysis; no source recommends them.

  1. Ask for a list of every agent running in the organization: who owns it, what it can read, and what it can change.
  2. Give agents read-only access by default. Allow writing, sending, or deleting only with a technical limit, a log and human approval for steps that cannot be undone.
  3. Keep a monitoring record that is independent of the agent and its supplier.
  4. Measure a pilot's result before expanding, instead of counting tools bought.

The limits of what we know

Everything here is announced by companies that sell these solutions, or reported from them. No independent evaluation covers any of the products, and none of the sources gives performance figures. SailPoint's 79% and 2% come from a report we did not open. The Sentra news reached us through a trade publication, not the company's own release, and neither Sysdig nor Cloudflare says what "early access" includes.

We left out other launches this week, such as Perplexity's Teammates, because we opened only secondary coverage of them. The four items show a market. We found no independent incident or study on this problem this week. [1]–[5]

The 10X Lens

Start with the controls that let you stop any agent: a register, a named owner for each, permission limits, an independent monitoring record, and a measured result before scaling. Adding agents faster than you can see and control them can add risk before it adds benefit. For more, see the Agentic AI 10X page.

Key Terms

AI Agent
Software that plans and carries out steps with tools and permissions a person grants.
Least Privilege
Giving an agent only the minimum access its task needs.
Runtime Monitoring
Watching what a program actually does while it runs, not only what its logs say.
Access Certification
A recurring review in which an owner confirms an agent's permissions are still needed.

The Question That Remains

If you were handed tomorrow a list of every agent working in your accounts and data, for how many could you name an owner? How many could you stop within a minute?
