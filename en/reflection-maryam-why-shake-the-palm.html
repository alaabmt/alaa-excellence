Maryam: Why Shake the Palm Tree?

Where human effort meets divine provision

The pain of childbirth had brought Maryam (Mary), peace be upon her, to the point of wishing she had died before that day and been forgotten. She had taken shelter by the trunk of a palm tree, alone with her pain and her fear of facing her people. At that moment of distress, a call reassured her and told her of the water and provision God had granted her.

Then came the command:

﴿وَهُزِّي إِلَيْكِ بِجِذْعِ النَّخْلَةِ تُسَاقِطْ عَلَيْكِ رُطَبًا جَنِيًّا﴾Meaning in English: “Shake the trunk of the palm tree towards you; it will drop fresh, ripe dates upon you.”Surah Maryam, 19:25

A woman exhausted by labour. A palm trunk she was told to shake. Fresh dates falling to her by God’s command.

God could have provided for her without her shaking the tree. Why, then, was she asked to do so?

The Qur’an does not give an exhaustive explanation for this command, and we should not claim certainty where the text does not. Yet the presence of both effort and provision in this scene invites reflection on how we use the means available to us, and on the limits of what we can control.

What the commentaries say

Al-Tabari explains shaking as moving the trunk and records reports about the tree’s condition. Ibn Kathir presents differing accounts: some describe a dry tree, others a fruit-bearing one. Those differences should keep us from treating disputed details as established facts. The verse itself is clear: Maryam was instructed to shake the trunk, and dates fell to her. [1] [2]

Al-Qurtubi draws on this verse to explain that seeking a livelihood and taking practical steps towards it are compatible with trust in God. Provision comes from God; pursuing the means to it is among the actions He asks of His servants. [3]

Within the story, the instruction to shake the tree is part of the care shown to Maryam. It follows the call telling her not to grieve and is followed by:

﴿فَكُلِي وَاشْرَبِي وَقَرِّي عَيْنًا﴾Meaning in English: “So eat, drink and be comforted.”Surah Maryam, 19:26

Al-Saadi’s commentary explains this attention to her need for food, drink and reassurance. [4]

These established meanings provide a starting point for a contemporary reflection on effort, ability and outcomes. They do not give us licence to make the verses say more than they do, or to attribute modern management concepts to them.

We can make the effort without controlling the outcome

We sometimes measure the value of work by the effort it consumes. If we have worked for a long time without seeing results, we assume that more effort must be the answer. Sometimes it is. Sometimes it is not.

A person may do everything within their power and still face circumstances beyond their control. Equally, good may come to them far beyond what their efforts could explain. Help may arrive unexpectedly; doors they thought closed may open.

None of this diminishes work or gives us a reason to abandon it. It may, however, release us from a heavy burden: the belief that we must guarantee every outcome, solve every problem and be capable of everything asked of us.

Maryam was asked to shake the palm tree. She was not asked to produce its fruit.

That distinction invites us to separate our responsibility to act from our control over results. We use the means available to us, without attributing every achievement to our own ability or holding ourselves responsible for what lies beyond it.

When the call to act becomes a burden

Encouraging work and perseverance can be helpful. It can also become a burden when we overlook the condition of the person we are urging to try harder.

We tell the exhausted employee to make more effort. We tell someone in difficult circumstances not to give up. We tell a person facing obstacles they cannot remove to try again.

What they need first may be help that lightens the load, a tool that makes the task possible, or time to recover their strength.

In reflecting on Maryam’s story, we should not focus so narrowly on the command to shake the tree that we lose sight of the mercy surrounding it. She was told not to grieve, given news of water, provided with dates, and told to eat, drink and be comforted.

The instruction to shake the trunk cannot reasonably become a pretext for demanding that every vulnerable person overcome their difficulties alone. The care in this scene extends beyond an instruction to exert effort.

We can draw a practical lesson for our own lives: help people act, take their needs and limits seriously, and consider the means available to them before demanding results.

A 10X reflection: more effort, or better conditions for it?

Organisations often respond to challenges by asking people to do more. A project falls behind, so working hours increase. Results weaken, so monitoring intensifies. Ambitions grow, so teams are asked to launch more initiatives.

But more effort cannot solve every problem.

A team may already be working at full capacity while cumbersome procedures, outdated tools or decisions beyond its authority prevent progress. We may ask it to achieve a substantial breakthrough while leaving it with the same constraints that have held it back.

We therefore need to examine what we have made possible for people, alongside what they have contributed.

What is obstructing their work? What do they need? Could we remove a burden that drains their energy, or provide something that makes their effort more productive?

These are questions from contemporary management, not an interpretation of Maryam’s story. Reflection on effort and provision can nevertheless remind us to consider the conditions surrounding work as well as the amount of effort it receives.

Leaders who aspire to excellence do more than ask people to deliver more. They work to create conditions in which people can do their work well and develop their abilities without being depleted.

The change we seek may begin with removing an old obstacle, reconsidering an established practice, or giving capable people the authority to do work that the system currently prevents them from doing.

Longer hours are not always what we need. We may need to choose our work more carefully and provide the means to do it well.

When the results do not come

There is another part of this reflection that we should not overlook.

Taking the right practical steps does not guarantee the outcome we want. Nor does everyone who works hard see their effort bear fruit as they hoped. We may do what we can and still find that results are delayed or different from what we wanted.

Maryam’s story does not mean that every small action will produce a great result, or that help will always arrive in the form we expect. What she received was God’s grace and particular care for her. We should not turn this scene into an unconditional promise about every human endeavour.

Recognising that outcomes are not entirely in our hands can change how we understand effort. We act because action is within our reach. We use the available means responsibly because we are called to do so. Then we review our decisions and learn from what we missed, without making success or failure the sole measure of the value of our work.

Using the means available to us wisely may include changing a path that no longer works, seeking someone with greater ability, or stopping when continuing would do more harm than good.

Wisdom therefore involves more than working hard. It includes knowing what to do, when to do it, and where we should seek help or leave a task to someone else.

The 10X excellence question

Which trunk are you asking others to shake?

When you ask your team to deliver a new result, first consider the means at its disposal. Have you provided the tools, authority and time it needs? Or are you demanding an outcome without making it possible to reach?

Perhaps the most useful question to take into tomorrow is: What obstacle can I remove today to make other people’s effort more effective?

A final reflection

To reflect on Maryam beside the palm tree is to consider the whole scene: the pain of childbirth, the call that dispels grief, the water, the dates, the instruction to shake the trunk, and the reassurance in the words: “So eat, drink and be comforted.”

Here are effort and provision, human vulnerability and divine care.

What we take into our own lives may be a commitment to do what we can, use the means available without pride in our own ability, and help others when their circumstances leave them little room to act.

Trust in God includes acting within our capacity. Wisdom includes recognising what lies beyond it. And mercy includes helping others with what they cannot manage alone.
