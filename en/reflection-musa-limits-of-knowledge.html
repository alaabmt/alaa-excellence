Musa: What Do You Do When What You Know Is Not Enough?

Knowledge, Judgment, and the Capacity to Learn

العربية

Methodological note: This is a contemporary reflection inspired by the Qur’anic narrative and informed by meanings established in classical tafsir. It does not propose a new tafsir or attribute contemporary management concepts to the Qur’anic text.

“Moses said to him, ‘May I follow you on [the condition] that you teach me from what you have been taught of sound judgement?’”

Al-Kahf 18:66 — Saheeh International

There is a particular difficulty that comes with experience.

At the beginning of a career, we expect not to know. We ask questions, seek advice, and assume there is more to learn. Years later, knowledge accumulates, patterns become familiar, and people begin asking us for answers.

But experience can create a new blind spot: At what point does knowing a great deal make it harder to notice what we do not know?

That question makes Musa’s request remarkable. He does not arrive as a man without knowledge. Yet when he encounters knowledge he does not possess, his response is not to defend what he already knows. He asks to learn.

Perhaps expertise reaches one of its highest forms not when we have accumulated answers, but when we remain capable of becoming students again.

The Boundary We Cannot See

“And how can you have patience for what you do not encompass in knowledge?”

Al-Kahf 18:68 — Saheeh International

The challenge is not simply ignorance. It is incomplete perspective.

You can see something clearly and still not see all of it. You can possess accurate information and still lack the context that gives that information meaning. You can even reach a reasonable conclusion and later discover one fact that changes your understanding of the whole situation.

Think of how often we say: “That makes no sense.”

Sometimes it does not. But sometimes what we really mean is: “That does not make sense within what I currently know.”

This does not require us to abandon judgment or accept every unfamiliar claim. It asks something more demanding: Can I distinguish between what is wrong and what I do not yet understand?

The Story Does Not Leave Us With “I Don’t Know”

Recognising the limits of knowledge is not the conclusion. It is the beginning of a response.

Musa seeks the person who possesses knowledge he does not. He follows. He learns. And during that journey, he is asked to live for a time with events whose meaning has not yet been disclosed.

“Then if you follow me, do not ask me about anything until I make to you about it mention [i.e., explanation].”

Al-Kahf 18:70 — Saheeh International

The instruction belongs to the particular conditions of this Qur’anic encounter. It is not a general instruction to silence questions or suspend critical thought. But it opens a valuable space for reflection: Must every judgment be made at the first moment we encounter something we do not understand?

There are times when immediate action is necessary. There are also times when wisdom requires another discipline: wait until you know more.

“I will inform you of the interpretation of that about which you could not have patience.”

Al-Kahf 18:78 — Saheeh International

What had appeared one way begins to look different when previously unavailable knowledge enters the picture.

Recognise the boundary of your knowledge → seek knowledge beyond it → learn → resist premature conclusions → understand more fully.

The answer to “I don’t know” is therefore not merely humility. It is movement.

What Does Your Organization Not Know?

Imagine an executive team with sophisticated dashboards, strong performance, specialists, strategies, systems, and years of institutional knowledge.

Ask: What do we know? The organization can probably produce an impressive answer.

Now ask: What are we not seeing?

What does a frontline employee know that never reaches senior leadership? What change in customer behaviour is still too small to appear in the dashboard? What assumption remains embedded in the strategy because it was true for so long that nobody thinks to question it? What technology appears peripheral because we are evaluating it through the logic of our current business?

And perhaps the most uncomfortable question: Who knows something important that we do not—and would we listen if they told us?

An organization can possess enormous amounts of information and still be poor at discovering what lies outside its established field of view. That is not merely a knowledge problem. It is a learning-capacity problem.

When the Future Requires Knowledge We Do Not Yet Possess

This is where the reflection meets Tamayuz 10X.

Not because the Qur’anic narrative is teaching a modern theory of organizational transformation. It is not, and management terminology should not be projected back onto the Qur’anic text.

The connection emerges from a contemporary question the scene helps us examine:

Are the knowledge and capabilities that made us successful today sufficient for the future we are trying to create?

Organizations often approach the future by extending the present: improve today’s process, digitize today’s service, train people to perform today’s roles better, optimize today’s operating model.

All of those may be worthwhile. But a substantially different future may require knowledge the organization does not yet possess, capabilities it has never needed before, signals it does not currently measure, and questions its existing expertise has never had reason to ask.

This is why foresight and capability building belong together. It is not enough to ask: What might the future look like?

We must also ask: If that future arrives, what would we need to know—and be able to do—that we cannot know or do today?

A Different Response to Not Knowing

When you reach the edge of your knowledge: recognize the edge; move toward knowledge; become a learner again; know when judgment needs more context; and turn new knowledge into new capability.

Then ask yourself: Who can tell me today that there is something I am not seeing? Would I listen?

For a leader: Where does valuable knowledge exist in your organization without reaching the place where decisions are made?

And for anyone thinking seriously about the future: What must you begin learning today to become capable of something you cannot yet do?

The Question Remains

“May I follow you… that you teach me…”

Al-Kahf 18:66 — Saheeh International

Perhaps the real danger is not that there will always be things beyond our knowledge. There will. The greater danger is reaching the boundary of what we know without realizing that we have reached it.

A new capability may begin before the training, technology, restructuring, or strategy. It may begin with the intellectual honesty to recognise: There is something here I do not yet understand.

And then with the willingness to do something about it: seek, learn, wait for sufficient understanding, reconsider—and build a capability that did not exist before.

The future may not be beyond our reach because we lack ambition. We may simply be trying to reach it with only the knowledge that brought us this far.

Methodological Note

This article offers a contemporary reflection inspired by the Qur’anic narrative and informed by meanings established in classical tafsir. It is not intended to propose a new tafsir of the verses or to attribute contemporary management concepts to the Qur’anic text.

References to foresight, organizational learning, knowledge gaps, and capability building are contemporary analytical lenses used after engaging with the Qur’anic scene; they are not presented as meanings of the Qur’anic terminology itself.
