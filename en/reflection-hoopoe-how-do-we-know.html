The Hoopoe: How Do We Know What We Don’t Know?

From Distributed Knowledge to Organizational Intelligence

العربية

Methodological note: These are contemporary reflections inspired by the guidance of the Qur’anic narrative in light of meanings established by recognized works of tafsir. They are not intended to offer a new interpretation of the verses or to attribute modern management terminology to the Qur’anic text.

“I have encompassed in knowledge that which you have not encompassed, and I have come to you from Sheba with certain news.”

An-Naml 27:22 — Saheeh International

There is a remarkable statement in the story of Prophet Sulayman and the hoopoe: “I have encompassed in knowledge that which you have not encompassed.”

The hoopoe says this to Sulayman, even though Allah had granted him extraordinary knowledge: “And We had certainly given to David and Solomon knowledge.” Yet the hoopoe returned with knowledge that Sulayman did not have.

So perhaps the deeper question is not simply: What did the hoopoe know? But: How did it come to know what was not already known?

It was absent, then returned with knowledge

“And he took attendance of the birds and said, ‘Why do I not see the hoopoe — or is he among the absent?’”

An-Naml 27:20 — Saheeh International

The Qur’an does not tell us the details of its journey or exactly how it gathered this knowledge. We should therefore resist turning the episode into a modern process the text itself does not describe. What we can observe is simpler: the hoopoe encountered a reality that was not present before Sulayman, and returned with knowledge of it.

Can we discover what we do not know if we always look from the same place?

It returned with more than a fragment

“Indeed, I found a woman ruling them, and she has been given of all things, and she has a great throne. I found her and her people prostrating to the sun instead of Allah…”

An-Naml 27:23–24 — Saheeh International

The report contained connected observations: a ruler, a people, resources and power, a great throne, and the religious condition of the society. This opens an important distinction between data, knowledge, and intelligence.

An organization may possess enormous amounts of data without understanding what they mean. Its people may know important things that never reach the place where those insights can influence decisions.

How much does your organization actually know of what its people already know?

A frontline employee may see something senior leadership does not. A customer may detect a change before it appears in performance indicators. The problem is not always a lack of information; sometimes it is our inability to access and connect knowledge that already exists.

New knowledge still requires verification

The hoopoe described its report as “certain news.” Yet Sulayman did not simply accept the claim:

“[Solomon] said, ‘We will see whether you were truthful or were of the liars.’”

An-Naml 27:27 — Saheeh International

He did not dismiss the information because of its source, but neither did he accept it without verification. Listen, then verify. That distinction is fundamental to building trusted intelligence.

The Tamayuz 10X Lens

The story is not a Qur’anic explanation of the modern concept of organizational intelligence. But the scene raises a question that naturally intersects with the Tamayuz 10X pillar of Maximizing Organizational and Technological Intelligence:

How can distributed knowledge become an organizational capability for understanding and decision-making?

An organization may collectively know far more than any single person within it. But that advantage disappears when knowledge remains fragmented across employees, documents, systems, customers, and datasets.

Discover → Share → Connect → Verify → Understand → Decide → Learn.

This is a modern organizational lens, not an interpretation of the Qur’anic verses.

AI makes the question even more important

Organizations today possess enormous bodies of knowledge. The challenge is increasingly shifting from “Where can we find information?” to: How do we know what we already know?

AI can help search organizational knowledge, connect sources, retrieve experience, and identify patterns. But the greater our ability to generate and retrieve information, the more important Sulayman’s response becomes: “We will see whether you were truthful.” AI does not eliminate verification; it may make verification even more important.

Can the “hoopoe” speak in your organization?

Can someone far from the center of authority say, “There is something important you do not know”?

An organization can invest in sophisticated technology and still remain weak in intelligence if its culture prevents knowledge from traveling from the edges to the center.

What don’t we know? Who might know it? How can their knowledge reach us? How will we verify it?

The Question the Hoopoe Leaves Us With

Perhaps the most important question is not: What do you know? It is: How will you discover what you do not know?

Between “I have encompassed in knowledge that which you have not encompassed” and Sulayman’s “We will see whether you were truthful” lies a powerful sequence: Discover knowledge. Allow it to reach the decision. Then verify it before acting on it.

Perhaps the most intelligent organization is not the one with the most information. It is the one where important knowledge is not lost simply because it existed somewhere no one thought to look.
