10X Case · Government and Public Policy

Why Finland Made a Home the First Step for Homeless People, Not the Last

For years, many homeless people in Finland moved between shelters and hostels, waiting to show they were ready for a home of their own. In 2008, the state reversed that order: housing became the first form of help, not the final reward. Long-term homelessness fell by about 70% by 2023. Then it began to rise again as rents went up and support was cut.

Executive Summary

In 1985, close to 20,000 people in Finland had no home, most of them single men. [6] The numbers fell after the government made ending homelessness a goal in 1987, but one group stayed stuck: people who had been homeless for years and were also dealing with addiction, mental illness or debt. Services expected them to complete treatment, stay sober or work their way up from shelters through temporary housing before they could have a flat. Many fell off at some step and had to start again. [5]

This mattered because these people faced the deepest exclusion, and treatment and support rarely worked while they had nowhere stable to live. [5]

On 14 February 2008, the government launched a national program to reduce long-term homelessness, known as PAAVO. It was built on the Housing First principle: give a person a permanent home with an ordinary lease and no preconditions, then bring support to that home. [5][9] The state signed letters of intent with the ten cities that had the most homeless people, and shelters with shared dormitories were converted into self-contained flats. [9]

Between 2008 and 2015, about 2,500 homes were built or bought and about 350 housing support workers were hired. [9] Long-term homelessness fell by about 70% between 2008 and 2023, according to municipal data. [3] Then the trend reversed. Homelessness rose in 2024 for the first time since 2012 and climbed another 20% in 2025. Municipalities blame social security changes, higher rents and living costs, and unemployment. [1][2]

The 10X lesson is that Finland did not build a better shelter. It stopped making the shelter a required stop and changed the assumption the whole service rested on. But the shift depends on affordable homes and steady prevention, and it slips when either weakens.

A Home at the Top of the Staircase

The old approach was known as the staircase model. A person started in a shelter, moved to temporary housing and then to supported housing, and reached a home of their own only after proving at each step that they were committed to treatment or sobriety. The Y-Foundation (Y-Säätiö), one of Finland’s largest non-profit landlords for people who have been homeless, says moving between steps often failed, leaving people stuck in temporary housing for years. [5]

A report called Name on the Door changed the thinking. It treated long-term homelessness as a structural problem rather than a personal failure and concluded that services do not work without permanent housing. A home became the starting point, not the end goal. [5]

The 10X Move

The state used to offer a home as a reward for recovery. It began offering it as a condition for recovery. A person now receives a flat with an ordinary lease that protects them like any other tenant, support comes to their door in whatever amount they need, and keeping the home does not depend on accepting treatment. The question stopped being “Is this person ready for a home?” and became “What support does this person need to stay in their home?” [5][9]

From Report to City Streets

  1. In 1987, the government set a goal of ending homelessness within five years and asked the Y-Foundation, founded two years earlier, to supply 200 homes a year. By the end of 1991, it had secured 1,470. [6]
  2. In 2008, the government launched PAAVO I in ten cities, aiming to halve long-term homelessness by 2011. [9]
  3. By the end of 2011, 1,519 homes and supported housing units had been completed, and long-term homelessness had fallen by 28%, short of the target. [9]
  4. From 2012 to 2015, PAAVO II closed temporary hostels, allocated more than 3,000 flats to homeless people and expanded scattered housing in ordinary apartment buildings. [4][9]
  5. From 2016 to 2019, the AUNE program shifted the focus to prevention and to stopping people from becoming homeless again. [4][10]
  6. In late 2023, the Ministry of the Environment launched a program to end long-term homelessness by 2027. [8]
  7. In 2024, homelessness rose to 3,806 after 11 years of decline. In 2025, it reached 4,579. [1][2]

Who Paid, Who Owned the Homes, and Who Provided Support?

The Ministry of the Environment ran the program. The Housing Finance and Development Centre of Finland (ARA) financed the building and purchase of homes and allocated them to homeless people, and Finland’s Slot Machine Association, which funds social work from its revenue, shared the cost. [4][9] It was the first time the state also helped pay for support staff, not just buildings. [9]

Cities handled planning, land and social housing, and charities ran many of the units. The clearest example is Alppikatu in Helsinki. It had been an emergency shelter since 1937 and once held more than 500 places in shared dormitories. It became 80 flats with permanent tenancies, run by the Salvation Army. [9] The Y-Foundation bought scattered flats in ordinary buildings, so people who had been homeless became neighbors of other tenants. [9]

Residents choose what support to accept, and services deal with substance use through harm reduction rather than abstinence rules. [9] The state also expanded housing advice, which helps tenants who fall behind on rent or debt before they are evicted. Government officials estimated that it kept about 200 people a year from losing their homes. [9]

The Evidence and Its Limits

The figures come from an annual survey of municipalities, run since 1986, that records the situation on 15 November each year. [3] A 2015 international review, commissioned by the Ministry of the Environment and ARA and led by Nicholas Pleace of the University of York with researchers from the United States, Sweden and Finland, concluded that the program had achieved its main goal of a lasting national reduction in long-term homelessness. It called Finland one of the best examples in the world of Housing First reducing long-term homelessness. [9] Researchers at the University of Turku used national registers from 2000 to 2017 and found that, among homeless people treated for substance use, the share with permanent housing two years later rose during the program years. [10]

The survey is not official statistics. Participation is voluntary, only 72% of municipalities responded in 2023, and Helsinki changed its counting method in 2018, so its figures are not fully comparable with earlier years. The report itself warns that long-term homelessness figures are estimates and the true number may be higher. [3] Finland’s definition also includes people staying temporarily with friends or relatives, who made up 64% of the total in 2025. Comparisons with countries that count only people sleeping rough or in shelters are therefore misleading. [1]

Data on whether residents keep their homes are not collected in a uniform way, although service providers describe the rates as very high. [9] The international review found that the European evidence on cost savings is not yet strong. [9] The Turku study notes that housing outcomes had begun improving before the programs, so the gains cannot be attributed to them alone, and it found no clear change in deaths in this group. [10]

The 10X Lens

Redefining value

A home stopped being the reward for recovery and became its foundation. Everything else was built on that change of assumption. [5]

Leadership and future-oriented governance

The programs continued across successive governments from 2008, and letters of intent tied each city to specific targets and funding. [9][10]

Reinventing the operating model

Shelters became flats with leases, the right to housing was separated from accepting treatment, and support moved into the resident’s home. [9]

Sustained value and results

Long-term homelessness fell for many years. The rise in 2024 and 2025 shows that keeping those gains takes affordable homes and continuous prevention. [1][3]

From Desired Future to Impact

The desired future was a country where no one stays homeless for long. [8] Getting there required small, affordable flats, workers who bring support into people’s homes, and a partnership that keeps the state, cities and charities working together. Finland built the homes, hired the staff and turned shelters into flats, so that people moved into a home first and support was built around them. [9] The result was a steep fall in long-term homelessness through 2023, followed by a rise that shows the gains do not last without prevention and affordable housing. [1][3]

Insider Critique and Evidence Limits

If the people who led PAAVO read this case, they would likely object to reducing it to “give people a home.” The international review notes that the Finnish model relies heavily on supported communal units as well as scattered flats, and that Housing First is not right for everyone, so housing built around sobriety should stay available for those who want it. [9]

They might also point out that the first program missed its target. Long-term homelessness fell 28%, not 50%, and the government’s foreword to the review said the main goal had not been reached. [9] The program learned and changed course, moving from building units, to scattered housing, to prevention.

Critics inside the sector warn that the gains can erode. In 2025, the Y-Foundation’s chief executive said funding for housing advice had been halved and that cuts to housing benefits were pushing tenants out of their homes. [7] That is the view of an organization with a stake in the issue, but municipalities gave similar reasons in the official survey. [1]

What can be said with confidence is that long-term homelessness in Finland fell sharply after 2008, and that this was associated with national programs that applied Housing First. What cannot be said with confidence is how much of the fall came from the programs rather than the wider economy and housing market, or how much money was saved.

Practical Use

Look for a service in your organization that makes people prove they deserve help before they get what they need to succeed. Then ask whether you could provide the foundation first and build support around it. If you do, plan from the start for what will protect the change from sliding back: the resources it depends on, the prevention that limits new cases, and the indicator that warns you before the trend turns.

Transferability

Finland had conditions that made this easier, including about 400,000 state-subsidized rental flats, a broad social security system and a small population. [5] A country short of affordable housing will find that Housing First runs into a shortage of homes. Still, the idea travels to many sectors, such as health, education and employment, wherever people are asked to become stable before they receive what would help them become stable.

The 10X Question

Ten years from now, what would a service look like if no one got stuck waiting to prove they deserved help? What would we give a person on their first day with us? And if that is what we want, which resources, partnerships and skills must we start building now so the gains do not reverse when the economy or the budget changes?
