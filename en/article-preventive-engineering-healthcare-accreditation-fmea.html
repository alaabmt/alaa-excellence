From Non-Compliance to Prevention: FMEA for Sustaining Hospital Accreditation

Accreditation is not sustained by closing findings before the next survey. A three-hospital Delhi NCR study used 289 recorded non-conformities, Pareto analysis, FMEA and root cause analysis to turn audit findings into a risk-prioritization model. The useful part is not only the ranking — it is what the study teaches us about converting compliance data into a preventive system.

What was the study trying to do?

The study focused on non-accredited hospitals in Delhi NCR — National Capital Region and asked what prevents organizations from achieving and sustaining NABH — National Accreditation Board for Hospitals & Healthcare Providers accreditation.

The authors analysed 289 recorded non-conformities (NCs) from three hospitals over two years. Their mixed-methods design combined document review with semi-structured interviews involving nine quality and nursing professionals: three Quality Managers, three Chief Nursing Officers and three Senior Quality Coordinators. Pareto analysis identified recurring areas, FMEA prioritized five critical cases, and RCA — Root Cause Analysis with Ishikawa diagrams was used to explore systemic causes. [1]

Evidence boundary: this is not a fully prospective healthcare FMEA that starts with a process map and asks what could fail before an event occurs. The study begins with non-conformities that already happened, then applies FMEA-style prioritization to them. That makes it useful for transforming audit data into risk priorities, but different from a complete prospective Healthcare FMEA.

The first step was not RPN: it was understanding where non-conformities accumulated

Pareto Analysis ranks problems by frequency or contribution so a team can see where the largest concentration sits. The study reports COP — Care of Patients as the largest non-conformity category, followed by AAC — Access, Assessment and Continuity of Care, then HIC — Hospital Infection Control.

Look at the table below. These numbers describe frequency of non-conformity by accreditation chapter. Frequency is not the same thing as risk severity.

The paper later states that roughly 80% of non-conformities arise from about 20% of categories and links that statement to the first three bars, but the published chart and cumulative percentages do not support 80% for those three categories. That internal inconsistency should remain visible rather than being repeated as fact.

From frequency to risk: five cases prioritized with FMEA

The study then assigns values for S — Severity, O — Occurrence and D — Detection, and calculates RPN — Risk Priority Number as S × O × D. Severity reflects consequence, Occurrence reflects estimated frequency, and Detection reflects how difficult the issue is to detect with current controls before the effect propagates.

However, the paper does not publish detailed rating scales for S, O and D. We can therefore reproduce the multiplication, but not independently reproduce why a specific case deserved a 7 rather than a 6.

Read the table from top to bottom. It is sorted by the published RPN, not by how frequently the accreditation chapter appeared in the Pareto chart.

The important reading: the highest published RPN is 336 for accessibility, followed by 294 for incomplete medication orders and 240 for transfer summaries. But some items under “Current Process Controls” are written like future interventions — for example “update the template” or “install ramps.” In a rigorous FMEA, an existing control, a proposed action and evidence of effectiveness should be recorded separately.

Root cause was broader than “staff non-compliance”

The study uses Ishikawa / Fishbone diagrams to organize systemic causes into six categories: Equipment, Process, People, Material, Environment and Management.

Published examples include inconsistent patient identifiers, delayed investigations, weak corrective/preventive-action documentation, missing discharge or transfer information, gaps in follow-up, cross-contamination risks, missing safety signage, insufficient training and weak policy enforcement. [1]

Read the analytical chain below. Each tool answers a different question.

Non-conformity data→Pareto screening→S/O/D risk determination→RPN→Root cause analysis→System action→Effectiveness verification

Why this matters: frequency alone does not establish risk, RPN does not explain cause, and RCA alone does not tell an organization where to begin. The tools are complementary when their roles remain distinct.

The lesson: using the name FMEA is not enough. The quality of the analysis depends on clear functions, failure modes, effects, causes, real current controls, explicit rating criteria and evidence that actions worked.

The nine-stage Preventive Engineering journey: follow incomplete medication strength from finding to prevention

Continuous case: we will follow “medication orders lacking strength,” the second-highest published RPN at 294. It is a useful clinical example because it shows how a documentation non-conformity can become a patient-safety risk. The published S=7, O=6 and D=7 are used as source values; the paper does not publish the scoring rubric behind them, so they should not be transplanted into another hospital.
1

Define the scope: which part of the medication journey?

What are we doing here? We define a start and end point instead of analysing “medication management” as one huge topic.

In this case: the scope covers medication-order creation and documentation through verification/dispensing handoff, with one question: does the order contain enough information to identify the intended medication strength without unsafe assumption?

Common mistake: treating all medication errors as one failure mode despite very different prescribing, dispensing, administration and monitoring risks.

Stage output: a bounded ordering process and a clear information-completeness requirement.
2

Required function: what must the medication order accomplish?

What are we doing here? We define the job before naming the failure.

In this case: the order must communicate the necessary medication information completely and unambiguously so the next person in the chain does not have to guess.

Common mistake: writing “medication error” as the failure mode without stating which required function failed.

Stage output: a complete, unambiguous and verifiable medication order.
3

Failure mode, effect and potential cause

What are we doing here? We separate the failure itself from its consequence and why it can occur.

In the published case: the failure mode is inconsistent documentation or an incomplete order lacking medication strength. The potential effect is an adverse drug event or patient harm. The listed cause is ambiguous or incomplete ordering that can lead to incorrect assumptions.

Common mistake: writing “patient harm” as the failure mode. It is the consequence, not the way the order failed.

Stage output: incomplete order → unsafe interpretation → possible medication-related harm.
4

Current prevention and detection: what actually exists?

What are we doing here? We document the current system before designing the solution.

In the paper: “update medication-order templates to require medication strength” appears under Current Process Controls, but its wording sounds like a proposed change. In a real hospital we would verify whether a mandatory field already exists, whether incomplete orders can be signed, and whether an independent verification step catches them before administration.

Common mistake: putting the future solution into the current-control column and then assuming Detection has already improved.

Stage output: a clean separation between existing barrier, proposed action and evidence of barrier performance.
5

Risk determination with S/O/D: how does the case reach RPN=294?

What are we doing here? We use the published values but keep the evidence limit visible.

In this case: S=7, O=6 and D=7, therefore 7 × 6 × 7 = 294. Because the paper does not publish the rating scales in detail, another hospital should not copy those numbers.

Common mistake: carrying 294 into a different organization without local evidence and a local scoring scale.

Stage output: a published priority value whose arithmetic is reproducible, while the rating rationale is not fully reproducible from the paper alone.
6

Priority: what does an RPN of 294 actually mean?

What are we doing here? We use the number to open a decision, not end the discussion.

In the paper: this is the second-highest RPN among the five selected cases. In practice the team should also consider drug type, patient vulnerability, high-alert medication categories and how reliably the system prevents execution of an incomplete order.

Common mistake: prioritizing by product alone without reading severity and the real barriers.

Stage output: a justified decision to address medication-order completeness early.
7

Design treatment: what did the paper propose?

What are we doing here? We convert risk into a system change.

The paper proposes: update medication-order templates so medication strength is mandatory and train prescribers on the importance of specifying it.

Educational extension: depending on the hospital's technology, this could be tested through required electronic fields, validation rules or redesigned paper forms and independent review. These are examples of implementation design, not interventions reported as completed by the study.

Common mistake: choosing training as the only action when the process or form design still allows the same failure.

Stage output: a design barrier that reduces opportunity for omission, supported by training rather than replaced by training.
8

Verify effectiveness: how do we know risk actually fell?

What are we doing here? We separate implementation from effectiveness.

In practice: track incomplete medication orders per 1,000 orders, clarification requests, orders intercepted before execution and relevant medication-harm events. After a sufficient follow-up period, reassess O and D using local evidence.

Common mistake: lowering O or D immediately after adding a field to a form.

Stage output: before/after evidence that shows whether the failure became less frequent and easier to catch.
9

Review continuously: when should the analysis reopen?

What are we doing here? We connect the analysis to clinical and technical change, not only to accreditation survey dates.

Review triggers: EHR changes, prescription-form changes, new formulations, medication incidents or near misses, changes to verbal-order policy, or rising clarification rates.

Common mistake: reopening FMEA only when an external surveyor is coming.

Stage output: FMEA becomes part of daily learning rather than an accreditation-season document.

From accreditation compliance to patient safety

The paper recommends leadership commitment, structured training, competency checks, staff participation, gap analysis, PDCA cycles, cross-department audits, centralized electronic documentation and benchmarking against accredited hospitals. [1]

The stronger use of those ideas is not “how do we pass the next survey?” It is: how do we turn accreditation requirements into everyday operating barriers that prevent harm and surface deviation early?

Compare the two mindsets below.

From closing a finding to managing risk
Audit mindsetPreventive Engineering mindset
Finding: incomplete medication orderFunction: produce a complete, unambiguous order every time

The point: accreditation can be a trigger, but it should not be the reason the system remains safe. A safe system performs correctly even when no surveyor is in the building.

How can a hospital start a practical workshop from this study?

Audit data are a powerful input, but the workshop should not stop at re-ranking old findings. Start with one defined process, combine frontline clinical experts with quality professionals, and move from the recorded event back to required function, failure mode and current controls.

Use the table below as a small pilot.

What changes compared with the study? We retain the value of audit evidence, then add prospective process mapping, a multidisciplinary team, explicit rating scales, a clean distinction between current controls and future actions, and post-action verification.

Limits that should remain visible

The sample is small and purposive: three non-accredited hospitals. S/O/D scoring involved nine quality and nursing professionals, and the paper does not publish the scoring rubrics in detail. The analysis begins with recorded non-conformities rather than a full prospective process analysis. No measured post-intervention before/after results demonstrate lower RPN or improved patient outcomes. The paper also contains a Pareto inconsistency and a mismatch between the five cases in the priority table and the three failure examples highlighted in the conclusion. Those issues do not eliminate the study's value; they define what can be transferred with confidence and what needs redesign.

What should an FMEA expert learn from this paper?

The strongest lesson is not “accreditation needs more training.” The stronger lesson is that audit findings can become a learning system when each finding is connected to a required function, failure mode, effect, cause, barrier and effectiveness measure.

The second lesson is that frequency is not risk. Pareto tells you where problems recur; FMEA tries to tell you where consequence may be higher or detection weaker. The third lesson is that RPN is not the end of the work; it is the start of a decision that should end with action and evidence of effectiveness.

The Preventive Engineering question

If you take the last ten accreditation non-conformities in your hospital, can you connect each one to a required function, a failure mode, an effect, a cause, a current barrier and evidence of effectiveness — or do most of them end with “staff were trained” and the finding was closed?
