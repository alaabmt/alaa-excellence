When Hospital Care Moves Into the Patient's Home: West Hertfordshire

How West Hertfordshire's hospitals reorganized acute care, and shortened the time patients spend on a hospital ward.

Executive Summary

Some patients stay on a hospital ward not because every part of their care needs a hospital bed, but because the treatment and monitoring they still need are organized around one. Beds stay occupied, and patients spend days away from home. West Hertfordshire Teaching Hospitals NHS Trust, working with community health teams from Central London Community Healthcare NHS Trust, asked a different question: could some patients with acute illness finish, or even start, their treatment at home, with tests, monitoring and medical intervention still available when needed?[1][2]

The answer was not a box of monitoring devices. It took joint working between the hospital and community teams, clear responsibility for each patient's follow-up, and agreed steps for bringing a patient back to hospital if their condition worsened.

A peer-reviewed study published in April 2026 found that, among patients whose home care began after a hospital admission, hospital stays were an estimated 3.13 days shorter than for statistically matched patients who received usual care. The researchers estimated that the program saved 13,119 bed days and produced a net financial benefit of £3.79 million over 33 months. These are estimates for the study's patients and the trust's own costs, not results that any hospital can expect to repeat.[1]

The 10X point is the assumption the case challenges: that acute care has to take place in a hospital bed. When care, oversight and escalation can follow the patient home, the place of treatment becomes a clinical decision rather than a default.

The Challenge: When Is a Hospital Stay Necessary?

Some patients need to stay in hospital because their treatment or monitoring cannot safely be provided anywhere else. Others become stable enough to finish treatment at home, provided that clinical follow-up, tests and a fast response are available. The challenge was to identify those patients, not to move as many people out of hospital as possible.

The service covered patients with heart failure, airway disease and acute respiratory infection. Heart failure is a condition in which the heart does not pump blood as well as it should; fluid can build up and cause breathlessness, and patients may need their medicines adjusted and their symptoms and weight monitored. Some patients with respiratory illness need their breathing, oxygen levels and response to treatment watched closely. Home care is not suitable for every patient with these conditions, and the decision to accept a patient remains a clinical one.[1]

The Decisive Shift: Care Continues After the Patient Leaves Hospital

The partnership built a hospital at home (HAH) service: hospital-level care for suitable acute conditions, delivered in the patient's home while clinical responsibility stays with the specialist team. It is also called a virtual ward, but the name does not mean care by video call alone. The service can include home visits, tests and hands-on treatment.

The service ran three pathways. Admission avoidance treats patients at home from the start, when that can be done safely. A second pathway moves an eligible patient from the emergency department or a ward to home care early. Early supported discharge sends a patient home before their usual discharge date, with treatment and monitoring continuing. Nursing teams contact patients every day, doctors review progress and adjust treatment when needed, tests and visits are arranged according to each patient's condition, and there are agreed steps for returning to hospital if the patient deteriorates.[1]

What the Evidence Shows: Shorter Stays, Continued Treatment

The study drew on 2,972 hospital at home episodes. After exclusions, it matched 1,488 episodes that began in hospital with patients who received usual care, and analyzed 754 admission-avoidance cases separately. It used propensity score matching, a statistical method that pairs patients who are similar on measured characteristics before comparing their outcomes. The method cannot remove the effect of differences that the data do not capture.[1]

The estimated difference in hospital length of stay was 3.13 days, with a 95% confidence interval of 2.60 to 3.67 days. This refers to days spent in hospital; treatment did not end when the patient went home. The researchers estimated that the program's pathways saved 13,119 bed days. That is a calculation of avoided inpatient days, not evidence that a specific number of beds was closed.[1]

By the trust's calculations, a day of home care cost about £118.50, compared with £569 for a day in hospital. After program costs were included in the economic model, the net benefit was estimated at about £3.79 million over 33 months. That does not mean actual cash spending fell by that amount.[1]

The study also found lower 30-day readmission and 90-day mortality in the home care group. It is a retrospective, non-randomized study, however, so it cannot show that the program alone caused these differences. Of the patients who answered the preference question in the experience survey, 95.8% preferred care at home; that figure does not represent everyone who was offered the service.[1]

The Tamayuz 10X Lens

Value and strategic direction: Staying inside a hospital building is no longer the only way to receive some forms of acute care. The place of treatment can now follow the patient's needs and whether care can be delivered safely.

Capability multiplication and empowerment: The service brought hospital doctors, nurses and community teams into a single pathway and extended monitoring and treatment beyond the inpatient wards.

Operating-model redesign: Referral, early discharge, home monitoring and escalation became connected parts of one service, instead of the team's responsibility ending when the patient left hospital.

Institutional and technical intelligence: Monitoring devices, communication and shared information helped clinical teams follow patients and decide when to visit, adjust treatment or bring a patient back to hospital. The technology did not replace clinical judgment.

Value and sustainable results: The evaluation showed shorter stays, an estimated financial benefit and strong acceptance among survey respondents. This study does not show whether the results will hold at larger scale or in other organizations.

How Health Organizations Can Use This

The first step is to identify which patients can be treated at home safely, taking into account their condition, the treatment they need, their living situation and how easily the team can reach them. Then assign clear responsibility for reviewing observations and test results, daily contact, adjusting treatment, urgent visits and bringing patients back to hospital.

Measure length of stay, readmissions, deterioration, the full cost of care, and the experience of patients and the burden on their families. A rising number of patients on a virtual ward is not success if it comes with slower intervention or care work quietly shifted onto families.

Transferability and Evidence Limits

Other organizations can use the principles, but buying monitoring equipment is not enough. The program needs trained home-care teams, access to tests, reliable transport and response services, and real coordination between the hospital and community care. Digital skills, or a relative who is free to help, should not become an unstated condition for getting appropriate care.

The study was published in a peer-reviewed journal, but several of its authors work for the organizations that run the service. It is retrospective and did not randomize patients, and the financial benefit rests on local costs and modeling assumptions. The long-term effects of scaling up, the burden on families, and the experience of patients who declined the service all need further evidence.[1][2]

From Future to Impact

The desired future is one in which every eligible patient receives the right care in the place best suited to their condition, without hospital stays that are not medically necessary. That requires shared clinical teams, reliable monitoring, fast access to tests and a clear escalation path.

What must be built today are precise criteria for selecting patients, joint training, secure information sharing, defined responsibilities, and indicators for the service's safety, cost and fairness. Then the place of treatment becomes a flexible clinical and organizational decision, not an automatic result of whether a bed happens to be free.

The 10X Question

What will a hospital look like in ten years if its beds are no longer the necessary place to treat every patient? If an eligible patient can finish treatment at home with a fast response guaranteed, which teams, skills, monitoring tools and home services should we start building now? And how will we show that the change has reduced the burden on patients, rather than moving the hospital's workload onto their families?
