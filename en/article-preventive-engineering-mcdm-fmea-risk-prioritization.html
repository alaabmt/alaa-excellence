When RPN Is Not Enough: Multi-Criteria Risk Prioritization in FMEA

A 169-paper systematic review shows that risk ranking is more than multiplying three scores. Criteria, weights, uncertainty and sensitivity all become part of the decision system.

169 studies show that prioritization is bigger than one multiplication rule

Liu, Chen, Duan and Wang systematically reviewed 169 journal papers published from 1998 to 2018 that used MCDM — Multi-Criteria Decision Making to evaluate and prioritize failure modes in FMEA. The papers were classified into ten method families and analyzed by risk factors, weighting approaches and uncertainty-handling methods. [1]

The motivation is straightforward. RPN — Risk Priority Number typically multiplies Severity, Occurrence and Detection as S × O × D. That is simple and familiar, but it does not by itself answer whether the three criteria should always have equal importance, how to treat identical products of very different risk profiles, or how to represent uncertain expert judgments and context-specific criteria.

Time boundary: the Scopus search was completed in December 2018 and the review was published in 2019. It is a strong map of literature up to that point, not a current 2026 state-of-the-art review. It also maps published method use; it does not prove that one MCDM method is operationally superior in every organization.

What was most common in the reviewed literature?

169papers passed final screening from 1,509 initial Scopus records.
10major MCDM method categories.
51 · 30.2%papers in distance-based methods, the largest category.
25 / 22papers using GRA and TOPSIS respectively, the two most frequent individual methods.

GRA — Grey Relational Analysis compares alternatives through relative relational closeness, often under incomplete information. TOPSIS — Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution ranks alternatives by closeness to an ideal and distance from a worst case. Their popularity does not make either a universal default; the review itself notes that different MCDM approaches can produce different rankings. [1]

Fuzzy set theory was the most frequently used family for handling imprecise expert assessments. The review also finds substantial attention to risk-factor weights — subjective, objective, combination and incomplete weighting approaches.

The key point: MCDM does not remove judgment. It makes more of that judgment explicit. Criteria, weights and aggregation rules therefore need governance and sensitivity testing.

One continuous case: an automated bolt-tightening station

This is an educational case, not one of the review's empirical cases. It shows where MCDM belongs in the nine-stage FMEA journey: primarily in prioritization, after the engineering logic and evidence are sound.
1

Define scope

What are we doing? Bound the process step.

Application: an automated station that tightens one critical fastener from part arrival through tightening-result confirmation.

Common mistake: choose an MCDM algorithm before understanding the process.

Output: clear start, end and interfaces.
2

Define required function

What are we doing? State what success means.

Application: achieve specified torque/angle and retain traceable confirmation for each part.

Common mistake: describe the tool instead of the function.

Output: measurable function statement.
3

Define failure mode, effect and cause

What are we doing? Build the engineering chain before scoring.

Application: failure mode: under-torque. Effect: joint fails to meet its mechanical requirement. Candidate cause: verified calibration drift or another demonstrated process condition.

Common mistake: jump from “low torque” to a priority number.

Output: function → failure mode → effect → cause.
4

Document current prevention and detection

What are we doing? Establish the real barriers.

Application: prevention may include calibration and scheduled maintenance; detection may include torque/angle signature monitoring and independent verification, if those controls actually exist.

Common mistake: credit a control that is only assumed.

Output: current controls and evidence of execution.
5

Rate Severity, Occurrence and Detection

What are we doing? Apply the organization's calibrated rating scales.

Application: S comes from consequence, O from cause/failure evidence, and D from demonstrated capability of current controls.

Common mistake: let a sophisticated priority method hide weak rating definitions.

Output: evidence-backed S/O/D ratings.
6

Choose and stress-test the priority method

What are we doing? This is where MCDM enters.

Application: compare the current RPN or approved priority rule with an MCDM method in shadow mode. Publish the criteria and weights, then vary plausible weights to see whether rankings are stable.

Common mistake: choose TOPSIS or GRA because it is popular in the literature rather than because it fits the decision.

Output: priority ranking, sensitivity analysis and method rationale.
7

Design treatment

What are we doing? Act on cause or control, not on the ranking model.

Application: if calibration drift is verified, treatment should reduce or detect that drift earlier.

Common mistake: improve the ranking model while leaving the failure mechanism untouched.

Output: owned action with due date and acceptance test.
8

Verify and reassess

What are we doing? Measure actual risk change.

Application: compare nonconforming tightening, detection performance and tool stability before and after action; change O or D only when evidence supports it.

Common mistake: treat a lower MCDM rank as evidence that risk fell.

Output: effectiveness evidence and residual risk.
9

Review continuously

What are we doing? Reopen both the engineering analysis and priority model when the system changes.

Application: changes in tool type, product, rating scales, criteria or weights require documented review.

Common mistake: freeze weights as if they were physical constants.

Output: living governance record of criteria, weights, rankings and decisions.

A safe pilot: run the new priority method in shadow mode first

Institutional MCDM pilot for FMEA prioritization
StepActionMeasure
1Select 15–30 existing failure modes with strong evidenceRating and evidence quality
2Run current priority method and candidate MCDM methodRank changes
3Review the largest differences with domain expertsEngineering meaning versus mathematical noise
4Vary weights within plausible rangesRanking sensitivity and stability
5Measure analysis effort and explainabilityOperational burden and team comprehension
6Approve, revise or reject the methodDocumented governance decision

Pilot success is not “more sophisticated numbers.” It is a decision that better preserves risk meaning, remains explainable, and is reasonably stable under plausible assumptions.

Evidence limits and methodological critique

The review is limited to Scopus journal articles from 1998–2018, so it does not cover every database, conference or post-2018 method. Frequency of GRA or TOPSIS does not establish operational superiority. The literature spans multiple industries, so popularity cannot be transferred directly to healthcare, aerospace or manufacturing. Most importantly, the review itself notes that different MCDM methods can produce different rankings. There is no neutral ranking machine: criteria, weights and aggregation are governance choices that require sensitivity analysis.

Future directions identified by the review

The paper points to better uncertainty handling, expert-consensus methods, additional risk factors where justified, improved weighting, dynamic FMEA, large and unstructured data, and combining AI with MCDM. [1]

The professional lesson is not to add an algorithm whenever RPN feels inadequate. It is to build a decision system that preserves the meaning of consequence, occurrence and detection, makes additional assumptions explicit, and shows how priority changes when those assumptions change.

The Preventive Engineering question

If two failure modes have the same RPN today, can your organization explain why they truly deserve equal priority? And if you add weights or criteria, can you show that the new ranking is stable and understandable rather than simply more mathematical?
