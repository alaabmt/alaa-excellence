How McLeod Health Turned Ambient AI Documentation into an Institutional Capability

The important story is not that AI can draft a clinical note. It is how a regional health system built a disciplined way to choose the technology, test it beyond vendor marketing, integrate it into clinical workflow, measure value from its own operating data, and then redesign adoption and commercial terms when the evidence justified scale.

Executive Summary

McLeod Health is a nonacademic health system with seven hospitals and more than 1,200 providers serving 18 counties across North and South Carolina. It faces the same structural problem as many regional systems: improve care and workforce sustainability while making consequential technology decisions without the research infrastructure of a major academic medical center.[1]

Rather than buy an ambient AI product on the strength of a vendor demonstration, McLeod designed a multiphase decision process beginning in February 2024: market scan, live simulation of four vendors against 15 complex standardized scenarios, blinded review of generated notes, Epic workflow demonstrations by the two finalists, and a 90-day real-world pilot before system-wide expansion.[1]

Among the 23 providers who met the analysis criteria, median time in note fell from 2.8 to 2.0 minutes per appointment, a 28.34% reduction (P<.001). Median “pajama time”—EHR work outside scheduled patient-care hours—fell from 5.6 to 3.6 hours per four-week period, but P=.054, so it should be treated as a promising trend rather than a conventionally significant effect. By publication, 250 providers had been onboarded, adoption among them was 81%, and nearly 150,000 notes had been generated.[1]

The Challenge: Documentation Was a Workflow Problem, Not Merely a Writing Problem

Ambient AI — Ambient Artificial Intelligence is technology that listens in the background of a clinical encounter and produces a structured draft note. In this model, the clinician remains responsible for reviewing, editing and approving the documentation; the AI does not autonomously finalize the medical record.[1][2]

EHR — Electronic Health Record is the digital clinical workspace where patient information, documentation and follow-up are managed. That distinction matters because a tool can generate impressive prose yet still fail if it adds clicks, copying, context switching or new operational friction. McLeod therefore treated Epic integration as part of the product decision, not an implementation detail to solve later.[1]

What McLeod Actually Changed

February 2024 — Design the decision before selecting the tool. The market scan excluded vendors with concerns around security, financial stability or scalability and considered product roadmaps beyond documentation.[1]

March–June 2024 — Test reality, not the sales demo. Fifteen standardized cases across family medicine, cardiology and general surgery included difficult accents, conflicting family input and interruptions. Vendor identity was removed before evaluators scored the notes.[1]

Workflow phase — Separate note quality from institutional fit. The top two vendors demonstrated live integration with Epic. Physicians assessed usability while informatics and security teams evaluated architecture, data safety and the implementation roadmap. The vendor that led the first-stage note-quality ranking did not become the final choice; workflow fit and broader capability changed the decision.[1]

October 2024 — Run a 90-day pilot. McLeod focused on real ambulatory use and required meaningful utilization plus baseline data for inclusion in outcome analysis. Leadership explicitly emphasized that efficiency gains should reduce after-hours documentation rather than simply increase patient volume during the pilot.[1]

January 2025 — Scale after evidence. Results were presented to senior leadership and system-wide implementation began. Growing demand then exposed a commercial constraint, so McLeod negotiated usage-based pricing with a monthly cap to reduce the risk of paying for unused licenses.[1]

March 2025 — Simplify onboarding. Self-onboarding was introduced to make adoption easier across a decentralized network of clinics and facilities.[1]

The 10X shift

From “Which AI documentation tool should we buy?” to “How do we build an institutional capability that tests claims, measures value in our own data, preserves clinician accountability, and scales only what proves useful?”

Forty-nine clinicians were onboarded during the three-month pilot and 39 were active adopters, a 79.6% pilot adoption rate. Only 23 met the predefined criteria for the quantitative effectiveness analysis. That distinction prevents an adoption metric from being confused with the evidence sample.[1]

Same-day note closure did not change significantly. Pajama time declined 35.4%, but the P value was .054. The evidence therefore supports a statistically significant reduction in time in note, while other efficiency signals should be described more cautiously.[1]

Patient experience: meaningful signal, not a clinical outcome

Across 21 providers, patient-experience scores increased by 8.4 points for “provider listened,” 7.5 for trust, 8.8 for knowing what to do with questions, and 10.4 for receiving enough treatment information; all were P<.001. The median survey response rate was 32.7%. These are patient-experience measures, not evidence of improved diagnosis, safety or clinical outcomes.[1]

Financial impact: modeled, not audited causal revenue

E/M — Evaluation and Management coding classifies the complexity of clinical work for billing. The study observed shifts in coding and an 8.5% increase in established-patient volume, producing a modeled combined gain of $2,629 per provider per month. The authors explicitly note that there was no concurrent control group and seasonal variation cannot be excluded. The figure is therefore an estimate, not a proven causal revenue gain.[1]

What Could the Organization Now See and Decide That It Could Not Before?

McLeod could compare vendors on standardized encounters, distinguish note quality from workflow fit, connect actual use to EHR-derived metrics, see adoption as an operating variable, and redesign commercial terms when unused-license risk threatened scale. The deeper capability was therefore not “having AI.” It was having a better institutional method for deciding what AI deserved to become part of routine care.

Evidence Boundaries

Discipline matters more than excitement. This was a before-and-after implementation without a concurrent control group. The study authors were McLeod employees. The effectiveness sample was limited. Patient-experience measures are not clinical outcomes. Full post-scale analysis was still in progress. Published sources do not provide complete internal change, training and integration costs; long-term burnout or retention outcomes; post-scale note error/correction rates; or detailed performance by specialty and demographic group. Those gaps should remain gaps rather than be filled by inference.

What Leaders Can Transfer

  1. Design the decision process before vendor demonstrations. Define scenarios, measures and exclusion criteria first.
  2. Test difficult reality. Include interruptions, accents, ambiguity and conflicting information.
  3. Separate output quality from workflow quality. The best note is not automatically the best enterprise solution.
  4. Measure from your own systems. Do not make the vendor dashboard the only source of truth.
  5. Decide what efficiency gains are for. McLeod initially directed gains toward reducing after-hours work rather than increasing volume.
  6. Treat pricing as part of the operating model. A technically successful solution can still fail to scale under the wrong commercial structure.

Transferability

Other organizations can copy the decision architecture: standardized blinded testing, multidisciplinary evaluation, a bounded pilot, baseline measures, internal data verification and explicit human accountability. They should not copy McLeod’s percentages as promised outcomes. Those results belong to a specific system, user cohort, EHR environment and time period.

Company Insider Critique

An independent critique simulating the perspective of a senior internal expert—without claiming to represent McLeod—identified four corrections that shaped this revision. First, do not reduce the case to an “AI scribe”; the hard work was selection, Epic integration, measurement, pricing and adoption. Second, preserve the timeline so scale is not presented as preceding evidence. Third, do not turn P=.054 or modeled financial impact into proven causal effects. Fourth, make clinician review and the denominator behind the 81% adoption figure explicit. The case has been rewritten accordingly.

The future-focused 10X questionIf the desired future is one in which clinicians spend most of their time and attention with patients while clinical conversations become reliable, reviewable documentation with minimal administrative burden, what capabilities, governance, data, integrations and skills must the organization build today to reach that future safely?

Backcast from that future: define the capabilities, establish baselines and data, assign human accountability, design the test environment, build the adoption model, and decide what evidence is strong enough to justify moving from pilot to institutional capability.
