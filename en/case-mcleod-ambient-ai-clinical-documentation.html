From Documentation Burden to Ambient Clinical Capacity: How McLeod Health Redesigned Clinical Documentation

The case is not simply that a health system bought an AI documentation tool. McLeod Health built a way to compare alternatives, test them against standardized clinical scenarios, measure impact before scaling, and then change onboarding and commercial arrangements to make adoption more practical.

Executive Snapshot

McLeod began designing a multiphase selection process in February 2024. Four vendors were tested in live simulations using 15 complex outpatient scenarios. The strongest candidates advanced to integration and usability evaluation, followed by a 90-day pilot across five ambulatory specialties beginning in October 2024. System-wide rollout began in January 2025. In the pilot sample, median time in notes fell from 2.8 to 2.0 minutes per appointment, a 28.34% reduction (P<.001, n=23). By the 2026 publication, 250 providers had been onboarded, adoption among them was 81%, and nearly 150,000 notes had been generated.[1]

The Challenge

Ambient AI — Ambient Artificial Intelligence refers here to technology that operates in the background of a clinical encounter, captures the conversation and generates a structured draft note. The clinician remains responsible for reviewing, editing and approving the documentation. The desired capability was therefore not autonomous charting; it was lower administrative burden while preserving clinical judgment and accountability.[1][2]

EHR — Electronic Health Record is the digital clinical workspace in which patient information, documentation and related workflows are managed. The solution therefore had to work inside the EHR workflow rather than merely produce good text in isolation.[1]

The 10X Move

Shift the decision from “Which tool should we buy?” to “How do we build an institutional capability to test claims, measure value, preserve clinician accountability and scale what proves useful?”

The evaluation was designed to reduce brand effects and groupthink. Vendors were challenged with standardized scenarios containing interruptions, complexity and contradictory dialogue. Physicians, revenue-cycle experts and nonclinical reviewers assessed generated notes. The strongest solutions then faced workflow-integration and usability testing before a real-world pilot.[1]

That sequence turned technology procurement into a testable decision system: output quality, workflow fit, documentation burden, provider and patient experience, and financial implications. Self-onboarding was introduced in March 2025 to simplify expansion, while usage-based pricing reduced the risk of paying for unused licenses.[1]

Evidence of Impact

−28.34%Median time in notes: 2.8 to 2.0 minutes per appointment; n=23; P<.001.[1]
−35.43%Median “pajama time”—EHR work outside scheduled patient-care hours—fell from 5.6 to 3.6 hours per four weeks, but P=.054. This is a notable trend, not a conventionally statistically significant result.[1]
81%Adoption among 250 onboarded providers, with nearly 150,000 generated notes at the time of publication. It is not 81% of every McLeod provider.[1]

Patient-experience measures improved across listening, trust, knowing what to do with questions, and receiving sufficient treatment information among 21 providers. These are patient-experience measures, not clinical outcomes; the median survey response rate was 32.7%.[1]

E/M — Evaluation and Management coding is the US coding framework used to represent the complexity of clinical evaluation and management work for billing. The study observed coding shifts and an 8.5% increase in established-patient volume, associated with a projected US$2,629 monthly revenue gain per provider. This is an estimate associated with coding and volume changes, not audited incremental revenue causally attributable to the tool. There was no concurrent control group, and the authors explicitly could not exclude seasonality.[1] KLAS also reported McLeod within a professional ROI validation across three organizations, but that report sits in a commercial product context and is not an independent academic replication.[3]

Evidence limits: the design is before-and-after rather than randomized; the principal study authors are from McLeod; some coding and financial extracts came from the vendor and were internally checked by McLeod; detailed data are proprietary; and full post-scale analysis was still underway. The broader evidence base was also still being assembled systematically in 2026, so one health system’s results should not be generalized without local validation.[1][4]

The 10X Lens

Foresight and Ambition: McLeod documents a future-oriented direction in which technology works quietly in the background so clinicians can focus more fully on human care. There is no public evidence of a formal foresight methodology.[2]

Leadership, Transformation Culture and Future Governance: clinical, operational, financial, IT and informatics leaders participated in evaluation and selection, with deliberate safeguards against biased vendor choice.[1]

Capability Multiplication and Empowerment: reduced documentation time can release clinician capacity. The strongest evidence is time in notes, not a proven long-term reduction in burnout.[1]

Enterprise and Operating-Model Reinvention: selection, integration, onboarding, pricing and scaling mechanisms changed—not merely the software.[1]

Innovation and Experimentation: simulation, pilot testing and measurement preceded scale.[1]

Institutional and Technological Intelligence: McLeod became able to compare vendors using standardized clinical challenges, read documentation effects from EHR data and make scaling decisions from operational evidence rather than vendor claims alone.[1]

From Future to Impact

Desired Future: clinical encounters in which the clinician’s attention remains with the patient while conversation becomes reliable, reviewable documentation with minimal administrative friction.

Required Capabilities: reliable capture and drafting, clinician oversight, EHR integration, continuous measurement, privacy and security governance, and adoption capability.

What Must Be Built Now: standardized test cases, baseline measures, multidisciplinary evaluation, human-review controls, technical integration, and a commercial model compatible with gradual adoption.

New Operating Behavior: define the future first, test the capabilities required to reach it, and scale only what demonstrates value.

Outcomes and Impact: this case documents a significant reduction in time in notes and improvements in patient-experience measures, with operational and financial signals that require more cautious interpretation.[1]

Practical Use for Leaders and Institutions

The transferable mechanism is the decision architecture. Build test cases around difficult real work, reduce brand cues where possible, combine clinical, operational and financial assessment, establish baselines before the pilot, and define in advance what evidence will justify scaling or stopping. During rollout, measure actual adoption rather than licenses purchased.

Transferability

The staged selection-and-measurement method is highly transferable. The effect sizes are not. Specialty mix, workflow, baseline burden, EHR environment, patient population and usage model can materially change results. Organizations operating under different language, privacy or regulatory conditions should re-test accuracy and fairness locally.

The Future-Focused 10X Question

If the desired future is one in which clinicians can keep most of their attention on patients while clinical conversation becomes reliable, reviewable documentation with minimal administrative burden, what capabilities, skills, data, systems, governance and ways of working must your institution build now to reach that future safely?
