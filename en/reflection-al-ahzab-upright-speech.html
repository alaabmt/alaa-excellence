What Makes Speech Upright When Admitting Error Is Hard?

A reflection on Al-Ahzab 33:70–71, upright speech, and how truth, justice and repair meet when admitting error is difficult.

Imagine a team discovers that a public presentation included the wrong number. An initial decision has already been made on the strength of that figure. The manager now faces an uncomfortable choice. A vague statement—“new information has emerged”—might protect appearances. A clear correction would acknowledge that the original information was wrong, explain what changed, and reopen the affected decision.

The difference is not simply one of tone. It is a question of purpose: Is the correction meant to restore the truth and repair the consequences of the error, or to manage impressions while others continue to act on incomplete information?

Two verses in Surah Al-Ahzab open a wider way of thinking about that choice. They do not reduce good speech to partial accuracy or persuasive delivery. They call for qawlan sadida: speech that is upright, just, and directed toward what is right.

Speech that aims at truth without injustice

Allah says:

“O you who have believed, fear Allah and speak words of appropriate justice. He will [then] amend for you your deeds and forgive you your sins. And whoever obeys Allah and His Messenger has certainly attained a great attainment.”

>

Surah Al-Ahzab, 33:70–71. Translation: Sahih International.

The command follows a warning not to be like those who harmed Moses through what they said; Allah cleared him of their allegations.[1] The sequence makes the consequences of speech difficult to ignore. Words do not always disappear once spoken. They can become injury, accusation, or a false account that continues to shape how people think and act.

Al-Tabari explains qawlan sadida as speech that is rightly directed rather than unjust—truth rather than falsehood—and records meanings that include honesty and justice.[2] Ibn Kathir describes it as straight speech, free from crookedness or deviation.[3] Al-Qurtubi gathers several related meanings: what is right, true, and sound; agreement between outward words and inward intent; reconciliation; and speech that encompasses good.[4] Al-Sa‘di includes speech that conforms to what is right, or comes close to it when certainty is unavailable. He also includes sincere counsel, gentleness, and pointing to what is best.[5]

Together, these readings demand more than the instruction to “tell the truth.” A sentence can be technically accurate yet omit what another person needs to understand the situation. It can be direct yet unfair, assigning blame that has not been established. It can sound considerate while allowing the error to keep doing harm. Upright speech seeks truth, aims at what is right, treats people justly, and looks toward repair.

Why does the repair of deeds follow the command about speech?

The verses move from the command to be mindful of Allah and speak uprightly to a divine promise: Allah will amend the believers’ deeds and forgive their sins. Al-Tabari reads the amendment of deeds as Allah granting success in righteous action. Ibn Kathir likewise speaks of being guided to good deeds. Al-Sa‘di explains that mindfulness of Allah and upright speech are causes of sound deeds, their acceptance, and their protection from what would spoil them.[6]

This is a promise from Allah within the life of faith and obedience. It is not a management formula in which every candid admission guarantees a favorable worldly outcome. A correction may bring embarrassment, delay, or cost. Trust may not return quickly. The verses nevertheless refuse to separate speech from action.

We may reflect that the desire to repair one’s actions sits uneasily with speech that deliberately leaves falsehood in place. Words chosen to protect the speaker while preserving the effects of an error move away from the meaning of uprightness presented in the tafsir.

Clear speech can open a path to repairing action. Naming an error makes it possible to revisit a decision built on it. Distinguishing what is known from what is assumed reduces the risk that confidence will harden into false certainty. Recognizing who has been affected shifts correction away from reputation management and toward responsibility for consequences.

A hypothetical correction that does not hide behind ambiguity

Return to the imagined presentation. The team discovers that the reported figure combined two different categories, producing a result higher than the actual one. The error was not intentional, but it influenced the initial decision made after the presentation. The manager has at least three possible responses. They may look similar, yet they do not carry the same meaning.

The first is to replace the figure silently in the online version. Some people will see the corrected number. Those who attended the presentation, however, may continue to believe that the decision rested on valid original data.

The second is to announce that “the data has been updated.” The statement is not an explicit lie, but it conceals the error in the calculation and the need to reconsider the affected decision.

The third is a direct correction. It identifies the number originally presented, explains the mistake in the calculation, gives the corrected figure, names the decision that may have been affected, and provides a process for reviewing it. It can also state what remains unresolved instead of filling the gap with a new guess.

This example is not an interpretation of the verses, and it does not place every professional mistake in the category of the harm described in the preceding context. It is a limited contemporary application of one question: Does our speech move toward truth and repair, or does it use careful wording to leave others acting on an incomplete account?

Upright speech when certainty is incomplete

The right words may seem easier to find when all the facts are available. Many consequential situations offer no such comfort. We may know that a number is doubtful without having the final replacement. We may know that a complaint requires investigation without being ready to reach a judgment.

Al-Sa‘di’s reference to speech that conforms to what is right—or comes close to it when certainty cannot be reached—helps define the responsibility here.[5] Incomplete information does not require manufactured certainty, nor does it justify silence about the limits of what is known.

Upright speech might say: “This information has not yet been verified.” It might distinguish the confirmed portion from what remains under review. It might explain that a decision has been paused until the evidence is complete.

Acknowledging the degree of certainty does not weaken a statement. It prevents the statement from carrying more weight than the evidence can bear. It also preserves the difference between an established fact, a likely interpretation, and an untested possibility. The desire to sound decisive should not push speech ahead of proof.

Uprightness is not harshness

Clarity does not require humiliation. Al-Sa‘di includes gentle speech, sincere advice, and pointing toward what is best among the forms of upright speech.[5] A correction can therefore be candid while preserving dignity. It can name the action and its effects without claiming to know another person’s intentions. It can allow a response and distinguish an error that can be repaired from a deliberate pattern that calls for a different form of accountability.

Gentleness, however, cannot mean softening the truth until it disappears. A considerate statement that hides information capable of changing another person’s decision is not fully considerate. A harsh statement may contain part of the truth while losing justice and the intention to repair. Upright speech rejects the false choice between cruel honesty and comforting deception. It seeks words that are truthful, fair, and constructive to the extent we are able.

From the speaker’s image to responsibility for impact

When status feels threatened, a hidden question can take over: How little can I disclose without being accused of lying? Mindfulness of Allah changes the center of the decision: What speech would please Allah, aim at the truth, and avoid injustice?

That change affects how errors are corrected. A correction becomes more than a defensive statement. It carries responsibility for what the earlier words set in motion. Depending on the circumstances, repair may require an apology, a corrected record, a review of the decision, notice to those who relied on the wrong information, and a process that reduces the chance of repetition. These steps are not presented as a tafsir of the verses. They are contemporary ways of testing whether speech is serious about repairing the effects of error.

Good intentions alone do not complete that repair. Someone may apologize sincerely yet leave the old figure on a dashboard, preserve the affected decision, or allow the misleading message to keep circulating. The distance between words and action then remains.

The question to ask before speaking

Upright speech is not a perfect phrase discovered after every uncertainty has disappeared. It is an orientation that begins with mindfulness of Allah, proceeds through truth and justice, aims at what is right, and seeks repair without pretending to possess certainty that is not there.

Before correcting an error, answering an accusation, or passing along incomplete information, we can ask: Will people understand both what I know and the limits of what I do not know? Have I treated those affected justly? Do my words open a path to repair, or do they leave others under the influence of incomplete information?

Allah’s promise is greater than our calculations: He will amend the believers’ deeds and forgive their sins. Our responsibility is to be mindful of Him, to speak in a way that aims at truth without injustice, and to follow those words with action that repairs their effects.

Methodological note

This reflection draws on the tafsir of Al-Tabari, Ibn Kathir, Al-Qurtubi, and Al-Sa‘di before moving into reflection and a hypothetical contemporary application. The professional example is not offered as an interpretation of the verses, nor does it establish a new religious ruling. Questions of intention, harm, responsibility, accusation, and individual rights require qualified scholarly and professional judgment in each specific case.
