10X Case · Energy and Public Policy

How Uruguay Made Its Electricity Almost Entirely Renewable in About Ten Years

In 2007, Uruguay was rationing electricity and bracing for blackouts. It has no oil, no gas, and no coal. About a decade later, it was generating roughly 98% of its electricity from water, wind, biomass, and sunlight. The political agreement came before the turbines, and everything else was built on it.

Executive Summary

Until 2008, Uruguay’s electricity came from hydroelectric dams and imported oil. When rainfall dropped, so did hydro output, and the country had to burn oil or buy power from Argentina and Brazil at high prices. In severe drought years, oil supplied 30% to 40% of the power mix. [9] There was no easy way out: Uruguay has no proven reserves of oil, gas, or coal, and no room for new large dams. [1]

Meanwhile, the economy was growing at about 6% a year, demand for electricity kept rising, and households faced rationing, early blackouts, and rising bills. [1][10] A case study by SOAS (School of Oriental and African Studies), University of London, says the country spent as much as 2% of GDP on energy imports in some drought years. [8]

In 2008, the government adopted a long-term plan, the Energy Policy 2005–2030 (Política Energética 2005–2030), with short-, medium-, and long-term goals. In 2010, a commission of every party in parliament endorsed it, turning a government program into a state commitment that would outlast any one administration. [1][2][5] Ramón Méndez, a physicist who served as national energy director from 2008 to 2015, led the work. [1]

Two things changed in how the system operated. First, the state utility ran competitive auctions in which private companies built wind farms and the utility bought their output at an agreed price for 20 years, with no direct subsidies. [1][10] Second, the grid’s operating rules were rewritten so that wind became the base supply, with hydro following it hour by hour to fill the gaps. [1]

Wind capacity grew from zero in 2007 to 1,508 megawatts across 44 wind farms by 2020. [1][2] In 2019, 98% of the electricity generated came from renewable sources. [2] A multiyear drought pushed fossil fuels up to 15% of generation in 2021, but their share fell to 8% by 2023, and renewables reached 99% in 2024 and 98% in 2025. [4][5][6]

The shift has been mostly limited to electricity, however. Oil products still made up 33% of primary energy supply in 2025, and transport accounted for 63% of carbon dioxide emissions. [6] The long-term contracts also locked in purchase prices that now look high next to today’s cheaper renewables, and consumers did not see the price drop many expected. [8]

For 10X purposes, the lesson is in the order of events. Uruguay first asked what kind of electricity system it wanted by 2030, then worked back to what it had to build now: political consensus, contracts, technical models, and operating rules, all before the turbines went up. The state’s long-term commitment itself became the guarantee that brought investors in.

When Rainfall Set the Price of Power

In wet years, dams on the Uruguay and Negro rivers supplied most of the country’s electricity. In dry years, imported fuel filled the gap, and costs climbed with every barrel. [1][9][12] Méndez remembers it as a time when electricity was hard to come by and blackouts were starting. [10]

The main option on the table was a nuclear plant. Méndez, a nuclear physicist, wrote a paper arguing that wind combined with other renewables was the better path. The paper reached President Tabaré Vázquez, who asked him to run the country’s energy policy. [8][10] According to the SOAS study, citing the New York Times, experts at the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund told Uruguay that a transition on that scale was not possible without subsidies the country could not afford. [8]

The government did not sell the plan as a climate pledge. It framed it as energy sovereignty: cheaper, steadier power produced at home. Méndez says he told people it was the best option even if they did not believe in climate change, because it was the cheapest and did not depend on swings in oil prices. [8] His own early presentation of the policy described it as adaptation first, with emissions cuts a secondary concern. [1]

The 10X Move

Uruguay used to buy fuel year after year and absorb the swings in both oil prices and rainfall. It switched to buying electricity itself, from domestic sources, under 20-year contracts. That turned an unpredictable fuel bill into a fixed, known obligation, and it moved the cost of construction onto investors. Investors accepted that deal because every party had signed on to the same policy, so a contract no longer depended on who won the next election. [1][8][10]

From Policy Paper to Power Grid

  1. In 2000, a team of engineers from the University of the Republic installed the country’s first modern wind turbine on Cerro de los Caracoles, a hill in southeastern Uruguay. [12]
  2. In 2007, the government launched a wind energy program with a $1 million grant from the Global Environment Facility (GEF) and $6 million from its own budget. The program trained workers, built a demonstration wind farm, created university courses, and reached out to developers and investors. [9]
  3. In 2008, the executive branch adopted the Energy Policy 2005–2030, and Ramón Méndez became national energy director. [1][2]
  4. In 2009, Uruguay began holding auctions in which wind companies competed on price, and the state utility signed 20-year contracts to buy the winners’ power. [10]
  5. In 2010, a cross-party energy commission endorsed the policy, and the new president, José Mujica, kept Méndez in his post to keep the plan on track. [2][8]
  6. In 2011, an auction sought 150 megawatts of wind power, about 5% of generating capacity. More than 20 developers bid, and the team accepted far more offers than planned, adding more than 40% to the country’s generating capacity. [10] Wind power that year cost $62 per megawatt-hour. [1]
  7. In 2013, Uruguay became a net exporter of electricity to Argentina and Brazil. [2]
  8. In 2019, 98% of electricity came from renewable sources, and exports topped 3,000 gigawatt-hours, or 21% of generation. [2]
  9. Starting around 2020, a long drought pushed fossil fuels up to 15% of generation in 2021, before their share fell to 9% in 2022 and 8% in 2023. The energy ministry says interconnections with its neighbors reduced the need to run thermal plants. [3][4]
  10. In 2024, renewables supplied 99% of electricity, and in 2025, 98%. [5][6]

Who Decides, Who Builds, and Who Runs the Grid?

The roles were clearly divided. The Ministry of Industry, Energy and Mining sets and coordinates policy. The state utility, UTE (Administración Nacional de Usinas y Trasmisiones Eléctricas), and the state oil company are its main instruments. Private companies build and operate most new generation under a published, stable regulatory framework. [1]

The grid stayed in public hands. UTE runs the auctions, buys the power, and handles transmission and distribution. [8][10] It also built some wind farms itself, working with universities so its own staff learned the technology firsthand, and the government invested in transmission and distribution lines. [8]

Before the wind farms went up, planners needed to know when the wind would blow and when the rivers would run. A team at the University of the Republic built a model using a century of data to forecast wind, water, and solar availability. That allowed the system to rely on the cheapest sources, with hydro filling in when needed and no battery storage. [8]

Day-to-day operations changed as well. Wind became the base supply, hydro followed it hour by hour, and biomass and natural gas filled in. Dispatch rules and grid expansion criteria were rewritten, and interconnection capacity reached 2,000 megawatts with Argentina and 570 megawatts with Brazil. [1]

The shift also needed the support of the people it affected. When fossil fuel plants closed, the government negotiated with energy unions and used early retirement and retraining. Cattle ranchers could lease land to wind farms and keep grazing their herds beneath the turbines. [8][9]

The Evidence and Its Limits

Generation figures come from the BEN (Balance Energético Nacional), the national energy balance that Uruguay’s energy directorate has published every year since 1965. Independent sources match the overall picture. The World Resources Institute (WRI) puts wind at about 40% of electricity, and NPR (National Public Radio) reports that 98% of the grid is renewable in a typical year. [5][9][10]

Wind’s share moves with the rain, though. In 2024, a year when the dams were back at high output, 43% of electricity came from hydro, 28% from wind, 24% from biomass, and 4% from solar. [5] Not all of the renewable share is a result of wind policy, either. Biomass power rose 43% in 2024 once a third pulp mill, which generates most of its own energy from wood waste, was running at full capacity. [5]

The investment figures, $7.1 billion between 2010 and 2015 and more than $8 billion since 2010, come from government sources, and this analysis found no independent check of them. [1][2] The SOAS study cites a secondary source for an estimate of about 50,000 jobs created, which should not be treated as an established result. [8]

This analysis found no independent series of consumer electricity prices before and after the transition. NPR reports that inflation-adjusted prices fell. The SOAS study says UTE is locked into contracts that cost more than it would pay today, and that the government spent part of the savings on social programs instead of cutting bills, which disappointed consumers who expected bigger reductions. [8][10] The renewable share also refers to electricity, not to total energy: renewables made up 64% of primary energy supply in 2024. [5]

The 10X Lens

Foresight and Ambition

In 2008, the government adopted a policy running to 2030 with short-, medium-, and long-term goals. The sources do not show a formal foresight method, but they do show a clear long-term orientation. [1][2]

Leadership and Future-Ready Governance

Cross-party agreement turned a government program into a state commitment, and the next president kept the same delivery team in place. [2][8]

Reinventing the Operating Model

Private investors built generation, the state kept the grid and the purchasing role, and operating rules were rewritten around wind. [1][8]

Multiplying Capabilities

Technical capacity was built in universities and inside the state utility through models, training, and a demonstration wind farm, both before and during the scale-up. [8][9]

From Desired Future to Impact

Desired future → Stable, affordable, domestic electricity that does not depend on rainfall or oil prices. [1][8]

Required capabilities → A political agreement that would last, a contract framework investors could trust, detailed knowledge of wind and water patterns, and a grid able to run on a variable source.

What was built → A cross-party commission, auctions with 20-year contracts, a university planning model based on a century of data, new operating rules, and interconnections with neighboring countries. [1][8][10]

New operating behavior → Wind became the base of generation with hydro as its backup, and private investors built while the state bought the power and ran the grid.

Outcomes and impact → About 98% renewable electricity in most years, plus exports to neighbors. Transport and industry have not made the same shift, and that is the next stage. [2][5][6]

Insider Critique and Evidence Limits

If this case were shown to the team at Uruguay’s national energy directorate from that period, they would probably object to reducing the story to wind. The plan was broader. The main actions listed in Méndez’s 2015 presentation also included a liquefied natural gas terminal, domestic oil and gas exploration, energy efficiency, and access to electricity as a human right. [1]

They might also point out that the framework adapted along the way. Contract terms changed from one auction to the next through successive decrees, and the team accepted far more offers than planned when interest exceeded expectations. [2][10] They might add that criticism of prices ignores what drought years would have cost if oil had remained the backup. That argument is reasonable, but the available sources do not measure those avoided costs.

Other criticism carries weight. In 2015, Méndez himself listed demand management, storage, and transport as “work in progress.” [1] In 2024, Brazilian researchers, most of them from the Federal University of Santa Catarina, warned that the lack of explicit plans to expand wind capacity could lead to a decline in wind generation. [11] In 2025, the ministry published an expansion plan through 2043. It projects that demand will exceed 14,000 gigawatt-hours a year by 2035, calls for new solar and wind capacity, considers backup gas turbines from 2032 in higher-demand scenarios, and finds that batteries could delay that need by four years if they become available at competitive prices. [7]

What can be said with confidence is that Uruguay’s electricity has been about 98% renewable in most recent years, and that this outcome is closely tied to a long-term policy and a new contracting and operating model. What cannot be said with confidence is the exact effect on consumer bills, the number of jobs created, or how much of the result comes from policy rather than rainfall and industry.

Practical Use

When a change will take longer than a leader’s tenure, start with what will keep it alive afterward. Ask who must agree so that the decision is not reversed at the first change of leadership, and what long-term commitment would give partners the confidence to invest alongside you. Then design the rules for running the new assets, not just the assets themselves. And watch from the start for advantages that could turn into burdens, such as a long contract that ends up priced above the market.

Transferability

Uruguay had several conditions in its favor: strong winds, existing dams that could serve as backup, a small population, a state utility with a high credit rating, and political and regulatory stability that reassured investors. [8] A country without dams would need storage or regional interconnections to play the same role. Still, the underlying approach can work for many governments and companies: an agreement that lasts, clear contracts, local technical capability, and operating rules designed around the new source.

The 10X Question

Fifteen years from now, what would it look like for your organization to keep delivering, no matter how the price of a resource it does not control moves? What could it do then that it cannot do today? And if that is the future you want, which agreements, contracts, skills, and operating rules do you need to start building now, before you buy any technology?
