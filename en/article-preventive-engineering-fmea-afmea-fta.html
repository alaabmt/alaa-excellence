When One View of Failure Is Not Enough: Connecting FMEA, AFMEA and FTA

As systems become more complex, a component list alone can hide the path to failure. A 2011 comparison shows how top-down fault logic can be connected to bottom-up FMEA so causal structure becomes an owned control and action.

Three methods, three starting points

Yu, Yang, Liu and Pan compare three reliability-analysis approaches. FMEA — Failure Mode and Effects Analysis is presented as an inductive, bottom-up method that begins with components, functions and potential failures. AFMEA — Advanced Failure Modes and Effects Analysis adds behavior modeling and reasons from system-level behavior. FTA — Fault Tree Analysis is deductive and top-down: it starts with an undesired top event and decomposes the logic into lower-level events and causes. [1]

The useful distinction is not that one method is universally superior. Each forces a different starting question. FMEA connects failure to the physical component and the work that can be changed. FTA organizes causal logic. AFMEA pushes the team to examine behavior and interaction patterns that may not appear if the analysis starts only from a parts list.

Evidence boundary: this is a 2011 paper using two illustrative cases. Several claims about which method is “more suitable” for simple, complex, stable or unstable products are the authors' interpretation from those examples, not a universal standard. The stronger lesson is about analysis direction and combination, not a rigid method ranking.

Why behavior analysis can reveal a different class of failure

The first case uses a box fan. The FMEA view decomposes the product into components and relationships and records failure modes, causes and effects. The AFMEA view starts with product behavior — moving air, controlling airflow and auxiliary behavior — then looks for missing, undesired or misused behavior. [1]

One example is exposure to substantially higher supply voltage, which can damage a capacitor and prevent startup. Another is an operating action around the timer that produces a result different from the user's intent. The practical question is broader than “which component breaks?” It is: what if the components are individually plausible, but environment, interaction or use produces an undesired system behavior?

Compare the three starting points below. Treat them as complementary lenses rather than competing checklists.

The pattern: a component-only analysis can miss interaction scenarios; a logic-only tree can stop before responsibility and controls become operational. The combination is valuable when it closes that gap.

The aircraft-flap case: use FTA to structure causes, then FMEA to anchor action

The paper proposes a combined approach using an aircraft flap system. Its scope includes the flap, flap cabin and slides connecting the flap to the wing, while excluding the driver from the case. The selected top event is that the mechanism used to open and close the flaps becomes stuck. A fault tree is built, bottom events are identified, and those events are mapped to physical components through FMEA. [1]

The reported table includes causes or conditions such as poor surface roughness, low accuracy, excessive clearance and a stuck kinematic pair. It also lists operating and maintenance factors. These entries organize investigation; they are not automatically standard FMEA risk ratings.

Define system and interfaces→Define top event→Build FTA→Identify bottom events→Map events to components with FMEA→Assign preventive action

The operating logic: FTA explains how the top event can be reached; FMEA helps decide where a design, process or control change belongs.

What did the paper actually do with risk ratings?

In the box-fan example, the paper publishes numeric columns for probability, severity and “check,” then multiplies them — for example 3 × 10 × 6 = 180. The attached paper does not publish a detailed rating rubric or calibration rules. In the FTA-guided flap example, the table uses normalized condition values and does not present a conventional, fully defined Severity/Occurrence/Detection rating set for the case. [1]

Severity (S) should represent consequence, Occurrence (O) the likelihood of the cause or failure under the organization's scale, and Detection (D) the ability of current controls to detect the problem before the effect reaches its target. The flap table's normalized values should not be converted automatically into S/O/D scores.

If the organization uses RPN — Risk Priority Number, typically S × O × D, it should not become the sole treatment decision. Severe effects, critical controls and the organization's approved prioritization method may override simple RPN ordering.

A nine-stage reconstruction using the same flap event

This is an educational reconstruction, not nine steps reported by the paper. It keeps the paper's top event — a stuck flap opening/closing mechanism — and extends it through a complete preventive-engineering workflow. Any risk rating, control claim or effectiveness result must come from the organization's actual system data.
1

Define the analysis scope

What are we doing? Fix the system boundary so causes and interfaces do not drift.

Application: use the paper's scope: flap, flap cabin and connecting slides; record the driver as excluded. Top event: opening/closing mechanism stuck.

Common mistake: calling the scope “wing system” and mixing structural, drive, control and maintenance causes without boundaries.

Output: explicit boundary, interfaces and top event.
2

Define components and required functions

What are we doing? State what each item must accomplish.

Application: the motion path must allow controlled flap travel; guides, pulleys and limiting pairs must constrain that motion without binding or unacceptable clearance.

Common mistake: listing components without writing the required function.

Output: component-to-function map.
3

Separate failure mode, effect and cause

What are we doing? Keep “what failed,” “what happened” and “why” distinct.

Application: failure mode: motion binds or stops; effect: flap mechanism cannot open/close as required; reported candidate causes: poor surface roughness, low accuracy, excessive clearance or a stuck motion pair.

Common mistake: writing “flap stuck” in every column.

Output: function → failure mode → effect → cause chain.
4

Document actual prevention and detection controls

What are we doing? Identify what prevents the cause and what detects it before consequence.

Application: the paper mentions maintenance level/frequency and dimensional or surface factors, but it does not publish a complete control architecture. The real team must document specifications, process checks, lubrication/maintenance controls, inspection and functional testing that actually exist.

Common mistake: recording a proposed future action as if it were already a current control.

Output: evidence-backed current-control list.
5

Rate Severity, Occurrence and Detection

What are we doing? Convert local evidence into calibrated ratings.

Application: do not reuse the paper's normalized flap values as S/O/D. Severity comes from the effect in the approved system context; Occurrence from defect/failure evidence; Detection from verified capability of current checks to catch the condition in time.

Common mistake: inventing ratings because the source happens to contain numbers.

Output: documented ratings with evidence — or an explicit data gap.
6

Set treatment priority

What are we doing? Combine fault-tree logic with risk evidence.

Application: bottom events on important causal paths deserve attention, but tree position alone does not set priority. Consider consequence, occurrence evidence, detection strength and the organization's approved prioritization rule.

Common mistake: treating either FTA position or RPN as an automatic decision.

Output: prioritized causes with rationale.
7

Design and implement treatment

What are we doing? Change a verified cause or control.

Educational application: if surface finish or clearance is confirmed, treatment could involve tighter specification, stronger process capability, standardized verification or maintenance redesign. The actual engineering choice must come from system data and authority.

Common mistake: writing “improve maintenance” without owner, target condition or acceptance test.

Output: cause-linked action, owner, due date and acceptance criterion.
8

Verify effectiveness and reassess

What are we doing? Prove that treatment changed the risk, not just the paperwork.

Application: compare dimensional/surface inspection, functional-motion test results and binding/failure history before and after action. Lower O or D only when evidence supports the change.

Common mistake: equating action completion with risk reduction.

Output: before/after evidence and residual-risk assessment.
9

Keep the two models synchronized

What are we doing? Maintain the connection between fault logic and the FMEA record.

Application: changes in materials, tolerances, maintenance pattern, usage rate or connection design should reopen the linked FTA branch and FMEA row.

Common mistake: updating one artifact while the other remains obsolete.

Output: a living trace from top event to bottom event, component, control, action and evidence.

How to run a small institutional pilot

Start with one meaningful top event in one bounded system. Build a compact fault tree, then map bottom events into the organization's existing FMEA rather than rebuilding the entire library.

The team should include system ownership, design/engineering, quality or reliability, maintenance/operations and someone authorized to approve change. Minimum evidence includes failure history, design specifications, existing controls, inspection/test records and change history. Mark hypotheses as hypotheses; do not promote a plausible cause to a proven cause without verification.

Success is not the number of tree branches. It is whether bottom events gain clear ownership, whether the combined view exposes a cause or interaction that was previously obscured, and whether the resulting action can be verified with evidence.

Evidence limits and expert critique

The paper uses two illustrative cases and does not provide a large comparative experiment measuring each method's failure-discovery sensitivity. The FTA-guided FMEA method is explicitly tentative. The authors also acknowledge that a one-to-one mapping from bottom events to physical components is not always possible. Rating scales in the box-fan example are not documented in enough detail for safe reuse. The paper is therefore strongest as a reasoning and structuring framework, not as a ready-made scoring standard.

What this changes in preventive engineering practice

The combined approach creates a sequence of questions. FMEA asks what the function is and how it can fail. FTA asks which causal paths can create the top event. AFMEA adds a behavior-level challenge: could environment, interaction or use create an undesired outcome that a parts-first analysis would miss?

Used together, the methods can become a decision system: behavior analysis widens the search, the fault tree structures causal logic, and FMEA connects that logic to components, controls, actions and effectiveness evidence.

The Preventive Engineering question

Take the most consequential top event in one of your systems. Can you trace it to bottom events and then connect each event to a function, component, failure mode, current control, owner and effectiveness test — or do your fault tree and FMEA still live as separate documents?
