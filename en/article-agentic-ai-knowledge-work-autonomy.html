From Answers to Completed Work: How AI Agents Change the Workflow

Dr. Alaa Mohammad Ahmed

Ten suppliers, an approaching committee meeting and an unfinished report. AI search may find information in seconds, but someone must still complete the task. A study of Perplexity Search and Computer examines how agents take on more execution, while the Tamayuz 10X lens asks how an organization should test the value of redesigning the work.

1. A task that takes hours even when information is available

A procurement analyst must compare ten suppliers before a committee meeting: prices, payment terms, delivery times and risks. Finding supplier names is only the start. The analyst must reconcile offers, identify missing details and produce a comparison the committee can trust. This is a hypothetical illustration, not a task documented in the study. It raises a practical question: should organizations accelerate each manual step, or redesign who performs those steps?

2. Three ways to do the work

Manual workflow: The analyst searches sources, extracts figures, standardizes offers, drafts a report and checks it.

Search: Perplexity's AI search product retrieves and synthesizes information and cites sources. It may answer supplier questions quickly, while the analyst still completes the comparison.

Computer: Perplexity's agentic product can pursue a goal through multiple steps and tools, subject to access and permissions. It may collect information, structure it and draft an initial comparison. It cannot guarantee complete or accurate results: the analyst must resolve missing information, verify figures and approve the outcome. An AI agent is software that performs a sequence of actions toward a goal within defined authority.

3. The research question

If Search returns an answer in seconds while Computer runs for many minutes, comparing response times alone can be misleading. Search may stop after supplying information; Computer may continue executing steps the human would otherwise perform. The researchers therefore investigated how much execution agents take on and estimated the human work that might remain.

They analyzed real Perplexity usage and matched 10,000 pairs of sessions with nearly identical initial requests. This is an observational comparison, not randomized assignment [1][2].

Measured: Computer ran for an average of 26 minutes per session, versus 33 seconds for Search. Median machine execution was nine minutes versus 14 seconds. Longer machine runtime is neither proof of slower task completion nor a direct measure of human time saved [1][2].

Modeled: Using the researchers' tool-based model, estimated total task time was 269 minutes for Search plus human work versus 36 minutes for Computer plus remaining human work and oversight. The 233-minute difference relative to the 269-minute baseline is about 87%. The model also estimated a 94% average cost reduction. These estimates depend on assumptions about equivalent human execution and oversight; they were not obtained by timing the same employee doing the same task twice [1][2].

Experience signal: Meaningful next-turn dissatisfaction appeared in 1.3% of matched multi-turn Computer sessions versus 2.9% for Search. These are behavioral indicators defined by the study, not objective accuracy or error rates. The corresponding counts for every reported percentage are not established here [2].

Separate interview evidence: Researchers conducted 25 semi-structured interviews with active Computer users. Reported speedups varied widely, but this small, selected sample is not interchangeable with the matched-session analysis [2].

5. What the evidence cannot establish

Matching initial requests cannot eliminate differences between users or tasks. Sessions are imperfect proxies for completed work, and Computer tasks without a close Search counterpart fall outside the matched comparison. Time and cost savings depend on assumptions about human-equivalent execution and review. The data cover Perplexity usage, not an organization's full workflow. The results therefore do not establish identical savings across organizations, improved decision quality or guaranteed returns [1][2].

6. The Tamayuz 10X lens: redesign the task

The procurement example points to a broader operating-model decision. Rather than merely buying a faster assistant, an organization can map the task, delegate repeatable steps with appropriate permissions, and retain human accountability for exceptions and approvals.

Before delegation: Measure human hours, cost, missing data, errors, rework and report usability for the same ten-supplier comparison. After delegation: Measure the completed task again, separating agent runtime from human review and correction. Test whether the team can handle more work with the same resources without sacrificing quality.

Governance is part of the business case: An agent may gather offers, normalize fields and draft comparisons, while authorized staff assess supplier risk and approve purchasing decisions. Saved execution time has little value if it reappears as prolonged review or undetected mistakes.

The 10X opportunity is to rethink the division of labor across the whole task: people define goals, authority boundaries and decisions; agents take on verifiable execution. The study does not establish a universal tenfold improvement.

Evidence Pack

Matched-session counts and runtime measurements come from the technical paper and institutional research report [1][2]. The 269-to-36-minute comparison and cost estimates are model-based and subject to human-time and oversight assumptions [1][2]. Dissatisfaction is a behavioral measure, not an independent accuracy assessment [2]. The supplier scenario is illustrative rather than an observed study case.

Conclusion

The shift from search to agentic execution is not primarily about producing answers faster. It is about potentially transferring parts of a workflow from people to software. The Tamayuz 10X lens turns that possibility into a testable institutional question: measure completed work, redesign responsibilities, include verification costs and retain human decision authority.
