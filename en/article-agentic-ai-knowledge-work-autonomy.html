From 269 Minutes to 36: What Changes When an AI Agent Does the Work?

Dr. Alaa Mohammad Ahmed

The costly part of knowledge work often begins after an AI assistant answers. Someone still has to open the tools, move information between them, build the deliverable and check the result. Production data from Perplexity offers an unusually concrete look at what changes when an agent takes on more of that execution.

The operating problem: answers are not finished work

Agentic AI describes goal-directed AI systems that can take a sequence of actions—searching, browsing, writing, running code or using connected tools—to complete work. An AI agent is the software component taking those actions within defined permissions. The study contrasts Perplexity Search, which primarily retrieves and synthesizes information, with Computer, a general-purpose agent designed to plan and execute multi-step workflows [1][2].

What the researchers actually did

The analysis uses production behavior rather than a laboratory simulation. To approximate the same underlying task attempted with both products, the researchers matched 10,000 pairs of sessions whose initial requests were nearly identical. That makes the comparison grounded in real use, but it remains observational rather than a randomized experiment [1][2].

They examined autonomy, efficiency and task scope. Human counterfactual time cannot be directly observed, so the efficiency analysis triangulated three approaches: estimates based on the agent's tool actions, estimates from a large language model, and 25 semi-structured interviews with active Computer users [2].

Across the matched pairs, Computer performed an average of 26 minutes of machine execution after a request, compared with 33 seconds for Search—about 48 times as much. The medians were nine minutes and 14 seconds, roughly a 40-fold difference. These are machine-runtime measurements, not 26 minutes of human time proven to have been saved [1][2].

The tool-based efficiency model then estimated 269 minutes for a Search-plus-human workflow and 36 minutes for a Computer-plus-human workflow. The 233-minute difference divided by the 269-minute baseline is about 87%. Combining model costs with domain-specific U.S. wage data produced an estimated average cost reduction of 94%. Across 18 domains, estimated time savings ranged from 79% to 92%, and cost savings from 87% to 96% [2].

Quality signals are not accuracy

In matched multi-turn sessions, the study reports meaningful next-turn dissatisfaction in 1.3% of Computer sessions versus 2.9% for Search, a relative reduction of about 55%. When mild signals were included, the figures were 10.8% and 16.6% [2]. These are behavioral dissatisfaction signals defined by the researchers. They are not objective error rates.

That distinction matters. The published percentages do not justify saying that Computer was “98.7% accurate,” nor can a correct-versus-incorrect count be reconstructed without the corresponding denominator for each reported rate. The evidence supports a lower dissatisfaction signal in the matched sample, not a universal accuracy claim.

Side evidence — user interviews: The study conducted 25 semi-structured interviews with active Computer users. Reported speedups ranged from 5× to more than 300×, with a median of 25×. This is a small, selected interview sample and is best treated as corroborating evidence rather than the main estimate [2].

An illustrative operating scenario

Hypothetical example, not a documented case from the study: A strategy team needs a report that combines internal files and public sources, cleans a spreadsheet, calculates metrics and drafts a presentation. With a conversational assistant, an employee may obtain research quickly but still perform the cross-tool execution. With an appropriately permissioned agent, the workflow can be delegated as a sequence while the employee specifies the objective, approves access and verifies the figures, sources and final deliverable. The useful metric is not response length; it is the human work removed without weakening verification.

Where the evidence stops

The observation window was early, and early adopters skew toward AI-native users. Matching on initial requests does not create random assignment, and it omits Computer tasks with no close Search equivalent. Sessions are also an imperfect proxy for complete tasks. Most importantly, the 87% and 94% figures depend on assumptions about human-equivalent execution time and oversight time. Sensitivity analyses and alternative estimation methods point in the same direction, but the paper explicitly treats the exact magnitudes as approximate [1][2].

The researchers observe activity only inside the Perplexity ecosystem. The study therefore does not establish that every organization will realize the same savings, or that agent adoption by itself improves revenue, decision quality or organization-wide productivity.

The Tamayuz 10X decision

Measure completed work, not answer speed. Track human time before and after delegation, machine runtime, review time and rework. Separate execution authority from approval authority. An agent may gather, transform and draft while consequential approval remains human. Price verification into the business case. Execution savings are not organizational value if they reappear as review burden or undetected error. Start with a task that has a baseline and a denominator. The credible comparison is the same unit of work before and after redesign.

The 10X shift is not simply making an AI tool ten times faster. It is redesigning the division of labor: people define goals, context and authority boundaries and verify outcomes; the system absorbs more of the sequential execution.

Terms and measures at a glance

Agentic AI
Goal-directed AI that can perform multiple actions and use tools toward an outcome.
Matched pairs
Sessions with near-identical initial requests, used here to approximate the same task across two products.
Machine execution time
How long the system continues working after a request; it is not automatically equivalent to human time saved.
Relative reduction
The reduction divided by the original baseline: 233 minutes ÷ 269 minutes ≈ 87%.
Dissatisfaction signal
A behavioral indicator defined by the study; it is not an objective accuracy or error rate.

Evidence Pack

10,000 matched pairs; 26 minutes versus 33 seconds: production-data analysis reported in the technical paper and Perplexity research summary. Confidence is high in the reported sample statistic; the limitation is observational matching rather than random assignment [1][2].

269 versus 36 minutes; 87% time and 94% cost: model-based estimates using tool classification and domain wage data. Confidence is stronger in the direction than in the exact magnitude because human-equivalent time and oversight are assumed rather than directly observed [1][2].

1.3% versus 2.9%: meaningful next-turn dissatisfaction in matched multi-turn sessions. It supports the named behavioral metric only, not objective output accuracy [2].

Broader work: among 8,000 users in eight occupation clusters, 59% of Computer work crossed the inferred primary occupation versus 50% for Search. In samples of 5,000 queries per product from dual-product users, 76% of Computer queries were classified as requiring higher-order cognition versus 55% for Search. Model-based classification adds measurement noise [2].

Conclusion

The most useful finding is not that an agent “saves 87%” in every setting. It is that greater autonomy relocates human effort. When software can execute a chain of steps, the bottleneck moves from operating the workflow to specifying it, granting authority and verifying the result. Organizations seeking a 10X effect need to measure that shift at the level of the completed task, with review and authority boundaries designed in from the start.
