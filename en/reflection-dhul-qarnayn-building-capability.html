10X Reflections from the Qur’an | Surat al-Kahf

Dhul-Qarnayn: Building Capability Rather Than Dependence

What begins with divine enablement and the means to act becomes a striking Qur’anic scene of shared effort, disciplined execution, and a change in the very conditions of a recurring threat.

العربية

Methodological note: This is a contemporary reflective reading that draws lessons from the Qur’anic scene in light of meanings established by classical exegetes. It does not propose a new tafsir, nor does it claim that modern management terminology is embedded in the Qur’anic text.

Capability begins before the barrier

The episode of Dhul-Qarnayn is often remembered for the formidable barrier built between two mountain sides. Yet the Qur’an prepares the reader for that achievement much earlier. Before the construction, before the iron, and before the people ask for protection, we are told:

“Indeed, We established him upon the earth, and We gave him to everything a way. So he followed a way.”

Al-Kahf 18:84–85 — Saheeh International

Classical tafsir treats sabab here as a means, route, knowledge, or instrument by which an objective may be reached. The next verse shows Dhul-Qarnayn proceeding by a means available to him. The text therefore does not merely present a man who possesses resources; it presents a man who acts through the means God has granted.

That distinction opens a useful contemporary question. Organizations often speak of capability while listing what they possess: talented people, data, technology, budgets, partnerships, or specialist knowledge. But possession is not yet capability. The more demanding question is not “What do we have?” but “What can we reliably do with what we have?”

This is not a definition supplied by the verse. It is a modern reflection prompted by its sequence: enablement, means, and purposeful action.

A community asks for an outcome

Later, Dhul-Qarnayn reaches a people who struggle to communicate and who describe the corruption caused by Gog and Magog. Their proposal is transactional:

“They said, ‘O Dhul-Qarnayn, indeed Gog and Magog are [great] corrupters in the land. So may we assign for you an expenditure that you might make between us and them a barrier?’ He said, ‘That in which my Lord has established me is better, but assist me with strength; I will make between you and them a dam.’”

Al-Kahf 18:94–95 — Saheeh International

They are willing to pay for a result. Dhul-Qarnayn does not accept their money as the organizing principle of the solution. Instead, he asks for their strength.

The exegetical tradition is important here. Al-Tabari, Ibn Kathir, al-Qurtubi and al-Sa‘di, in different formulations, connect this assistance with labor, physical effort, tools, or the practical work required for construction. The community is therefore not absent from the making of the solution.

A contemporary leadership reflection follows: there is a profound difference between solving a problem for people and organizing a process in which their own capacity becomes part of the solution.

The hidden cost of the indispensable expert

Many institutions celebrate the person who can solve every difficult problem. When something fails, everyone calls the same expert. When a decision becomes complex, it rises to the same leader. Over time that individual becomes more capable while the surrounding system becomes more dependent.

This can look like excellence. It can also be a warning.

If the expert leaves and the same work can no longer be performed, the organization may have been resolving problems without building capability. The distinction matters because dependency can masquerade as high performance for a long time.

Dhul-Qarnayn possesses unusual enablement, yet the Qur’anic scene does not depict him as making the people irrelevant. “Assist me with strength” places their contribution inside the work itself.

From this we may reflect: capability building does not always begin by giving people something they lack. Sometimes it begins by recognizing the strength already present and organizing it toward a purpose.

From beneficiary to participant

“Bring me sheets of iron” — until, when he had leveled [them] between the two mountain walls, he said, “Blow [with bellows],” until when he had made it [like] fire, he said, “Bring me, that I may pour over it molten copper.”

Al-Kahf 18:96 — Saheeh International

The verse moves through material, sequence, instruction, effort, and execution. The people who initially presented themselves as those in need are now involved in the work.

Modern organizations often reduce capability building to training. Training can matter greatly, but capability is broader. It can include human competence, tools, information, operating processes, decision rights, governance, partnerships, and the ability to coordinate these elements under real conditions.

The crucial test is therefore not simply what was delivered. It is what became possible afterward. Did a course change what a team can perform? Did a consultant leave behind internal competence? Did a technology implementation alter the organization’s ability to decide and act? If the external expert must return to perform the same essential task indefinitely, a service may have been transferred without a durable capability being built.

When improvement is not enough

The most provocative part of the episode for a 10X reflection comes after the construction:

“So Gog and Magog were unable to pass over it, nor were they able [to effect] in it any penetration.”

Al-Kahf 18:97 — Saheeh International

The classical exegetes explain the verse in its direct sense: the barrier could neither be climbed nor breached. We should not turn that exegetical meaning into a modern management theory. Yet the resulting change invites a legitimate contemporary analogy.

Before the barrier, the community faced a recurring threat. One possible response would have been to become faster, stronger, or better organized each time the threat arrived. That would improve performance inside the existing arrangement.

The constructed barrier changed the arrangement itself.

In contemporary organizational language—not as tafsir—we might call this system re-engineering: instead of optimizing the response after the problem reaches you, redesign the conditions through which the problem reaches you.

This distinction is central to transformative thinking. Improvement asks, “How can we perform the existing process better?” Re-engineering asks, “Does the structure of the process itself need to change?”

A 10X transformation does not always mean becoming dramatically better at solving the same problem. Sometimes it means redesigning the system so that the problem no longer operates in the same way.

Capability is not an end in itself

Read as a sequence, the episode offers a striking architecture for reflection: God grants enablement; means are available; means are pursued; the strength of others is brought into the work; resources are organized; a solution is constructed; and the conditions of the threat are changed.

A disciplined contemporary formulation would be:

Enablement → means → acting through the means → involving others → organizing resources and capability → constructing the solution → changing the structure of the problem → a different outcome.

The point is not to retrofit the 10X model into the Qur’an. The point is to allow the Qur’anic scene to interrogate a common organizational mistake: treating capability building as the final destination.

Organizations do not need more capability merely to report that more people were trained or more systems installed. Capability matters because it makes new forms of value and performance possible. It should change what the organization can actually do.

Borrowed strength and internal capability

An institution can appear powerful because it is surrounded by consultants, vendors, specialists, and advanced platforms. Some of that external expertise may be indispensable. The question is what remains when the engagement ends.

Does external expertise leave the institution more capable after it departs?

If not, a successful project may still have failed as capability building. A useful question for every transformation program is therefore: What will this organization be able to do for itself at the end of this work that it cannot do for itself today?

Leadership is neither substitution nor abandonment

Participation does not mean that expertise disappears. Dhul-Qarnayn directs the work. The text repeatedly records instruction: “Bring me,” “Blow,” and again “Bring me.” His leadership and knowledge remain consequential.

This protects the idea of empowerment from a common distortion. Empowerment is not a leader withdrawing and leaving people without direction. Nor is leadership doing everything on their behalf.

Capability-building leadership asks: What can others already contribute? What must they learn? What needs coordination? What requires specialist judgment? And what should the leader do now so that the system is stronger later?

The achievement does not become self-glorification

After the work is complete and the barrier has proven formidable, Dhul-Qarnayn says:

“This is a mercy from my Lord.”

Al-Kahf 18:98 — Saheeh International

This statement completes the moral architecture of the episode. At the beginning, God says, “We established him upon the earth.” When the people offer payment, Dhul-Qarnayn says, “That in which my Lord has established me is better.” After the achievement, he calls it “a mercy from my Lord.”

Between those acknowledgments lies strenuous human action: travel, judgment, mobilization, materials, instruction, construction, and execution. The Qur’anic account neither erases human agency nor permits achievement to become a theology of the self.

For leadership, that balance is demanding: use the means fully, build seriously, lead competently—and do not confuse being enabled with being self-sufficient.

A 10X lens on the scene

The 10X framework is not a tafsir of these verses. Used carefully, however, it can help formulate practical questions prompted by them.

Capability multiplication and empowerment: the scene moves from possessing means to using them and from individual enablement to mobilizing the strength of others.

Leadership and transformation: leadership neither monopolizes the work nor abandons responsibility. It organizes contributions toward a result.

Redesign: as a contemporary analogy, the move from recurring exposure to a newly constructed barrier invites us to distinguish process improvement from structural redesign.

Value and outcomes: the significance of the work lies not merely in the volume of construction but in the changed outcome described in 18:97.

There is no need to force every dimension of a modern framework onto the Qur’anic narrative. Intellectual discipline requires using only the connections that the scene can responsibly sustain.

What keeps reaching the leader’s desk?

A revealing measure of organizational maturity may be the problems that repeatedly reach the same senior person. If the same issue arrives every week, the difficulty may not lie only with the team. The system may never have built the capability to resolve the issue where it occurs.

This changes the leadership question from “How do I solve this?” to “What capability, if built now, would mean that this problem no longer needs to be solved by me in the same way?”

Then comes the more radical question: Are we building a better capability to cope with the problem, or can we redesign the system so that the problem itself no longer behaves in the same way?

The 10X question

The next time a team brings you a difficult problem, ask three questions. What part genuinely requires my expertise? What capability could we build together through solving it? And are we merely improving our response—or is there an opportunity to change the structure that keeps producing the problem?

The deepest question may be the most personal:

What work are you still doing today because you have not yet built the capability that would allow others to do it tomorrow?

Conclusion

The Dhul-Qarnayn episode begins with enablement and means, proceeds through action and shared effort, reaches a structural intervention, and closes with humility before God. Its sequence resists two opposite errors: possessing resources without using them, and using power in a way that makes everyone else dependent.

As a contemporary reflection, its challenge can be stated simply:

Do not confuse resources with capability.
Do not confuse solving for others with strengthening them.
Do not assume every recurring problem needs a better response; sometimes the system itself needs redesign.
And when achievement comes, do not forget the One from whom enablement came.
