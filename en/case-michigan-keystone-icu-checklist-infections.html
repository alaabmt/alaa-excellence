10X Case · Healthcare

How Did Michigan’s ICUs Cut an Infection Doctors Had Accepted as Inevitable?

In intensive care, bloodstream infections from central lines were treated as a routine complication. Then 103 units, most of them in the US state of Michigan, agreed on five well-known practices and gave nurses the authority to stop a line insertion if a step was missed. Infection rates fell by up to 66% within 18 months and were still low three years later.

Executive Summary

Many intensive care patients need a central venous catheter, or central line: a thin tube placed in a large vein near the heart to deliver drugs and fluids. Bacteria can travel along it into the blood and cause a serious infection. These infections were so common that clinicians regarded them as a routine complication. [6] A study by the program team, citing published estimates, put the US toll at about 82,000 infections a year in intensive care units, up to 28,000 associated deaths, and roughly $45,000 per infection. [2]

The problem was not missing knowledge. The preventive steps had been taught for years but were skipped under pressure. When nurses at Johns Hopkins Hospital watched doctors insert lines for a month, at least one step was missed for more than a third of patients. [6]

In 2003 the Michigan Health & Hospital Association asked Dr Peter Pronovost and his Johns Hopkins team to bring their approach to the state. The result was the Keystone ICU (Intensive Care Unit) project. [6] It changed who was allowed to intervene: a nurse now witnessed each insertion and could stop it if a step was missed, every unit was paired with a senior hospital executive, and infection rates were measured monthly and fed back to the teams. [5]

Across 103 units, the mean rate fell from 7.7 infections per 1,000 catheter-days (each day a patient has a line counts as one) to 1.4 after 18 months. [1] In the 90 units that kept reporting, it stood at 1.1 after 36 months. [2] But the original study compared units with their own past, not with units that did not take part, and when the program was copied in England it did no better than an improvement already under way. [8]

The 10X lesson is that Michigan introduced no new treatment. It broke the assumption that this harm was the unavoidable price of intensive care, and moved prevention from the vigilance of individual doctors to a system shared by the unit, its leaders and its data.

An infection treated as part of the treatment

In 2001 Pronovost wrote the steps for inserting a central line on a single sheet of paper. He then persuaded his hospital to let nurses stop a doctor who skipped a step, and to ask every day whether each line was still needed. The share of lines infected within ten days fell from 11% to zero. [6] Many doctors were unconvinced. The result came from one well-resourced hospital, with the checklist’s author walking its corridors. [6] Would it hold in hospitals overwhelmed with patients, such as a Detroit hospital that had cut a third of its staff between 2000 and 2003? [6]

The 10X Move

Prevention used to depend on a doctor’s memory; when it failed, the failure was put down to individual lapse or bad luck. Keystone made it a team responsibility. The checklist made the minimum standard explicit. A nurse witnessed the procedure and could halt it. One cart held every item needed. Senior executives cleared obstacles. Data showed each unit where it stood. The question shifted from “Who made the mistake?” to “How do we design the work so the mistake is hard to make?” [5][6]

From one hospital to a whole state

  1. 2001: Pronovost introduces the central-line checklist in the Johns Hopkins intensive care unit. [6]
  2. 2003: The Michigan hospital association asks for the checklists to be used statewide. The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ), the federal agency that funds research on care quality, pays for the first phase, from October 2003 to September 2005. [2][6]
  3. 2004: Conference calls with hospitals begin. In the first month, executives find that chlorhexidine antiseptic is stocked in fewer than a third of the units; within weeks, every unit has it. [6]
  4. December 2006: First results are published in the New England Journal of Medicine. [1]
  5. After publication: The federal Office for Human Research Protections (OHRP) halts data collection, ruling that the project was research requiring ethics board approval and consent from patients and clinicians. [10]
  6. 2009–2011: Forty-four states begin a national version of the program, covering more than 1,000 hospitals and 1,800 units. [7]
  7. 2010: A follow-up study shows the reduction held for 36 months. [2]

Who sees the data, who decides, who acts?

To join, each hospital had to send a letter of commitment signed by a senior executive, and form a team that included that executive, the unit director, the nurse manager, and a doctor and a nurse who would each give 20% of their time to the project. [2] The project used five practices recommended by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention: hand washing, full sterile barrier precautions, cleaning the skin with chlorhexidine, avoiding the groin vein where possible, and removing lines that were no longer needed. [2]

Around these practices sat the Comprehensive Unit-based Safety Program (CUSP), which taught staff why errors happen, asked each unit to learn from one defect a month, and tied the team to its executive partner. [5] Units designed their own checklists, as long as the five principles were kept, and set up a cart holding everything needed for an insertion. [5]

A nurse witnessed every insertion, recorded whether the steps were followed and stopped the procedure if they were not. [5] Data were collected centrally. Each hospital received a report comparing its rate with anonymized rates for the others; nothing was published, to protect the teams’ trust. [5] When a unit fell behind on reporting, program leaders called its chief executive. [5] The insurer Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan paid participating hospitals small bonuses. [6]

The evidence and its limits

In the first study, the median rate fell from 2.7 to zero within just three months. [1] A later comparison of patients covered by Medicare, the US government health insurance for people aged 65 and over, found that the odds of death fell more in 95 Michigan hospitals than in 364 hospitals in neighboring states; there was no clear difference in length of stay. [3] In six hospitals, the program was estimated to prevent about 30 bloodstream infections and 18 cases of ventilator-associated pneumonia per hospital each year, at a cost of $3,375 per infection averted in 2007 dollars, against $12,208 to $56,167 for each infection. [4]

The first study compared units with their own earlier rates rather than with units outside the program. Of the 103 units, 48 had no baseline data, 40 of them because they started the intervention during the baseline period. [2] Infection rates were reported by the hospitals themselves, the mortality and cost studies came from the same academic group, and costs were measured in only six hospitals. [3][4] The team acknowledges that it did not formally evaluate what sustained the results, and that the effect of the insurer’s incentive payments is unknown. [2]

The 10X Lens

Redefining value

The infection became preventable harm rather than an expected complication, and zero became a realistic target. [5][7]

Leadership and governance

Each team had an executive who visited and removed obstacles, such as missing antiseptic and sterile drapes. [6]

Reinventing the operating model

Nurses gained the authority to stop a procedure, supplies were gathered on one cart, and removing lines became a daily question. [5][6]

Institutional intelligence

Every unit saw its own rate each month alongside others’, which turned data into a reason to act. [5]

From future to impact

The desired future was intensive care in which line infections were no longer accepted as fate. That required an explicit minimum standard, someone with authority to stop an error, leaders who removed obstacles, and shared measurement. Michigan built these in a network of units that learned together, and infections fell and stayed down. [1][2][5]

Insider Critique and Evidence Limits

The project’s leaders would probably object to the story being told as “a checklist.” An analysis co-written by Pronovost argues that the checklist exposed the gap between actual and expected practice and changed the nurse’s role, and that the project relied on real pressure, such as comparing rates and chasing units that failed to report. [5]

England shows how hard the model is to transfer. In “Matching Michigan,” which covered 215 English units, the mean infection rate in adult units fell from 3.7 to 1.48 per 1,000 catheter-days, but the decline did not speed up after training, and the researchers concluded it was probably part of a wider improvement in infection control. [8] A field study found that only one of 17 units transformed its practice and culture, while 11 changed little. The program was not an exact copy of the original, and staff saw national infection policies as imposed and punitive. [9]

What can be said with confidence is that line infections fell sharply in participating units and stayed low for three years. What cannot be established is how much of that was due to the program alone rather than the wider improvement and financial incentives.

Practical use

Find a practice everyone knows and many skip under pressure. Write down its minimum standard, give the person who witnesses the work the right to stop it, pair the team with a leader who can remove obstacles, and return its data every month. Before you start, make sure staff see the problem as their own, not as something imposed on them.

Transferability

Michigan had a specific, measurable problem, clear evidence, a hospital association linking units into one network, and a research team following every unit. [5] England suggests that copying the tools without those conditions is not enough. [9] Still, the idea travels to any setting where a harm with a known cause keeps recurring, in healthcare, manufacturing or services.

A 10X Question for the Future

If, ten years from now, we want an organization in which no recurring harm is accepted as inevitable, who will have the authority to stop the work when a step is missed? What data will every team see each month? And if that is the future we want, which roles, powers and skills must we start building now, so that prevention no longer depends on individual memory and vigilance?
