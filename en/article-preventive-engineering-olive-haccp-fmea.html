Does FMEA Show a Spanish Olive Plant Something a Simple Risk Matrix Misses?

An olive plant in Seville has to decide which hazards deserve the strictest control. Glass splinters, pesticides, and bacteria all compete for attention. European law requires the plant to run a food safety system called HACCP (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points), which finds food safety hazards and controls them. The law does not say how to score a hazard.

Spanish researchers compared two scoring tools at this plant. One is a simple matrix that combines two scores. The other is Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA), which adds a third score for how well a problem can be detected. Both tools flagged the same important hazards. But the third score gave glass splinters after labeling a value of 75, against 15 for most of the hazards shown. The study did not measure any result after the plant acted.

What problem did the plant face?

The plant is a medium-sized company in Seville that makes two kinds of table olives: green olives in the Spanish style and black olives in the Californian style. It exports much of its output to European countries. [1] The study reports that the European Rapid Alert System for Food and Feed recorded 26 notifications about table olives between 2020 and 2025. The most frequent problems were pesticide residues, heavy metals, microorganisms, and additives. [1]

The US Food and Drug Administration describes HACCP as “a systematic approach to the identification, evaluation, and control of food safety hazards.” [2] One of its outputs is the critical control point (CCP), a step where control can be applied and is essential to prevent a hazard or reduce it to an acceptable level. A hazard that needs no critical point is handled by prerequisite programs (PRPs), the good manufacturing and hygiene procedures that support the system. [2]

The study notes that laws and voluntary standards do not say how to choose a risk scoring tool, so the final ranking can depend on who does the scoring. [1] Five researchers from the International University of La Rioja and the Spanish National Research Council wrote the study.

How did the researchers compare the two tools?

The first tool is a 4×4 matrix that scores two things: how likely a hazard is and how severe it would be. The second is FMEA, which adds a third score. [1]

The three scores are known as the risk triad (Severity, Occurrence, Detection — S/O/D). Severity is how bad the harm would be. Occurrence is how likely the hazard is. Detection is how well the existing controls would catch it. Multiplying the three gives the Criticality Index (CI). A hazard counts as significant if its CI is 25 or higher, or if its severity is 4 or 5. [1]

Three experts scored the hazards together and agreed on each score by discussion, using the literature, legislation, sector guidelines, and historical data. The study says the scores are estimated categories, not measured frequencies. [1]

What did the study find?

Both tools flagged the same important hazards: biological hazards in fermentation, brine, storage, sealing, and heat treatment; foreign bodies; and chemical hazards such as heavy metals, pesticides, and mycotoxins. [1]

In the first four rows the scores were 5, 3, and 1, so the index was 15. In the last row only the Detection score changed, from 1 to 5, and the index rose to 75. The matrix rated this hazard “Major,” like the others, and did not set it apart. The study links the value to the severity of harm and to the X-ray detector sitting earlier in the line, so nothing after labeling would catch a splinter. [1]

Why did the two tools agree?

All 24 rows shown have a severity of 5. The index is 15 in 21 rows, 10 in two, and 75 in one. The study’s rule treats any hazard with severity 5 as significant, whatever its index, so these hazards counted whether or not they passed the cutoff of 25. The authors admit that this may reduce the influence of Detection and helped the two tools agree. [1]

The researchers concluded that the matrix suits early system building, long flow charts, and limited historical data, while FMEA suits checking an established system and supporting improvement. [1]

What decisions came out of the ranking?

The researchers applied the decision tree of the Codex Alimentarius, the international reference for food standards, to the significant hazards. On the green-olive line it produced nine critical control points: seven biological stages, from fermentation to pasteurization, plus metal detection and X-ray detection. The remaining hazards went to stricter prerequisite programs, such as supplier approval, preventive maintenance, and water control. [1]

On the black-olive line it found four critical points, including sterilization at about 121 °C for more than 15 minutes. The study does not report whether the plant carried out these decisions, or what followed. [1]

What do we not know?

The result rests on one plant with no measurement after the decisions. We label it a limited trial. [1]

  • Three experts agreed the scores from the literature and legislation, and no second team tested whether they would score the same way. Nothing was measured on the production line.
  • The two tools may agree partly because of the severity-5 rule, not because they are equivalent.
  • We show five of the 24 rows listed in Table 2. The other stages and the full scales are in the supplement, which we did not open.
  • The study does not say whether the plant acted on the glass-splinter risk after labeling.

How can you start a small pilot of your own?

  • Pick one production line, draw its stages, and list the existing controls at each one.
  • Have the same team score ten stages with both tools, and record where they differ.
  • Write down the severity rule and the cutoff before scoring, so they do not decide the result alone.
  • For each severe hazard, ask where the last detection control sits and whether a new step comes after it.

What does this case add to the 10X view?

The case does not show that the product became safer. It does show that a Detection score can expose a gap that a simple matrix does not: a step that comes after the last control.

What is your preventive engineering question?

On your production line, where is the last point at which the most serious hazard can still be detected? Does a step come after it that nobody watches?
