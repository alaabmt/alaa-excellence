What Happened When Hospital Teams Started Holding Safety Huddles?

Dr. Alaa Mohamed Ahmed

4 November 2026 · 7 min read

Hospital staff may notice a risk to a patient but have no obvious time or place to raise it. Many hospitals now hold a safety huddle: a short, structured meeting, often daily, where staff name what could harm patients and agree how to prevent it. In a trial in four academic hospitals, the rate of adverse events fell by 12% after doctors started these huddles. An adverse event is harm caused by care itself, not by illness. Yet other studies show that many huddles are not run the way they were designed.

Back to articles

This article draws on five studies published from 2021 to 2026: a trial in four academic hospitals (2025), a project across 92 wards in the UK (2021), a survey answered by UK intensive care units (2026), and two studies from South Korea (2023) and Taiwan (2022). All of them come from hospitals. Any point about other kinds of organizations is the author's inference.

What changed when doctors began holding safety huddles?

Researchers from Toronto, Canada, ran a trial in four academic hospitals in which doctors held a short, structured meeting about patient safety. In each huddle, doctors raised risks that could harm their patients and agreed what they would do to prevent them. [1]

The study was a stepped-wedge cluster randomized trial: the four hospitals started the huddles one after another, in an order chosen at random, and each kept them going for at least nine months. Reviewers then checked the records of 912 randomly selected patients, without knowing whether each patient was treated before or after the huddles began, so that this would not bias their judgment. [1]

The rate of adverse events fell by 12% after the huddles started. Doctors also rated their hospital's safety culture more positively. Safety culture here means how openly staff discuss mistakes and learn from them. On the survey's measure of communication about error, positive answers rose from 36% to 64%. [1]

But not every doctor attended: attendance ranged from 30% to 85% across hospitals. The average numbers of issues raised and actions taken per huddle both fell between 1.6 and 3.1. [1]

Why is holding a huddle not enough?

In the UK, a project tried to spread safety huddles to 92 wards in five hospitals, but the huddles were sustained on only 64 of those wards. Researchers also looked at fidelity, meaning how closely the huddles followed their design. Observed huddles included, on average, 4.9 of their 9 intended elements. [2]

Huddles were usually a place where people could speak without fear. But teams often left out two things: a chart showing harm over time, and a review of harm that had already happened. Even so, staff rated teamwork and safety culture more positively than before on 26 of 27 survey questions. [2]

In a survey published in 2026, 74 UK intensive care units responded, and 71 of them said they held safety huddles. Among units that answered the attendance question, 89% said nurses attended regularly, compared with only 32% for doctors. Most units did not follow a set format backed by research. [3]

Units also listed drawbacks: huddles take time, can seem pointless, can interrupt patient care, and may lack psychological safety. Psychological safety is the sense among staff that they can speak up about a mistake or a risk without fear. [3]

What changed on other wards?

In a teaching hospital in Seoul, South Korea, four surgical wards held safety huddles for 12 weeks. Huddle leaders first attended training workshops, then received coaching visits during the 12 weeks. Researchers compared survey responses from 89 nurses before and after the huddles and found improvement in 6 of the 9 areas they measured. The nine areas included speaking up about risks, mutual support among colleagues, job satisfaction, and quality of care. [4]

In interviews with the researchers, nurses described benefits of the huddles, but also difficulties in attending them. [4]

On a 74-bed medical ward in Taiwan, a multidisciplinary team started meeting twice a week, and 44 huddles were held over about a year. The team raised 81 problems and resolved all of them. Of these, 67 were "quick hits," defined as problems resolved within two days; on average, they took about 5 hours. The other 14 were more complex and took about 20 days on average. [5]

What can leaders do?

  1. Give the huddle one clear purpose: name what could harm patients today and agree how to prevent it. The huddles in the Toronto trial were built around this purpose. [1]
  2. Make sure doctors and other professional groups attend, not only nurses. In UK intensive care units, doctors attended far less often than nurses. [3]
  3. Follow up on every agreed action until it is done, then tell the team what was done. On the Taiwanese ward, every problem raised was resolved, most of them within hours. [5]
  4. Show the team harm data regularly. This was one of the elements most often left out in the UK project. [2]
  5. Measure two things: whether the huddle runs in full, as planned, and whether harm goes down. A huddle that skips its key elements may change nothing. [1][2] This is the author's inference from these two studies.
  6. Look for other teams in your organization that could benefit from a short risk huddle, such as operations, maintenance, or customer service. The studies did not test this; it is the author's suggestion.

Limits of the evidence

The Toronto trial took place in only four academic hospitals and included only doctors. Adverse events were measured by reviewing the records of a sample of patients. [1] The UK project measured how staff saw safety culture, not harm itself. [2] The UK intensive care survey describes what units do, but does not measure the effect of huddles. [3]

The Korean study compared results before and after the huddles but had no comparison wards, so it is not known whether results would have improved anyway. It relied on nurses' survey answers and interviews. [4] The Taiwanese study described a single ward, with no before-and-after comparison and no comparison wards, and assessed the huddles through a staff questionnaire. [5]

None of the five studies took place in an Arab hospital, and none tested the idea outside hospitals. Applying these findings to Arab hospitals or to other kinds of organizations is a reasonable inference, but it has not been tested.

The 10X lens

A huddle does not reduce harm just by taking place. Rather, it is likely to help when the people who can make decisions attend, when risks can be raised without fear, and when agreed actions are carried out. An organization that holds huddles regularly without these elements may gain a standing meeting, but not necessarily safer care. The real change begins when the huddle becomes the place where risks surface early, each risk gets an agreed action, and the whole team knows whether it was done.

Key terms

Safety huddle
A short, structured meeting, often held daily, in which a team discusses risks that could harm patients and agrees how to prevent them.
Adverse event
Harm caused by care itself, not by the patient's illness.
Fidelity
How closely the huddles are run as designed, with all of their elements.
Safety culture
How staff see safety at work: do they talk about mistakes, and do they learn from them?
Psychological safety
The sense among staff that they can speak up about a mistake or a risk without fear.
Stepped-wedge cluster randomized trial
A trial in which whole units, such as hospitals (clusters), start the new practice one after another, in an order chosen at random.

The question that remains

Three years from now, what do you want your team's huddle to be: a routine people attend and forget, or the place where risks are caught early and the team agrees how to prevent them? If you want the second, who needs to be in your huddle tomorrow, and how will you know that what was agreed was done?
