Is the Bigger Risk at a Medical Simulation Center a Failure That Stops the Session or One Nobody Notices?

At a medical school’s simulation center in Barbados, the video capture system failed on July 8, 2024, during a practical clinical exam. It recorded no picture and no sound. The exam ended without anyone noticing, and only later did staff learn that the single record of the student’s performance did not exist.

The center’s team analyzed 22 logged equipment incidents from 24 months, using failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA). The silent recording failure ranked first, with a risk priority number (RPN) of 378. A total manikin shutdown scored only 140, although it has the higher Severity score and happened more often. RPN scores run from 1 to 1,000. The team plans a post-session check that a recording exists. The study has not yet measured the effect of any fix, and its sample is small.

What problem did the center face?

The simulation institute at Ross University School of Medicine in Barbados trains about 1,000 students a year. All of its early, pre-clinical teaching rests on simulation. It has five lifelike training manikins, nine heart-and-lung patient simulators, and 25 audiovisual rooms. All of them connect to a video capture platform from an outside vendor. [1]

On July 8, 2024, capture stopped during an Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE), a practical exam in which students perform clinical tasks. The exam ran to the end without anyone noticing. Later, staff found that the only record of the student’s performance was missing. Graders and instructors use that record to review grades and to debrief, meaning to go over a session afterward. [1]

Technicians fixed each fault as it happened, but nobody saw all incidents together. A manikin shutdown halts the room and cannot be missed. A capture failure can stay hidden until someone asks for the footage. [1]

How did the team analyze the failures?

The team reviewed the incident log kept by the operations staff, from June 2024 to May 2026. It holds 22 incidents, which the authors later coded into nine failure modes, meaning the ways the equipment can fail. [1]

First came a Pareto analysis, which ranks categories by the number of incidents. Audiovisual and network problems made up seven incidents (31.8%), and manikin power or mechanical failures made up six (27.3%). Together they made up 13 incidents (59.1%). The team then asked “why?” five times about each group. Causes included instructor workstations nearly eight years old, no pre-session self-test, and cables in busy areas. The authors drew this from incident narratives, not a staff workshop. [1]

Next, the team rated each failure mode on three scales, known together as S/O/D. Severity is how serious the effect is. Occurrence is how often it happens. Detection is how hard it is to notice. Each runs from 1 to 10, and the product, the RPN, can reach 1,000. [1]

Institute leadership set the Severity anchors by consequence. A postponed or disrupted session scored 10, a lost OSCE record 9, a lost training recording 8, and everything else 6. The original protocol set Severity by equipment category. The team changed this to consequence during analysis, because the same fault could matter differently at different times. Occurrence came from the number of times a failure happened. Detection came from how a failure usually comes to light. A failure that stops the session scored 2, because everyone sees it. A silent failure found only on request scored 7. [1]

Manikin shutdown was the most frequent failure (four times) and had the highest Severity score (10). Yet it ranked fifth of nine, at 140. Capture failure during an OSCE happened three times with Severity 9, and it ranked first at 378. The whole gap comes from Detection. A shutdown scored 2, because everyone sees it at once. A capture failure scored 7, because it is found only when someone requests the recording. [1]

The study shows that shutdown scores higher on frequency and on Severity. Severity times Occurrence is 10 × 7 = 70 for a shutdown and 9 × 6 = 54 for the capture failure. The order reverses only when Detection is added. [1]

Which of FMEA’s nine stages does the study show?

FMEA is usually presented in nine stages. The mapping below is ours, not the study’s.

What the study shows, mapped to the nine stages (the mapping is ours)
StageWhat the study shows
1. ScopeThe equipment of one simulation center over 24 months.
2. Components and tasksManikins, simulators, audiovisual rooms, and the capture platform.
3. Failure modes, effects, causesNine failure modes, and root causes for the two most frequent groups.
4. Current prevention and detectionA scheduled vendor service contract and in-house manikin repair.
5. Risk evaluationThree scores from 1 to 10, with Severity anchors set by leadership by consequence.
6. PrioritiesPareto analysis, then ranking by RPN.
7. ActionsOne recommended action per failure mode, such as a checklist to confirm a recording exists after each session.
8. VerificationNot done yet; planned after a two-semester pilot.
9. Ongoing reviewMonthly review of incident counts and checklist use is planned.

The study reaches the choice of actions and stops before verifying them. So the result is a ranking of priorities. It does not measure any effect on recording quality.

What do we not know?

The result rests on one center and 22 incidents, with no measurement after any fix, so treat it as a limited local baseline, not a trial. The authors say other centers can reuse the method, but not the ranking. [1]

  • The voluntary log probably undercounts events.
  • Occurrence scores came from the same counts that produced the Pareto ranking, so the two analyses are not independent.
  • The author team scored Detection, and independent raters did not check it. The authors note that this is a known weakness of Detection scoring.
  • The two recommended fixes, a post-session recording check and cable securing, were still planned when the study was written, so their effect is unknown.

In an independent study, Shebl and colleagues asked two hospital teams to run an FMEA, then tested the results. Clinicians outside the teams found failures both had missed, and the authors questioned whether the scores should be multiplied at all. [2] So we read the score only as a way to rank priorities.

How can you start a small pilot of your own?

These steps follow the study’s advice, in our words.

  • Log every failure in one place, even a two-minute fix, with the time it was noticed and by whom.
  • Code failures into failure modes afterward, and keep apart those with different consequences.
  • The study advises agreeing on Severity anchors with leadership before scoring, and letting more than one person score Detection.
  • When a high score comes from poor Detection, first add a check that would notice the failure. Do this before trying to prevent the failure itself.
  • After the change, measure incidents the same way, and decide who reviews the results.

What does this case add to the 10X view?

Ranking risk by severity or frequency alone can put a loud failure above a silent one that leaves no evidence at all. The proposed response is a cheap check, such as confirming that the recording exists before the room is released. It is untested, and 22 incidents are too few to generalize.

What is your preventive engineering question?

In your work, which failure goes unnoticed until someone needs its result? What verification step would catch it before then?
