From a Hospital Patients Travel To, to a Network That Brings Expertise to Them: How Seha Virtual Hospital Redesigned Where Care Happens

What changes when specialist expertise no longer has to remain where the specialist is physically located? Seha Virtual Hospital in Riyadh points to a transformation deeper than video consultation: specialist capability can be organised as a networked resource rather than a site-bound one.

Executive Snapshot

Seha Virtual Hospital was established as a national virtual-care model connecting specialist expertise with healthcare facilities at a distance. Its strategic significance is not that an appointment can happen on a screen. It is that parts of specialist capability can move across geography while examination, intervention and continuity of care remain appropriately anchored in local facilities.

The evidence demonstrates substantial operational expansion and adoption. It does not yet establish, independently and over the long term, that the model improves clinical outcomes, system economics, equity or workforce outcomes. That distinction is central to the case.[5]

The Challenge: Expertise Exists, but Geography Controls Access

Highly specialised services are often concentrated in major centres. In the conventional model, the location of expertise becomes part of the service design: patients travel, are referred onward, or are transferred between facilities. The constraint is therefore not only whether knowledge exists. It is whether the patient and the expertise can meet in the same place at the required time.

A superficial digital response adds video to the existing pathway. A deeper redesign asks which parts of specialist judgement genuinely require physical co-location, which can travel digitally, what capability must remain local, and what information must move reliably for a safe decision.

The 10X Move: Redesign the Location of Capability, Not Just the Appointment Channel

Viewed through a Tamayuz 10X lens, Seha Virtual Hospital can be understood as a shift from a hospital defined mainly by its site to an expertise network. Specialists, local clinical teams, digital platforms, clinical data and referral pathways combine so that specialist input can be orchestrated across distance. The operating question changes from “Where is the specialist?” to “How can the right capability reach the right case at the right time?”

Evidence of Impact: What Has Actually Changed?

Operational use expanded

In January 2026, the Saudi Ministry of Health reported more than 16 million virtual appointments and medical consultations during 2025. This included 11.5 million virtual-clinic appointments, 56% growth over 2024, and more than 220,000 clinical cases, up 49% year on year.[1] These are official operational figures, not an independent impact evaluation.

The network widened

A June 2026 health transformation update reported that Seha Virtual Hospital had served more than 365,000 beneficiaries through 241 connected healthcare facilities.[2] An official August update described a wider footprint of 241 hospitals, more than 1,400 health centres and 114 primary and subspecialties.[3] The figures use different scopes or measurement points and should not be collapsed into a single network denominator.

Specific services scaled rapidly

A peer-reviewed retrospective analysis of Seha Virtual Hospital data reported stroke cases increasing from 41 in 2022 to 4,393 in 2024, oncology cases from 24 to 714, and outpatient visits from 1,717 in 2021 to 27,896 in 2024. Patient satisfaction was 86% in 2022 and 2024 and 82% in 2023.[4] The study supports a conclusion of scale and adoption; it does not, by itself, establish causal improvement in clinical outcomes.

Evidence boundary: A 2026 academic review of national virtual hospitals concluded that current evidence, including the Seha model, remains largely descriptive. Independent evidence on long-term clinical, economic, equity and workforce outcomes is still limited.[5]

The Tamayuz 10X Lens

Redefining Value and Strategic Direction

Value moves beyond offering a digital appointment toward making scarce specialist capability accessible to patients and local facilities regardless of where that expertise is physically based.

Multiplying Capability and Empowerment

Scarce expertise can serve a wider network when virtual-care skills, local clinical capability and clear workflows are built around it.

Reinventing the Operating Model

The hospital functions not only as a destination but as an expertise hub coordinating care with geographically distributed facilities.

Maximising Organisational and Technological Intelligence

The digital model creates more value when the relevant images, measurements and clinical information move with the consultation rather than when communication alone is digitised.

Maximising Sustainable Value and Results

Scale and access matter, but mature evaluation must also cover clinical quality, total cost, equity and sustainable experience. Evidence in these areas remains incomplete.

Practical Application: Separate Expertise From Location

When scarce capability is concentrated in one place, the first response does not always have to be another facility or more experts at every site. Break the service apart. Which activities require the expert to be physically present? Which decisions, interpretations or follow-ups can travel? What capability must remain local? What data must reach the expert for the decision to be safe?

This turns digitalisation from a communication channel into capability redesign. It also demands better measures than contact volume: time to specialist input, decision quality, safely avoided transfers, clinical outcomes, total cost and equity of access.

Transferability

The same logic can apply wherever scarce expertise can be separated, in part, from location: engineering consultation, cybersecurity, audit, government centres of expertise and advanced technical support. A communications platform is not enough. Roles, information, decisions and accountability must be redesigned together.

If your organisation’s best expertise is concentrated in one place, do you really need to replicate the expert at every location — or can you redesign the work so expertise reaches the point of need without losing quality or accountability?
