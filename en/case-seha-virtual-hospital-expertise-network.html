From Moving the Patient to Moving the Expertise: How Seha Virtual Hospital Redesigned Access to Specialist Care

Specialist healthcare has traditionally been tied to place. Seha Virtual Hospital represents a different operating model: specialist knowledge, clinical decisions, diagnostic support and selected forms of intervention can reach healthcare facilities without requiring every specialist to be physically present at every location.

Executive Snapshot

Specialist healthcare has traditionally been tied to place. The more specialized the expertise a patient needs, the more likely the patient is to travel to the hospital or referral center where that expertise resides.

Saudi Arabia’s Seha Virtual Hospital (SVH) represents a different operating model. Its significance goes beyond replacing an in-person appointment with a video consultation. It is building a national network through which specialist knowledge, clinical decisions, diagnostic support, and selected forms of intervention can reach healthcare facilities without requiring every specialist to be physically present at every location.

The Saudi Ministry of Health currently reports that SVH supports more than 242 hospitals, spanning 48 main specialties and 68 subspecialties.[1] In 2025, the Ministry reported more than 16 million virtual appointments and medical consultations, including more than 11.5 million completed virtual-clinic appointments, alongside more than 220,000 clinical cases.[2]

The breakthrough is not putting the consultation on a screen. It is separating access to specialist expertise from the requirement for the patient and the specialist to be in the same place.

Those numbers establish operating scale. They do not, by themselves, establish better health outcomes. Recent peer-reviewed analysis reaches a similar distinction: SVH demonstrates substantial national-scale operational feasibility, while independent comparative evidence on long-term clinical outcomes, cost-effectiveness, equity, and workforce effects remains limited.[4]

The Challenge: Expertise Exists, but Access to It Is Uneven

A health system can employ highly capable specialists and still have an access problem. Expertise may be concentrated in major medical centers. A complex case elsewhere may require a referral, patient transfer, travel to another city, a wait for a specialist, or reliance on whatever capability happens to be available locally.

This is not simply a communications problem. It is a capability-distribution problem. When specialist expertise remains structurally attached to the building in which the specialist works, expanding access often means either moving patients toward expertise or duplicating scarce capability across more locations.

A networked model poses a different question: Does the specialist have to be physically present in every hospital for that hospital to benefit from the specialist’s expertise?

The Future State: From a Network of Hospitals to a Network of Capabilities

Seha Virtual Hospital was established in 2022 as part of Saudi Arabia’s healthcare transformation. Its operating model connects geographically distributed healthcare facilities with specialist virtual services and clinical expertise.[1][4]

The model extends beyond conventional video appointments. Published descriptions include specialist clinics, urgent and critical-care support, multidisciplinary committees, diagnostic support, and other coordinated virtual-care pathways.[4]

Distributed healthcare facilities → shared clinical and digital network → accessible specialist expertise → decision or clinical support at the point of need

Place does not disappear from healthcare. Many examinations, procedures, and interventions still require physical presence. What changes is the degree to which access to expertise must depend on geography.

The Capabilities Behind the Model

Connecting Facilities, Not Just Digitizing Appointments

The Ministry of Health currently reports that SVH supports more than 242 hospitals.[1] The strategic importance of that number is not simply network size. Connected facilities can potentially draw on capabilities that do not need to be permanently replicated within each institution.

Expanding the Reach of Specialist Expertise

The current Ministry description covers 48 main specialties and 68 subspecialties.[1] As more specialties can operate through clinically appropriate virtual pathways, the system increases the range of situations in which the location of expertise and the location of care can differ.

Embedding Digital Connectivity in Clinical Work

A video connection alone does not constitute an operating-model transformation. The value increases when connectivity becomes part of an actual clinical pathway: information reaches the appropriate specialist, expertise enters diagnosis or consultation, decisions can be coordinated across institutions, and local teams can act on that expertise. At that point, connectivity becomes operational capability.

The 10X Move: From Moving the Case to Calling the Capability

A conventional pathway often looks like this: Specialist need → locate specialist → referral or transfer → reach specialist center → decision.

A networked pathway can, where clinically appropriate, look different: Specialist need → connect to network → call the required expertise → remote decision or support → continue care at the point of need.

This does not eliminate patient transfers. Nor should virtual care be treated as inherently superior to physical care. Instead, the model introduces another question before movement becomes the default: Which part of the expertise can move to the patient or the local clinical team?

Evidence of Impact

More Than 16 Million Appointments and Consultations: Evidence of Scale

The Ministry of Health reported more than 16 million virtual appointments and medical consultations during 2025. More than 11.5 million completed virtual-clinic appointments were included in that activity, representing a reported 56% increase over 2024. The Ministry also reported more than 220,000 clinical cases, up 49% year over year.[2]

Evidence boundary: 16 million appointments and consultations do not mean 16 million unique patients, and the figure does not independently demonstrate improved clinical outcomes. Activity measures how much the model is being used. Impact measures what changed because it was used.

What Does the Academic Evidence Show?

A retrospective study of SVH’s development found substantial growth during its early years. Among the reported changes, stroke cases increased from 41 in 2022 to 4,393 in 2024, while outpatient visits reached 27,896 in 2024. Reported outpatient satisfaction stood at 86% in 2024.[3]

The study provides useful evidence of expansion, adoption, and diversification of clinical activity. It does not provide a controlled comparison capable of establishing that SVH caused superior clinical or economic outcomes. There is another important qualification: the study is peer-reviewed, but its authors include researchers affiliated with the Saudi Ministry of Health and Seha Virtual Hospital.[3]

Where Does the Evidence Become Less Certain?

A 2026 analysis published in the Journal of Medical Internet Research (JMIR) offers an important counterweight to the scale narrative. Its assessment is that SVH demonstrates large-scale operational feasibility and rapid service expansion, while independent evidence remains limited on comparative effectiveness, cost-effectiveness, workforce effects, and equity outcomes.[4]

The ability to build and scale the model is increasingly well demonstrated. Its long-term system value still requires stronger independent evaluation.

From Moving Decisions to Moving Interventions

In August 2026, the Ministry of Health reported that Seha Virtual Hospital had completed 11 consecutive remote robotic surgeries through a national partnership. Surgical teams in Riyadh remotely performed procedures for patients receiving care in Hail and other locations.[5]

Eleven procedures are not sufficient evidence for broad claims about clinical superiority, cost-effectiveness, or large-scale safety. Their importance to this case is different. They illustrate an emerging progression: Remote information → remote consultation → remote decision → selected forms of remote intervention.

The 10X Lens

1. Future Foresight and Ambition

The case reflects a future-state view in which virtual care becomes part of healthcare’s operating architecture rather than an additional digital channel.

2. Redefining Value and Strategic Direction

Value begins to shift from locally owning every capability toward reliably accessing capability across the network.

4. Multiplying Capability and Empowerment

Connecting a local hospital to specialists, multidisciplinary teams, or diagnostic support elsewhere can expand the range of expertise the hospital can call upon without necessarily increasing its headcount.

5. Reinventing the Institution and Its Operating Model

The patient, specialist, clinical information, decision, and parts of treatment no longer have to occupy the same physical location in every pathway.

7. Maximizing Organizational and Technological Intelligence

Technology creates strategic value when the right clinical information reaches the right expertise at the right time and supports diagnosis, coordination, decision-making, and selected forms of intervention.

8. Maximizing Sustainable Value and Results

SVH currently has stronger evidence for scale and operational expansion than for every category of ultimate outcome. Measures such as time to specialist access, avoided transfers, clinical outcomes, readmissions, pathway cost, geographic equity, and specialist workload sustainability become increasingly important as the model matures.

Practical Application: Separate Scarce Capability From Its Location

The principle behind the case can travel beyond healthcare: When a scarce capability is tied to the location of an expert, identify which parts of that capability can move to the point of need instead of moving the need to the expert.

In industrial maintenance, specialist engineering expertise can reach the technician beside the equipment. In government services, scarce expertise can support multiple service locations without duplicating the same specialist team everywhere. Inside large organizations, legal, financial, cybersecurity, or technical expertise can be embedded into workflows rather than waiting for every problem to travel to a central function.

The transferable lesson is not to build a “virtual hospital” in every sector. It is to redesign access to scarce capability.

Transferability and Limits

The model becomes especially attractive when expertise is scarce, moving the beneficiary is costly or slow, sufficient information can be transferred reliably, and a capable local team can perform whatever still requires physical presence.

But separating expertise from place is not inherently better. Where direct examination is critical, data quality is inadequate, technical reliability is insufficient, risk is high, or accountability between remote and local teams is unclear, physical co-location may remain the better design. National-scale virtual care also raises questions of digital equity, governance, economic sustainability, workforce capacity, and long-term clinical effectiveness.[4]

The right question is therefore not: How many services can we make virtual? It is: Which parts of capability can we distribute without weakening the quality of the decision, the outcome, or accountability?

Where is your organization still moving the customer, employee, or problem to the expert simply because the work was designed when expertise could not travel? If that constraint no longer applies, will you add a digital channel to the old model—or redesign the work so scarce expertise reaches the point of need?
