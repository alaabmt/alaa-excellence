When Data Shows Where Time Is Lost: How a Canadian Government Organization Redesigned Employee Security Screening

The organization did not start by automating the existing steps. It used its own system records to see where applications waited, where work was repeated, and who spent time on tasks they did not need to do. Then it changed the process and measured the result.

Executive Summary

Before a new employee can work with protected government information in Canada, they must pass a personnel security screening. At one federal government organization, screening for Reliability Status, the basic level, took 30.9 days on average. A security briefing that lasts only 10 to 15 minutes was adding about 7 days, because a meeting had to be arranged between the applicant and an official. Nearly half of the applications in the first sample (48% of 273) were sent back after their first submission for corrections.[1]

A team from the Government of Canada and the University of Ottawa applied process mining (PM): analyzing the event logs that information systems already record in order to rebuild the path each application actually takes, and to measure where it waits, loops or deviates. The analysis exposed repeated resubmissions, waiting loops, and hiring managers pulled into security tasks that consumed their time.[1]

The organization then changed the briefing step twice. After the second change, the average time for the briefing stage fell from about 7 days to 45.9 hours, and the average time for the whole process fell from 30.9 to 25.8 days. The researchers estimated that the two changes together saved about 535 hours of staff time a year.[1]

The 10X point is the assumption the case breaks: that process improvement can start from how people believe the work flows. Here, the data showed where time was actually lost, and each change was measured before the next one was chosen.

The Challenge

Personnel security screening protects government information and assets. Reliability Status is a screening level that, once granted, allows access to protected government information and assets on a need-to-know basis. Official Canadian guidance states that granting Reliability Status is followed by a security briefing for the employee.[2][3]

Before the intervention, the process took 30.9 days on average. The one-to-one security briefing took only 10 to 15 minutes to deliver, yet it added about 7 days on average because of scheduling and waiting.[1]

The analysis also found that 48% of 273 applications in the first sample were rejected after their first submission because they needed correction; reasons included incomplete or reversed names and outdated identity documents.[1]

The 10X Shift

The organization moved from improving the process based on what the team believed was happening to rebuilding the actual process from data, then changing the step that created the waiting and measuring the result after the change.

What Changed in the Way the Work Is Done?

In the first intervention, one-to-one briefings were replaced with group briefings led by the security screening officer four times a week. This cut the average time for the stage from about 7 days to 4.6 days, but a large wait remained.[1]

In the second intervention, the group session was replaced by a recorded video that applicants watch in the online portal, followed by a digital confirmation that they had watched the briefing and accepted the terms of Reliability Status. The value was not the video itself, but removing the need to schedule a meeting between the applicant and a government employee.[1]

What the Evidence Shows

Evidence limits: The case covers one government organization and only Reliability Status screening, not higher clearance levels. Each intervention phase included about 190 cases. The phases also covered different time periods, so seasonal effects cannot be ruled out. Some activity durations are calculated from the time between the start of one event and the start of the next, so they mix working time and waiting time.[1]

The 10X Lens

Value and strategic direction

Value is not just completing the briefing step, but completing it without leaving the applicant waiting days for an appointment to do a short task.

Capability multiplication and empowerment

The second intervention removed the need for staff to attend every briefing, freeing their time for tasks that need professional judgment.[1]

Operating-model redesign

The organization did not simply speed up the individual appointment. It redesigned who delivers the briefing, how and when.

Institutional and technical intelligence

Process mining turned system records from a silent digital trace into a measurable picture of waiting, rework and how work is distributed.[1]

Innovation and experimentation

The team did not stop at group briefings. When measurement showed that waiting was still high, a second intervention followed and the process was measured again.[1]

Value and sustainable results

The documented impact is shorter process time and less staff time. Applying the approach elsewhere would require new measurement in each context.

What Leaders Can Use

Do not start by asking “What can we automate?” Start by asking “What is actually happening?” If your systems record when each step and status change occurs, you can map the real process and measure waiting, rework and exceptions.

Then choose an intervention aimed at the bottleneck itself, and measure again after the change. In this case, the first intervention was better than the original process but did not solve the problem well enough, and measurement showed the need for a second one.

Where the Principle Transfers

The same logic applies to services that leave a clear digital trail, such as licensing, procurement, internal requests and HR services. But process mining does not fix a process on its own. It shows what is happening; a leader and the process owner still need to explain why and decide what to change.

From Future to Impact

Desired future

A government service where no time is lost between steps unless the wait adds value or controls a risk.

Required capabilities

Reliable event logs, process analysis, operational understanding, and a process owner with the authority to change the process.

What must be built now

Data quality, a clear definition of where the process starts and ends, measures of time and cost, and governance for how the data is used.

New operating behavior

Improvement decisions built on the actual process, with results tested after implementation.

Outcomes and impact

Less waiting, better use of staff time, and faster detection of where things go wrong.

The 10X Question

If the future we want is a service where no customer or employee waits days between two steps unless it is truly necessary, what would that look like in practice? What data, skills, decision rights and data governance must we build today so that the organization sees a wait as soon as it appears, changes the way it works, and measures whether the change actually improved the result?
