AI Proposes, Experts Decide: A Human–AI Hybrid FMEA

A language model can widen the candidate set and turn technical documents into structured data. The same study draws a critical line: automated proposals are not validated risk decisions.

The most useful AI role is not owning the risk score

Meng Xu's RAMS 2025 paper tests ChatGPT inside FMEA. The proposed workflow has two broad steps: first create a system hierarchy in JSON — JavaScript Object Notation, a structured text format; then develop FMEA content for each component or sub-process in that hierarchy. [1]

This can accelerate candidate generation and keep complex hierarchies machine-readable. But the paper's more consequential finding is a boundary: generating plausible failure knowledge is not the same as assigning defensible risk ratings.

Time-specific evidence: the published experiments used ChatGPT 4o with specific prompts and datasets. They do not establish the performance of every later model or deployment. A coherent generated cause is still a hypothesis until engineering evidence supports it.

Three evaluations separate candidate generation from judgment

17 of 21reference car-seat failure modes appeared among 30 ChatGPT candidates — 81% coverage in this test.

The first evaluation compared 21 failure modes derived from car-seat customer feedback with 30 candidates generated by ChatGPT; 17 were covered. The second compared direct AI prioritization with RPN-based ordering and found inconsistency. The third removed Severity, Occurrence and Detection values from a published food-production FMEA and asked ChatGPT to fill them; the paper reports the negative correlations above and a tendency to stay in the middle of the 1–10 scale. [1]

Why structured JSON helps — and what it cannot guarantee

The paper argues that JSON preserves system hierarchy better than a flat spreadsheet and can be processed by Python or converted into CSV. That matters for large FMEA libraries: a component, its functions, failure modes, causes, effects, controls and actions can remain nested and traceable. [1]

But a structured output guarantees format, not truth. Every generated object should therefore carry knowledge status: candidate, verified, rejected or awaiting evidence.

One continuous case: powered seat adjustment

Educational reconstruction based on the paper's car-seat evaluation. “Electrical Motor Burnout in Seat Adjustment System” appears among the candidate failure modes. The stages below turn that candidate into a governed workflow. Causes, controls and ratings must come from the actual product.
1

Set the scope

What are we doing? Define what the human–AI team is analyzing.

Application: powered seat-adjustment path from user command to seat movement, with explicit motor, guide, control and interface boundaries.

Common mistake: upload many documents and let the model silently define the scope.

Output: approved boundary and source-document list.
2

Build and verify hierarchy and functions

What are we doing? Use JSON as a reviewable structure.

Application: AI drafts the hierarchy; design engineering verifies it. Required function: move the seat to the commanded position within design limits.

Common mistake: equating syntactically complete JSON with system completeness.

Output: verified component/function hierarchy.
3

Generate candidates, then classify them

What are we doing? Treat AI as a candidate generator.

Application: motor burnout appears as a candidate. The team asks whether it is best represented as a failure mode, mechanism or cause, what effect follows, and which causes are credible in the actual design.

Common mistake: import every generated line as established fact.

Output: candidates marked accepted, rejected or requiring evidence.
4

Document real prevention and detection controls

What are we doing? Tie analysis to controls that actually exist.

Application: the paper does not document this subsystem's controls. The team must retrieve protection specifications, end-of-line tests, current/motion diagnostics, warranty checks and service detection that are actually implemented.

Common mistake: allow the model to invent a plausible but nonexistent control.

Output: evidence-backed current controls.
5

Rate Severity, Occurrence and Detection

What are we doing? Keep numerical judgment calibrated to organizational evidence.

Application: S follows the approved consequence definition; O uses failure/cause data with the correct denominator; D uses demonstrated current-control capability. The paper explicitly cautions against having ChatGPT generate these values directly.

Common mistake: accept middle-range numbers because they look reasonable.

Output: traceable human-owned ratings.
6

Prioritize with the approved method

What are we doing? Convert evidence into a reproducible decision.

Application: AI can group and summarize, but Action Priority, RPN or another approved rule is applied by the team with explicit attention to severity and critical controls.

Common mistake: ask “what are the top three risks?” and accept the model's order.

Output: documented priority rationale.
7

Design treatment

What are we doing? Use AI to widen options, then engineer the selected action.

Application: the model can suggest protection, test or design alternatives; the team connects an action to a verified cause and evaluates feasibility and side effects.

Common mistake: confuse a generated action list with an approved engineering plan.

Output: approved action, owner, due date and acceptance test.
8

Verify effectiveness

What are we doing? Ask for operating evidence, not another model opinion.

Application: durability tests, failure/warranty rate and control-detection performance before and after action justify any O or D change.

Common mistake: ask ChatGPT to “re-score after mitigation” without post-action evidence.

Output: measured before/after evidence and residual risk.
9

Govern the living knowledge base

What are we doing? Keep JSON and the approved FMEA synchronized with design and field evidence.

Application: design changes, new failure patterns or control changes reopen affected nodes, with traceability for who proposed, verified and approved each change.

Common mistake: auto-update generated outputs without human review history.

Output: auditable living FMEA knowledge.

Before and after governance: the process change matters more than the prompt

No educational risk scores are invented here. The paper itself shows why numeric generation should not be the centerpiece.

The operating-model change: AI becomes a structured assistant rather than the authority that owns risk.

A pilot that tests value instead of assuming it

Choose one product family or process with an existing FMEA and historical evidence. Hide part of the known failure-mode set, ask the model to reconstruct hierarchy and candidates, then measure reference-mode recovery, expert-accepted new candidates, false or duplicate candidates, review effort and cycle-time reduction.

Do not make S/O/D generation the first success criterion. After hierarchy and candidate quality are proven, test AI support for evidence summarization or calibration questions while keeping final ratings with the team. Apply organizational data-security rules to any documents or field data sent to the model.

Evidence limits and insider critique

The 81% figure is 17 of 21 reference modes within a 30-candidate list in one test; it is not a universal accuracy estimate, and it does not by itself tell us the review cost or validity of the remaining candidates. The negative S/O/D correlations are a strong warning for that specific setup, not a blanket statement about all language models. A model's own statement that it relied on standards or “user expertise” is not auditable provenance; the source must be a document or dataset the team can inspect. Structured JSON improves consistency of representation, not causal validity.

The professional lesson

The division of labor supported by this study is simple: AI searches, structures and proposes; experts verify, rate and decide; evidence confirms or rejects. That preserves breadth and speed without handing risk authority to a model that may produce a persuasive but uncalibrated number.

The Preventive Engineering question

If you give a language model your current FMEA, can you clearly distinguish what the model proposed, what an expert verified, what data demonstrated, and who owns the final S/O/D decision?
