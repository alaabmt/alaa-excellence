From a Smelter in the UAE to a Technology Running in Bahrain: When Does Innovation Become an Exportable Capability?

It is easy for an organization to call an internal project an innovation. It is harder for that innovation to prove itself in operation, and then be chosen by another organization that builds part of its production capacity on it. This is the story of a smelting technology developed in the UAE that left its home environment to run at industrial scale in Bahrain.

OrganizationEmirates Global Aluminium (EGA)
TechnologyDX+ Ultra aluminum smelting technology
Transfer testPotline 6 at Aluminium Bahrain (Alba)
The central ideaOperational knowledge can become an exportable strategic asset

The problem is not only the metal

Aluminum smelting is an energy-intensive industry. A smelting technology is therefore judged not only by whether it can produce metal, but by how much metal each cell produces, how much energy it needs, how stable it runs, and how much space and capital the plant requires.

In simple terms: Think of a “reduction cell” as a large industrial production unit that uses electricity to separate aluminum from its raw material. The technical race is to make this unit produce more aluminum, with less energy and cost, and with stable performance.

EGA has developed its own aluminum smelting technologies in the UAE over several decades. According to its published technology record, this work, together with earlier efforts that began in 1980, cut the electricity needed to produce each tonne of aluminum by 37.5%, and its latest industrial technology, DX+ Ultra, delivers more than twice the productivity of the first in-house technology the company developed in 1990.[1]

The 10X MoveThe leap was not EGA using its technology in its own smelters. It was turning years of accumulated knowledge into a technology that another company could choose, test and operate outside the UAE.

From in-house expertise to transferable technology

In 2016, Aluminium Bahrain (Alba) selected DX+ Ultra for its Potline 6 expansion project after an international competition. It was the first time a UAE industrial company had licensed its core production technology internationally. EGA sent a team of 40 engineers and technicians to Bahrain and hosted Alba employees for training at its UAE sites.[2][3]

This detail matters: technology transfer is not the sale of an engineering drawing. Industrial knowledge includes tacit operating experience — how to control the process, how to respond to deviations and how to sustain performance. Developing people and transferring knowledge therefore become part of the technology product itself.

What is 465 kA? kA stands for kiloampere, a measure of the electric current used in the reduction cells. The original design level was 460 kA; in operation, the line proved it could run at 465 kA while keeping strong performance indicators, in other words at a higher load than the original design.[2]

What the Evidence Shows

465 kAOperating current in Alba’s performance test, above the original 460 kA design level
≈95%Current efficiency in the performance test: how effectively the current is used to produce aluminum
12.87 kWh/kgReported energy use per kg of aluminum in the performance test
424 cellsSize of Potline 6, which uses DX+ Ultra, according to technical documents

Alba’s own newsletter confirms that selected cells in Potline 6 passed their final performance tests with results above the guarantees in the technology transfer agreement, and that the line was running at 465 kA with 95% current efficiency and energy use of 12.87 kWh per kilogram of aluminum.[4] A technical paper presented at the ICSOBA conference also documents the successful performance test at 465 kA in October and November 2019, followed by a gradual increase in amperage in later operation.[5]

What do “current efficiency” and kWh/kg mean? Put simply, current efficiency tells us how much of the electric current actually turns into useful production. kWh/kg measures the electricity needed to produce one kilogram of aluminum. In an energy-intensive industry, using less electricity per unit of output while keeping operations stable has a direct economic and environmental impact.

EGA states that its technologies are licensed for more than half a million tonnes of installed smelting capacity outside the UAE.[1] In 2024, Alba and EGA signed a new technology services agreement for Potline 6 covering support, monitoring, advisory services, training and operational audits — further evidence that the technical relationship continued beyond the first installation phase.[6]

Critical note: The 37.5% cut in electricity use is a figure published by EGA about the development of its technologies over decades. It is not an independent result from Alba alone, so it should not be mixed up with the DX+ Ultra test results in Bahrain. The strength of the case comes from three kinds of evidence together: a long record of technical development, specific operating results at Alba, and published technical documents on testing and operation.

The 10X Lens: What the Case Shows About Tamayuz 10X

06

Innovation and experimentation

DX+ Ultra started with test cells in Jebel Ali before moving to large-scale industrial use. The point is not to jump from the lab to scale-up, but to build a sequence of testing, learning and proof before scaling.

04

Capability multiplication and empowerment

The technology did not travel alone; engineers’ know-how, training and operational support went with it. Human capability here is not a support activity; it is a condition for transferring the innovation successfully.

05

Operating-model redesign

When in-house expertise moves from a tool for improving one’s own plants to a technology licensed to others, the nature of value changes: the company no longer sells only its product, but starts turning its knowledge into an asset that can be transferred and marketed.

08

Value and sustainable results

The impact can be read through productivity, energy efficiency and the technology’s ability to work outside its original environment. That is stronger evidence than counting patents or initiatives.

What You Can Use

For leaders

For every successful internal innovation, ask: does the success depend on specific people, or has it become documented knowledge that can be taught and repeated? If the original team left, would the capability stay?

For organizations

Look for practices that deliver repeatable results, then turn them into “knowledge assets”: a clear standard, performance data, an operating method, training and a way to test them in a different environment. Transferability is a stronger test than the success of a single pilot.

What transfers, and what should not be copied?

What transfers

The method for building capability: accumulating knowledge, disciplined experimentation, measuring performance, documenting knowledge, training others, and then testing the solution outside its original environment before claiming it can scale.

What depends on context

The technical figures for the smelting technology are not a benchmark for organizations outside the aluminum industry. The capital investment and the engineering and regulatory environment are also specific to the sector. What transfers is the logic of building innovation, not its industrial specifications.

The Deeper Lesson

Many organizations say they “manage knowledge”, but they measure it by the number of documents or training courses. The EGA case suggests a harder test: can accumulated knowledge produce better performance, and then become a method that others can use to get results?

At that point, knowledge is no longer an archive and innovation is no longer a one-off event. Both become an organizational capability.

The 10X QuestionWhat knowledge has your organization built over years of work that is still locked in the experience of individuals or in internal procedures — knowledge that could become a documented, repeatable and transferable capability, and perhaps a new source of value?
