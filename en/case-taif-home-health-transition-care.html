From Discharge to Continuity of Care: How Armed Forces Hospitals in Taif Redesigned the Move Home

Discharge is no longer the point where the hospital’s responsibility ends. The team redesigned the pathway so that coordination with home care starts before the patient leaves, not after.

Executive Summary

For patients with chronic illness, the first days after leaving hospital are fragile: many need medicines, follow-up, education, equipment or nursing care at home. At Armed Forces Hospitals–Taif Region in Saudi Arabia, referral to home care started on average 4.3 days after discharge, leaving a gap between the end of hospital care and the start of care at home.[1]

Between September 2024 and June 2025, the hospitals redesigned how patients with chronic conditions move to home health care (HHC): care delivered in the patient’s home after discharge, when their condition allows it and their needs call for medical, nursing or rehabilitation follow-up. The changes included early electronic referral, assessment before discharge, transition coordinators, individual care plans, virtual follow-up and central coordination between teams.[1]

The average start of the referral moved from 4.3 days after discharge to 1.3 days before it. Patient satisfaction rose from 46% to 91%. The 30-day readmission rate fell from 35.8% to 7.7% in the measure the study reports.[1][2] This is a single-site quality improvement project without a control group, and the published paper gives two different baselines for readmission.

The 10X point is the assumption the case breaks: that the hospital’s job ends at discharge. When the handover is designed as part of the service, the next team knows what the patient needs before the patient leaves.

The Challenge

The first days after discharge become critical when a patient needs medicines, follow-up, education, equipment or nursing care at home. If referral starts only after the patient has left, a gap opens between the end of hospital care and the start of home care.

The baseline showed that referral to home care started, on average, 4.3 days after discharge. The team’s analysis found that 58% of process time was spent in steps that added no value, with reliance on manual referrals and uneven coordination.[1]

The Saudi Ministry of Health’s procedure states that an eligible patient should be referred to home health care 72 hours before discharge, with an initial assessment and a visit within one week of discharge.[3]

The 10X Shift

From a referral that starts after discharge and depends on manual steps, to a pathway that starts before discharge and links electronic referral, assessment, education, visit scheduling and follow-up in one flow.[1]

What the Evidence Shows

+4.3 → −1.3 daysStart of referral relative to the day of discharge

The average moved from 4.3 days after discharge to 1.3 days before it.[1]

46% → 91%Patient satisfaction with the care transition

Measured with the Care Transitions Measure-15 (CTM-15), a 15-item questionnaire on how prepared patients feel for discharge, how well they understand their care plan, and follow-up.[1]

35.8% → 7.7%30-day readmission

The study reports a large drop between baseline and project end, but a before-and-after design cannot prove causation on its own.[1][2]

On-time start of home care improved from 51% to 89.4% for high-priority cases and from 71% to 92% for low-priority cases.[1][2]

The researchers estimated total savings of SAR 4,960,536 to 4,982,136. This is an economic estimate based on assumed costs of avoided bed days and visits, not an audited financial saving.[1]

Evidence limits: The project is a quality improvement study in one setting without a randomized control group, and it included 170 patients. Its authors work for the organization that ran it. The published text also contains an inconsistency: the background section gives a readmission rate of 13.6%, while the results table and abstract use 35.8% as the baseline. The readmission result is therefore shown as reported in the results table, with this inconsistency noted.[1]

The 10X Lens

Value and strategic direction

Value is no longer completing the discharge procedure, but making sure the patient moves to the next stage of care without an unnecessary gap.[1]

Capability multiplication and empowerment

The team created a transition coordinator role and used a checklist setting out what must be completed before discharge.[1][4]

Operating-model redesign

The home care assessment moved from after discharge to before it, and the hospital and the home care team were reconnected in a single pathway.[1]

Innovation and experimentation

The intervention combined electronic referral, virtual follow-up and “teach-back” education, in which patients explain back what they have learned, while tracking measures and adjusting the work.[1]

Institutional and technical intelligence

The electronic referral system recorded times and flagged eligible patients early, and performance dashboards and monthly reviews made it possible to track the pathway after implementation.[1]

Value and sustainable results

Measures of timing, satisfaction and care start improved, and the project recorded fewer readmissions, with controls for sustainability such as monthly audits and ongoing training.[1]

What Leaders Can Use

Do not start with “How can we speed up discharge?” Start with: when does the care that needs our coordination actually end? Discharge can be complete in the system while the patient is still waiting for a visit, education or follow-up.

Give every transition an owner who sees the pathway from start to finish. Measure the gaps between steps, not only the time each department takes. Make early referral, assessment, education and follow-up parts of a single pathway that can be seen and measured.

Where the Principle Transfers

The principle applies wherever responsibility for a person passes from one team to another: from the emergency department to a ward, from hospital to rehabilitation, from healthcare to social services, or between government service units. The handover point should be designed as part of the service, not left as a gap between two organizations.

The numbers themselves do not transfer from this study; the results depend on a specific patient group, health system and operating context.

From Future to Impact

Desired future

No patient eligible for home care leaves hospital before the next stage of care is coordinated, understood and scheduled.

Required capabilities

Early identification of eligible patients, coordination across departments, education for patients and caregivers, and the ability to track the pathway.

What must be built now

Early electronic referral, a clear transition coordinator role, a checklist, time data and regular reviews.

New operating behavior

Planning for home starts before discharge, and the journey is managed as one pathway instead of a series of separate handovers.

Outcomes and impact

Faster start of care, a clearer experience for patients, and fewer gaps in follow-up and unplanned returns to hospital.

The 10X Question

What future do you want for your organization when a person moves from one team to another? What would that future look like in practice if no one had to wait because the next team did not know what they needed? And if that is the future we are aiming for, what skills, data, systems, decision rights and ways of working must we build today so that the transition is managed as one journey rather than as a boundary between departments?
