What Happened When Health Systems Showed Doctors Their Own Performance Data?

Dr. Alaa Mohamed Ahmed

13 November 2026 · 8 min read

A doctor may work for years without seeing numbers on their own practice, such as how often they prescribed an antibiotic that was not needed. Many health systems now measure how their doctors practice and show them the results. This is called audit and feedback. In a review of many trials, clinicians who received their data followed recommended practice more often than those who did not, by about 6 percentage points on average; for example, from 50% to 56%. But the effect varied widely, and some trials saw little or no improvement.

This article draws on five studies published from 2021 to 2026: a Cochrane review of 292 randomized trials (2026), a review of trials on antibiotic prescribing (2025), and three randomized trials of colonoscopy doctors in Canada (2021), pediatric emergency departments (2025), and bladder cancer surgery centers (2026). All of them come from health care. Any point about other kinds of organizations is the author's inference.

What does the review of 292 trials show?

Cochrane is an international network of researchers that gathers the published studies on one question, assesses their quality, and summarizes the results. This kind of study is called a systematic review. In 2026, Cochrane published a review of 292 randomized trials of audit and feedback. In a randomized trial, participants are split into two or more groups by chance, and the groups' results are then compared. [1]

Doctors received the data in 81% of these trials; other health professionals received it in the rest. The practices measured most often were prescribing and test ordering. [1]

In 177 of the trials, researchers compared clinicians who received their performance data with clinicians who did not. On average, recommended practice rose by 6.2 percentage points. For example, if doctors followed a recommendation in 50% of cases, they would now follow it in about 56%. The researchers rated their certainty in this figure as moderate: the true effect is probably close to it, but may differ. [1]

But the effect varied widely. Across the 558 results measured in these trials, half improved by 2.7 points or less, and at least a quarter showed no improvement. This figure is calculated differently from the average above, so the two should not be compared directly. Improvement was larger when doctors started from low performance, and in trials that added more methods alongside the data, most often education or reminders. [1]

Does showing the numbers work everywhere?

A 2025 review included 56 randomized trials of primary care doctors, the clinic and health-center doctors patients usually see first. In these trials, the doctors received data on their antibiotic prescribing. [2]

In the 21 trials that measured total prescribing, doctors who received the data prescribed about 11% fewer antibiotics than those who did not. In 16 trials, they started an antibiotic without need about 23% less often than their peers. Both are relative reductions, measured against doctors who did not receive the data. They are not percentage points, so they cannot be compared directly with the Cochrane figure. The researchers noted that results varied widely across trials, and the trials did not use the same definitions. [2]

In Ontario, Canada, researchers randomly assigned 833 doctors who examine the large bowel with a colonoscope, a thin tube with a camera at its tip. About half received a report on their performance, and the rest did not. One measure of colonoscopy quality is the share of exams in which the doctor removes polyps, small growths in the bowel. This rate rose by similar amounts in both groups, so the report showed no clear effect across all doctors. But lower-performing doctors who received the report improved more than lower performers who did not. [3]

In seven pediatric emergency departments, leaders received an automated monthly report on their department's performance, comparing it with a set benchmark. Recording of the child's vital signs, such as temperature, pulse, and breathing, improved, but some measures of speed got worse: patients waited longer to see a clinician, and more patients left without being seen. The researchers described the results as mixed. [4]

Another trial randomly assigned 201 bladder cancer surgery centers, which together enrolled 14,915 patients in the trial. Half the centers received reports comparing their performance with other centers, plus education. The other half had their performance measured but received no reports. In the centers that received reports, recording of key surgical findings improved, but two other measures did not, and early cancer recurrence did not differ. The researchers said that the centers without reports also improved, which may explain why some measures showed no difference. [5]

What makes the numbers more useful?

The Cochrane review tried to find out when the effect was larger. Most of these findings came from comparing results across trials, not from trials that tested approaches head to head. The researchers said they need confirmation. The effect was larger when: [1]

  • the report measured the doctor's own practice, not the team's or the organization's;
  • it compared that practice with top-performing peers or a set benchmark;
  • a local colleague the doctor already knew presented the data;
  • the data were shared both verbally and in writing, in a two-way conversation;
  • someone helped the doctor discuss the results, and the report came with a plan with specific steps to improve.

Comparison with the average of all peers showed no clear effect. The researchers expected repeated reports to have a larger effect, but repetition was linked to a smaller one. [1]

What can leaders do?

These steps are the author's inference from the studies. No study tested them together.

  1. Choose an important measure where performance is still far from the goal, not one where everyone already does well. [1]
  2. Show each person their own numbers, and compare them with top peers or a clear benchmark. [1]
  3. Have the data presented by someone from the same workplace whom the person already knows, face to face rather than by email. [1]
  4. Attach a plan with specific steps to every report. [1]
  5. Start with lower performers, because they improved the most. [1][3]
  6. Watch other measures that could get worse, not only the one you target. In the pediatric emergency departments, recording improved while, over the same period, speed got worse. [4]

Limits of the evidence

In 113 of the Cochrane review's trials (39%), flaws in design or conduct could make the result inaccurate; researchers call this a high risk of bias. Only 26 (9%) took place in low- or middle-income countries. Most trials combined the data with other methods, which makes it hard to separate the effect of the data alone. [1]

The review measured what health professionals did, not patient health. [1] In the bladder cancer surgery trial, cancer recurrence did not differ between the groups. [5] The pediatric emergency trial collected its data from 2013 to 2016, more than ten years ago, so the way these departments work may have changed since then. [4]

None of the study abstracts mentions a trial in an Arab country, and none of the studies tested the idea outside health care. Applying these findings to Arab organizations or to other sectors is a reasonable inference, but it has not been tested.

The 10X lens

Sending a report may not be enough on its own to change practice. It is more likely to help when it is about the person's own work, compares it with a clear target, is discussed with someone from the same workplace whom they know, and ends with a specific step. A report that reaches everyone and that nobody discusses may become one more document that people read and forget. The author believes the same idea may help other teams, such as sales, maintenance, or customer service, but these studies did not test that.

Key terms

Audit and feedback
Measuring the performance of a doctor or team over a set period, then showing them the results.
Systematic review
A study that gathers the published trials on one question, assesses their quality, and summarizes the results.
Randomized trial
A trial in which participants or centers are assigned to groups at random, and the results are then compared.
Percentage point
The difference between two percentages. Going from 50% to 56% is an increase of six percentage points.
Benchmark
A set level of performance that actual performance is compared with.
Risk of bias
The chance that a study's result is inaccurate because of how it was designed or carried out.

The question that remains

Three years from now, what should performance reports be in your organization? Documents that go to everyone and are soon forgotten? Or a regular meeting in which each person reviews their own numbers with a colleague they know, and together they choose one next step? Who will show your team its numbers, and what step will you ask each person to take?
