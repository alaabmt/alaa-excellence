What Happened When Hospital Nurses Began Scanning Barcodes Before Giving Medicines?

Dr. Alaa Mohammad Ahmed

27 November 2026 · 8 min read

In hospitals, a patient can receive the wrong medicine or the wrong dose. To catch these errors, many hospitals now ask nurses to scan a barcode on the medicine and another on the patient's wristband; the system warns them if either does not match the doctor's order. In one U.S. academic medical center, there were about 115 errors per 1,000 medication administrations before the system and about 68 after it. These figures leave out medicines given too early or too late. But the study compared the period before the system with the period after it. And in a U.K. study of five hospital wards, staff on one ward scanned only about 6% of medicines.

This article draws on five studies published from 2008 to 2025: a study in one U.S. academic medical center (2010), two reviews of earlier studies (2021), a study of how staff work around the system in five hospitals (2008), and a study that followed system use for 16 months on five hospital wards in the U.K. (2025). All of them are about hospitals. What the article says about other organizations is the author's inference.

What does the scanner do?

Before giving a medicine, the nurse scans a barcode on the patient's wristband, then a barcode on the medicine package. The system checks both against the doctor's order in the electronic record. If the medicine, the dose, or the patient is wrong, it shows a warning before the medicine is given. [1][4]

Errors made while giving a medicine are the least likely kind of medication error to be caught before they reach the patient. In a 2008 study of these systems, Koppel and colleagues wrote that claims that they reduce errors had not been clearly shown. [4]

What happened in the U.S. medical center?

Researchers watched nurses give medicines on units of an academic medical center, before and after the system started there. They recorded 14,041 medication administrations and reviewed 3,082 orders that had been copied from the doctor's prescription into the nursing record. [1]

The researchers split errors into two types. The first was timing errors, meaning a medicine given too early or too late. The second was all other errors. Before the system, an error other than a timing error happened in 11.5% of administrations. After it, the rate was 6.8%. That is a relative reduction of about 41%. [1]

In other words, for every 1,000 administrations, there were about 115 such errors before the system and about 68 after it. Among errors other than timing errors, those that could harm a patient fell from 3.1% to 1.6% of administrations. Timing errors fell by about 27% from their original number, but timing errors that could harm a patient showed no statistically significant change. Errors in copying orders occurred in 6.1% of copied orders before the system and disappeared after it. [1]

The researchers said the system cut errors substantially but did not eliminate them. This was a before-and-after study: it compared the same units in two periods and did not include units without the system over the same time. [1]

What do the reviews say?

A systematic review gathers the published research on one question and assesses its quality. A systematic review that searched studies published up to 2016, and appeared in print in 2021, gathered 10 studies on the effect of barcoding on medication errors in hospitals. All were before-and-after studies; nine were in the United States and one in the United Kingdom. All 10 reported better results after barcoding was introduced. The authors said barcoding may reduce medication errors, especially errors involving the wrong medicine, the wrong dose, or the wrong patient. [3]

In the same year, Cochrane, an international network of researchers that prepares reviews of medical evidence, published a review of 65 studies on ways to reduce medication errors in adults in hospital. Two of them, with more than 50,000 participants, were about barcoding. The authors said barcoding may reduce medication errors compared with usual care, but they rated their certainty in this evidence as low. Low certainty means the true effect may be quite different from what the studies found. [2]

Do staff actually use the scanner?

Researchers studied the system in five hospitals. They watched nurses at work in two of the hospitals, interviewed staff and leaders at all five, and analyzed logs of overridden warnings. They found 15 ways in which staff work around the system's steps. In some cases, a copy of a patient's wristband barcode was stuck on a computer cart, on the scanner itself, or on a door frame. In others, several patients' medicines were scanned in advance and then carried around on the cart. [4]

The researchers found 31 causes of these workarounds. They included medicine barcodes that could not be read (torn, smudged, or covered by another label), broken scanners, missing or wet patient wristbands, medicines with no barcode, failing batteries, unreliable wireless connections, and emergencies. Nurses overrode the system's warnings and continued for 4.2% of patients and 10.3% of medicines. [4]

In 2025, researchers analyzed 613,868 medication administrations on five hospital wards in the U.K. during the first 16 months after the system started. On one ward, staff scanned 5.6% of medicines; on another, 67%. Patient wristbands were scanned 4.6% of the time on one ward and 89% on another. [5]

The reasons staff recorded for not scanning included barcodes that could not be read and scanners that were not available. Scanning declined over time. Scanning was higher where there was a locally led quality improvement initiative, and it varied with the time the medicine was given and its form, such as tablets and liquids. Staff changed what they were about to do after 37% of the system's warnings, so most warnings did not change what they did. [5]

What can leaders do?

The author drew these steps from the studies; no study tested their combined effect.

  1. Measure each month the share of medicines scanned on each ward, instead of assuming that having the system is enough. [5]
  2. Ask why scanning did not happen and fix the causes: unreadable barcodes, broken scanners, and missing wristbands. [4][5]
  3. Make sure every medicine arrives on the ward with a barcode that can be read. [4]
  4. Review the warnings: which ones change what staff do, and which ones they usually override. [4][5]
  5. Support an improvement initiative on each ward, led by the staff themselves. [5]
  6. Look for other steps in your organization where a quick check could catch an error before it happens.

Limits of the evidence

The U.S. study took place in one medical center and was a before-and-after study, so it cannot show that the system alone caused the drop. [1] The first review gathered 10 studies, all of them before-and-after studies and most of them in the United States. [3] The Cochrane review rated its certainty in the barcoding result as low, and that result rested on only two studies. [2]

The workaround study was published in 2008, and devices and systems may have changed since then. [4] The U.K. study covered only five wards. [5] The published abstracts of the five studies do not mention any Arab hospital, and none of the studies tested the idea outside hospitals. The idea that it could help in other sectors is the author's inference.

The 10X lens

In the author's view, buying a checking system does not by itself change how people work. The author expects the real change to come when scanning becomes the normal way of working on every ward, and when the obstacles that push staff to work around it are removed. The author also thinks the same idea could help in other work that needs a check at the last step, such as issuing spare parts or picking orders in a warehouse.

Key terms

Barcode
Printed lines that a scanner reads to identify a medicine or a patient.
Before-and-after study
A study that compares results in the same place before and after a change, with no group where the change did not happen.
Systematic review
A study that gathers the published research on one question, assesses its quality, and summarizes the results.
Relative reduction
How much a number fell, measured against the original number. A drop from 115 to 68 errors per 1,000 administrations is a relative reduction of about 41%.
Certainty of evidence
The researchers' judgment of how close a result is likely to be to the true effect. Low certainty means the true effect may be quite different.
Workaround
A way staff skip a step in a system to get their work done, such as scanning a barcode stuck on a computer cart instead of the barcode on the patient's wristband.

The question that remains

Three years from now, will your organization still have a checking system that you paid for but people rarely use? Or will checking be a normal part of every important step, because you removed what got in the way?
