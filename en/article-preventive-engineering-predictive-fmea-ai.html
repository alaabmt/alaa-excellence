When Static FMEA Is Not Enough: From Periodic Review to Early Warning

The idea is not to let AI make supplier-quality decisions for the engineer. It is to help the organization detect weak signals, explain them, and bring them back into failure-mode reasoning before a defect reaches production or the customer.

Risk can change between review meetings

An automotive manufacturer may have an approved FMEA for a supplier, component and production step. The ratings look acceptable, the control plan exists, and no major customer complaint has appeared. Yet smaller signals begin to accumulate in the background: isolated defects across recent lots, delayed closure of corrective actions, modest drift in a process parameter, and previous cases in which similar defects escaped inspection.

None of these signals, by itself, may justify escalation. The problem is that risk does not wait for the next scheduled FMEA review. The preventive question is more demanding: can the organization recognize a risk pattern while it is forming and reopen the analysis before that pattern becomes repeated nonconformity, production disruption or a customer complaint?

A 2026 ECAI conference paper addresses that question with a conceptual framework that combines predictive quality analytics, explainable artificial intelligence and FMEA, while keeping human validation explicitly inside the decision path. [1]

What the paper actually proposes

Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA) is a proactive way to structure thinking about what must succeed, how it can fail, what the effects and potential causes are, which prevention and detection controls already exist, and what deserves action. The paper does not propose replacing that engineering logic with an algorithm. It adds a data layer intended to make the analysis more responsive to changing evidence.

Predictive quality analytics uses historical and operational data to identify patterns associated with a higher probability of future nonconformity. Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) adds the next necessary question: why did the model's risk signal rise? Was it influenced by recurring defects, supplier deterioration, weak detection history, process drift, or delayed corrective-action closure?

The signal still does not become a decision automatically. The proposed framework maps it into FMEA-relevant elements and asks a quality engineer or technical expert to test whether the relationship is plausible for the specific component, process and failure mode. The authors are explicit that explainability does not establish causality; engineering judgment remains necessary before a rating is changed or an action is authorized. [1]

Supplier & process data→Predictive risk signal→Explainable risk drivers→FMEA reassessment→Expert validation→Preventive decision→Feedback

What was tested — and what was not

The authors performed a simulation-based test on synthetic supplier-quality data. The dataset represented variables such as recent defect counts, 8D closure delays, recurrence, inspection escapes, process drift, audit findings and control-plan weakness. A Random Forest classifier was used as an illustrative predictive model. [1]

Those values are not evidence that the same model would perform that way in a real plant. The paper itself frames the exercise as an initial functional test of the decision logic rather than proof of industrial performance. The distinction matters: the simulation shows that the pathway can be constructed; it does not demonstrate that supplier quality improved in practice.

Evidence limit: the paper's main contribution is conceptual. It does not report a real-factory before-and-after evaluation showing lower defect rates, quality cost or customer complaints because of the proposed framework.

From the paper to Preventive Engineering: a nine-stage application

Method note: the nine stages below are the Preventive Engineering lens used in this series to turn the research idea into an operational application. The paper does not present itself as a literal implementation of these nine stages, so source findings and author-developed application are kept separate.
1

Define the analysis scope: what exactly are we analysing?

“Supplier quality” is too broad to be an actionable scope. A workable boundary identifies the component or component family, the relevant supplier process, the data and time window, and the start and end of the decision pathway. A practical example would be: “lots of a specified electromechanical component from one supplier, from acquisition of process and inspection data through lot-acceptance and production-release decisions.”

The exclusions matter as well. A supplier's general commercial performance may influence management decisions, but it does not automatically become a technical cause of a failure mode in a particular component. Good scoping prevents enterprise-level concerns from being mixed with failure mechanisms that require engineering evidence.

2

Identify the work components and their functions: what must succeed?

The proposed system is not one algorithm performing one job. It is a chain of interdependent functions. Each one must work well enough for the preventive decision to be trustworthy.

Supplier and process dataRepresent what is actually happening, accurately and in timeConsistent defect definitions, traceable lot/process links, complete critical fields

Expert validationTest technical plausibility and contextClear authority to approve, reject or request more evidence

3

Identify failure modes, effects and causes: how can the function fail?

The distinctions matter. A failure mode describes how the required function is not achieved. The effect describes what happens because of that failure. A potential failure cause explains why the failure mode could occur. A failure mechanism adds the “how it develops” layer when that improves understanding or action selection.

These are author-developed examples derived from the framework's architecture. They are not a published failure list from a real factory; the paper does not report a field implementation.

4

Identify current prevention and detection controls: what exists now?

The paper makes this stage especially important because some predictive signals are directly related to weak detection or weak control plans. A control's existence does not prove its effectiveness. Final inspection may be documented, yet the defect may have escaped it in previous lots. An 8D report may be administratively closed, while the same defect recurs.

A prevention control acts upstream to stop a cause or failure mode from developing — for example, locking a critical process setting or preventing use of the wrong material. A detection control finds the problem after it begins but before the effect reaches the customer or next process — for example, a test that is demonstrably capable of detecting the specific failure mode.

The predictive system itself also needs controls: data-quality checks, model-drift monitoring, an explanation trail, and human review before FMEA or supplier status is changed.

5

Assess the risk triad: severity, occurrence and detectability

Do not start with numbers. Start with the logical chain. A precise-looking score built on a weak description of function, failure mode, effect, cause and current controls does not become reliable merely because it is quantified.

Severity

Severity belongs to the failure effect. The paper notes that it is relatively stable unless product use, customer impact or safety relevance changes. A model should not increase severity merely because recurrence is rising.

Occurrence

This is where new evidence can matter directly. Repeated similar defects, supplier instability or process drift can justify reopening the occurrence assessment — not changing it automatically.

Detectability

Inspection escapes and weak control-plan history can show that detection is poorer than assumed. The question is whether the current control can actually find this failure mode before its effect propagates.

If the organization uses numeric rating scales, they should follow its approved definitions and the applicable FMEA method. There is no universal number in this paper that can simply be copied into another factory's analysis.

6

Prioritize treatment: what should be addressed first?

A predictive score does not become a priority simply because it is high. Priority comes from evidence interpreted in the context of risk: a severe effect, rising recurrence, a detection control with documented escapes, or a corrective action followed by renewed failure.

The paper connects these signals to familiar supplier-quality decisions — control-plan review, focused audits, additional process verification, corrective-action follow-up and supplier escalation. The key is that the action follows validated technical meaning rather than an unexplained algorithmic score.

7

Design and implement the treatment: what will change?

If a signal shows that defects are escaping inspection, the easiest response may be to add another inspection. Preventive Engineering asks a harder question first: can the process be changed so the defect is less likely to be created? If that is not possible, can a stronger barrier be placed closer to the source?

The action should identify the risk it targets, what will change in the design, process or control, who owns the change, when it will be implemented, and what evidence will prove implementation. Training and alerts may support the change, but they should not substitute for an engineering or operating-model change when the weakness is built into the system.

8

Verify effectiveness and reassess risk: did the treatment work?

Closing an action in a tracking system proves that the task was completed; it does not prove that risk fell. Baseline measures should be defined before implementation and then revisited afterward. Depending on the case, they may include defect recurrence, inspection escapes, process stability, time from signal to action, and the proportion of alerts that experts confirm as intervention-worthy.

QuestionExample effectiveness evidence
Did occurrence fall?Post-action recurrence trend with a clear denominator and observation period
Did detection improve?Fewer escapes plus evidence that the revised control can detect the failure mode
Is the predictive signal useful?Alerts compared with actual outcomes, including false alarms and missed cases
Did the risk profile change?Occurrence and detection reassessed using new evidence while severity remains tied to effect
9

Review and update continuously: how do we keep the methodology alive?

The paper's most important operational idea is that FMEA need not wait for an audit or a major quality event to be reopened. Review can be triggered when the data, process, supplier or predictive model changes.

Useful triggers include recurrence after corrective action, sustained process drift, a sub-supplier or material change, a new model version, degraded data quality, a newly observed failure mode, or evidence that a detection control is weaker in practice than it appeared on paper.

The proposed feedback loop returns action outcomes to the next round of analysis. That turns a one-way chain — data → prediction → decision — into a learning system: data → interpretation → decision → outcome → learning → reassessment.

What actually changes in AI's role?

The strongest idea in the paper is not that AI can “do FMEA.” Its proposed role is narrower and more useful: use data to surface early signals, explain the evidence behind them, and feed that evidence into engineering reasoning that experts can review.

This preserves a boundary between evidence and decision. A model may show that recurrence risk has risen because similar defects appeared recently alongside process drift and weak detection history. It does not prove by itself that the drifting parameter caused the defect. It does not autonomously decide how an occurrence rating should change. It does not authorize a supplier action without context.

Maintaining that boundary can expand the team's field of view without turning FMEA into a black box. Losing it risks replacing one form of subjective judgment with an algorithmic score that looks more scientific than it really is.

The evidence limits that must stay visible

First, the proposed framework is conceptual; the paper does not validate it on real supplier-quality data. Second, the simulation used 240 synthetic records, so the reported 0.88 accuracy or 0.91 AUC should not be presented as expected performance in another plant. Third, explainability supports interpretation but does not prove causality. Fourth, the approach depends heavily on data quality, consistent defect classification, and traceability across components, processes and historical records. Fifth, adoption requires organizational integration across data, quality, engineering, purchasing and supplier-management routines. A predictive model built in isolation is unlikely to become a preventive operating system.

A sensible first implementation would therefore be deliberately narrow: one component or process with sufficient historical data, a defined baseline, explicit human decision rights, and a plan to test whether the resulting treatment actually reduces risk.

The Preventive Engineering question

If your organization already holds data on defects, inspections, process drift and corrective actions, does that information remain only a record of what happened? Or can it become an early-warning system that links a weak signal to a clearly defined failure mode, opens a preventive decision, and then verifies whether the decision reduced risk before the pattern becomes a recurring defect?
