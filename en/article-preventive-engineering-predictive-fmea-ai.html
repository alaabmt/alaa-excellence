When Static FMEA Is Not Enough: From Periodic Review to Early Warning

The idea is not to let AI make supplier-quality decisions for the engineer. It is to help the organization detect weak signals, explain them, and bring them back into failure-mode reasoning before a defect reaches production or the customer.

Risk can change between review meetings

An automotive manufacturer may have an approved FMEA for a supplier, component and production step. The ratings look acceptable, the control plan exists, and no major customer complaint has appeared. Yet smaller signals begin to accumulate in the background: isolated defects across recent lots, delayed closure of corrective actions, modest drift in a process parameter, and previous cases in which similar defects escaped inspection.

None of these signals, by itself, may justify escalation. The problem is that risk does not wait for the next scheduled FMEA review. The preventive question is more demanding: can the organization recognize a risk pattern while it is forming and reopen the analysis before that pattern becomes repeated nonconformity, production disruption or a customer complaint?

A 2026 ECAI conference paper addresses that question with a conceptual framework that combines predictive quality analytics, explainable artificial intelligence and FMEA, while keeping human validation explicitly inside the decision path. [1]

What the paper actually proposes

Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA) is a proactive way to structure thinking about what must succeed, how it can fail, what the effects and potential causes are, which prevention and detection controls already exist, and what deserves action. The paper does not propose replacing that engineering logic with an algorithm. It adds a data layer intended to make the analysis more responsive to changing evidence.

Predictive quality analytics uses historical and operational data to identify patterns associated with a higher probability of future nonconformity. Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) adds the next necessary question: why did the model's risk signal rise? Was it influenced by recurring defects, supplier deterioration, weak detection history, process drift, or delayed corrective-action closure?

The signal still does not become a decision automatically. The proposed framework maps it into FMEA-relevant elements and asks a quality engineer or technical expert to test whether the relationship is plausible for the specific component, process and failure mode. The authors are explicit that explainability does not establish causality; engineering judgment remains necessary before a rating is changed or an action is authorized. [1]

Read the pathway below from supplier data to preventive decision. The point of the diagram is not simply to show technical steps. It shows that a predictive signal does not jump directly into an FMEA change or an action; it passes through explanation and engineering validation first.

Supplier & process data→Predictive risk signal→Explainable risk drivers→FMEA reassessment→Expert validation→Preventive decision→Feedback

What should you notice? Three gates sit between a signal and a decision: explain what drove the signal, map it to the correct FMEA element, and validate the engineering meaning. Skipping any one of them may make the decision faster, but less defensible.

Those values are not evidence that the same model would perform that way in a real plant. The paper itself frames the exercise as an initial functional test of the decision logic rather than proof of industrial performance. The distinction matters: the simulation shows that the pathway can be constructed; it does not demonstrate that supplier quality improved in practice.

The authors extracted feature importance from the Random Forest. These values show which variables contributed most to classification inside the simulation. They do not prove that a variable is the engineering cause of a failure.

Evidence limit: the paper's main contribution is conceptual. It does not report a real-factory before-and-after evaluation showing lower defect rates, quality cost or customer complaints because of the proposed framework. Feature importance also describes contribution to prediction, not causality.

Applying the research through the nine-stage Preventive Engineering journey

What are we analysing here? The nine stages are not a separate theory lesson, and the paper did not claim to apply them literally. We will use one continuous teaching case: an electromechanical component from an external supplier whose required function is to maintain a stable electrical connection. Weak signals begin to appear: recurrence of an “intermittent connection” failure mode, process drift in a fastening operation, and similar defects escaping the end-of-line test. The nine stages answer one question: how do we move from those signals to a preventive decision that can be shown to reduce risk?
1

Define the analysis scope: what exactly are we analysing?

What are we doing here? We draw the boundary before analysing risk so that “supplier quality” does not become an unmanageably broad topic.

In our case: the scope is a specified electromechanical component from one supplier, from the supplier fastening operation through process and inspection data to lot acceptance and production release. The monitored failure mode is “intermittent connection during operation.”

Common mistake: mixing every commercial or management problem involving the supplier into a technical FMEA line.

Stage output: one component + one process + one failure mode + a clear start and end point.
2

Identify system components and required functions: what must succeed?

What are we doing here? We decompose the system that creates and protects quality. We ask not only “what are the steps?” but “what function must succeed at each point?”

In our case: the fastening process must create a stable joint, the component must maintain electrical continuity, the end-of-line test must detect instability, and the data must correctly link the defect to the lot and process before a predictive signal deserves review.

Look at the table below. Read each row as component → required function → practical evidence that the function succeeded.

Components and functions in the case used across all nine stages
ComponentRequired functionPractical success condition
Supplier fastening processCreate a stable joint within approved process limitsCritical parameters remain stable and process capability is acceptable
Electromechanical componentMaintain stable electrical connection during operationNo interruption or instability outside specification
End-of-line testDetect an unstable connection before releaseDemonstrated ability to detect the specific failure mode
Supplier/process dataCorrectly link defect, lot, process and inspection evidenceTraceable, complete and consistent records
Predictive monitoringSurface an early signal that deserves reviewAn explainable signal based on sufficiently reliable data
FMEA reviewTurn new evidence into an engineering question and documented decisionNo rating change before technical validation

What does the table show? Risk does not live inside the model alone. The process, component, test, data and interpretation can each fail. Their functions must be clear before their failure modes can be analysed.

Common mistake: jumping directly to “the defect” without first defining what function should have succeeded.

Stage output: a function map that can support credible failure-mode analysis.
3

Identify failure modes, effects and causes: how can the function fail?

What are we doing here? For each required function, we ask how it can fail, what happens if it fails, and why it could fail. Failure mode, effect and cause remain separate.

In our core case: the required function is stable electrical connection. The failure mode is intermittent connection during operation. The effect may be loss of function, assembly disruption or a field failure. Supplier fastening instability is a potential cause, but it does not become a proven cause merely because a model finds a statistical association.

Look at the table below. Each row preserves the sequence: function → failure mode → effect → potential cause.

Failure-mode examples within the same case
Required functionFailure modePotential effectPotential cause
Create a stable jointFastening does not achieve required stabilityIntermittent electrical connectionProcess drift, tool wear or unstable setup
Maintain stable connectionIntermittent connection in operationLoss of function, assembly disruption or customer failureUnstable joint produced by the fastening process
Detect instabilityEnd-of-line test misses the failure modeDefective part escapes downstreamTest method does not reproduce the conditions in which the defect appears
Maintain traceabilityDefect or lot data are linked incorrectlyMisleading signal or the wrong FMEA line is reviewedInconsistent identifiers or data definitions

What should you notice? Weak detection is not a technical cause of the component failure mode; it is a failure of the detection barrier. Likewise, process drift is not a proven cause merely because feature importance is high.

Common mistake: writing an effect as a failure mode, or treating an association as a confirmed cause.

Stage output: a reviewable chain: required function → failure mode → effect → potential cause.
4

Identify current prevention and detection controls: what exists now?

What are we doing here? We separate controls that prevent the cause or failure mode from controls that detect it after it begins.

In our case: prevention may include fastening-process limits, tool maintenance and process-capability monitoring. Detection may include the end-of-line test. If similar defects have escaped that test, the escape history is evidence that detection may be weaker than FMEA assumed.

Common mistake: calling training or final inspection “prevention” when they do not prevent the technical cause from developing.

Stage output: a separated list of prevention controls and detection controls, with evidence of actual effectiveness where available.
5

Assess the risk triad: severity, occurrence and detectability

What are we doing here? We reassess risk only after the failure logic is clear. The paper does not provide a machine that calculates S/O/D from model probability; it provides evidence that may justify reopening certain ratings.

In our case: if the effect of intermittent connection has not changed, severity does not rise merely because recurrence increased. Confirmed recurrence may justify occurrence review, while escape evidence may justify detection review.

Severity

Tied to the failure effect. It changes when the consequence or application changes, not simply when recurrence increases.

Occurrence

Reopened when new evidence suggests that the same failure mode is recurring more often than previously assumed.

Detection

Reopened when the current control is shown to miss the failure mode or does not challenge the conditions in which it appears.

Did the researchers calculate a new risk triad?

No. The paper does not publish a conversion rule from predictive probability to an occurrence rating, or from escape counts to a detection rating.

Look at the table below. It shows when new evidence may justify reopening each risk dimension and which shortcuts should be avoided.

How new evidence enters risk-triad reassessment
Risk dimensionWhat may justify review?What should not be done?
SeverityA changed failure effect, product application, customer consequence or safety relevanceRaise severity because recurrence increased or model probability rose
OccurrenceConfirmed recurrence of the same failure mode or evidence that the prior estimate understated actual frequencyConvert model probability directly into an occurrence rating without the approved scale and engineering validation
DetectionEscape history or demonstrated weakness in the current detection controlConvert escape count directly into a detection rating without testing control capability

The key point: probability is not occurrence, feature importance is not cause, and escape history is not a detection rating. Data opens the question; the FMEA team decides whether the rating changes.

Stage output: a documented decision on whether S, O or D should be reassessed, and why.
6

Prioritize treatment: what should be addressed first?

What are we doing here? We turn assessment into prioritization. A high predictive score is not a priority by itself; priority comes from consequence, recurrence, control weakness and the organization's approved method.

In our case: a meaningful functional effect combined with growing recurrence and inspection escapes can justify faster action than a low-consequence issue with strong detection.

Common mistake: ranking risks by model score or RPN alone without considering the failure effect, control strength and the approved Action Priority method where applicable.

Stage output: a treatment priority justified by engineering meaning and the organization's method.
7

Design and implement treatment: what will change?

What are we doing here? We choose an action capable of reducing risk, starting with prevention of the cause where possible rather than defaulting to more inspection.

In our case: if fastening instability is validated as a cause, treatment may strengthen critical process limits, tool maintenance and process-capability verification. If the end-of-line test is weak, it should be redesigned to challenge the actual failure mode.

Common mistake: defaulting to 100% inspection while leaving the mechanism creating the defect unchanged.

Stage output: a defined action, owner, due date and an explicit expectation about what should change in occurrence or detection.
8

Verify effectiveness and reassess risk: did treatment work?

What are we doing here? We separate evidence that the action was implemented from evidence that risk actually fell.

In our case: we track recurrence of intermittent connection after process changes, escapes after test redesign, and process stability before reassessing O and D.

Look at the table below. Each question requires a different form of evidence; closing the action in a tracking system does not answer any of them by itself.

Evidence that treatment is actually effective
QuestionExample effectiveness evidence
Did occurrence fall?Lower recurrence over a defined number of lots and observation period
Did detection improve?Fewer escapes plus evidence that the revised test can detect the failure mode
Did the process stabilize?Improved capability/stability for the parameter technically linked to the failure mode
Did the risk profile change?O and D reassessed with new evidence while severity remains tied to the effect

What does the table show? There is no single universal “effectiveness metric.” The metric must test the assumption behind the action.

Stage output: before/after evidence that confirms or rejects risk reduction, followed by FMEA reassessment.
9

Review and update continuously: how do we keep the method alive?

What are we doing here? We turn FMEA from a calendar-driven document into an analysis that reopens when meaningful evidence changes.

In our case: review triggers include recurrence after corrective action, sustained process drift, a material/tool/sub-supplier change, degraded data quality, a new failure mode or renewed inspection escapes.

Common mistake: waiting for a major complaint before updating FMEA — or, at the other extreme, reopening it for every weak statistical alert without a validation gate.

Stage output: explicit rules for when review opens, what evidence is required, and who authorizes the update.

The operating rule is straightforward: predictive analytics should not modify FMEA; it should tell the team where a better engineering question needs to be asked.

Then the data changes

In this teaching scenario, the same defect begins to recur across recent lots, a process variable drifts before some events, and similar defects have escaped the end-of-line test. The predictive layer raises risk; its explanation points to recurrence, process drift and escape history.

The team still does not jump to new ratings. It first asks whether the events involve the same failure mode, the same process and plausible mechanism, whether lot traceability is reliable, and whether the current test was actually challenged against this failure mode. Only then is the rating review opened.

How to start in your organization without building a large AI program

Start small: one process or component family with an existing FMEA and enough historical data to make comparison meaningful. The opening question should not be “How do we deploy AI?” It should be: which FMEA assumptions may change faster than our current review cycle, and which data could reveal that change?

Use the table below as a start-up plan. Do not move forward until the previous step has a clear output, especially scope, data validity and the validation gate.

What actually changes in AI's role?

The strongest idea in the paper is not that AI can “do FMEA.” Its proposed role is narrower and more useful: use data to surface early signals, explain the evidence behind them, and feed that evidence into engineering reasoning that experts can review.

This preserves a boundary between evidence and decision. A model may show that recurrence risk has risen because similar defects appeared recently alongside process drift and weak detection history. It does not prove by itself that the drifting parameter caused the defect. It does not autonomously decide how an occurrence rating should change. It does not authorize a supplier action without context.

Maintaining that boundary can expand the team's field of view without turning FMEA into a black box. Losing it risks replacing one form of subjective judgment with an algorithmic score that looks more scientific than it really is.

The evidence limits that must stay visible

First, the proposed framework is conceptual; the paper does not validate it on real supplier-quality data. Second, the simulation used 240 synthetic records, so the reported 0.88 accuracy or 0.91 AUC should not be presented as expected performance in another plant. Third, explainability supports interpretation but does not prove causality. Fourth, the approach depends heavily on data quality, consistent defect classification, and traceability across components, processes and historical records. Fifth, adoption requires organizational integration across data, quality, engineering, purchasing and supplier-management routines. A predictive model built in isolation is unlikely to become a preventive operating system.

A sensible first implementation would therefore be deliberately narrow: one component or process with sufficient historical data, a defined baseline, explicit human decision rights, and a plan to test whether the resulting treatment actually reduces risk.

What should a reader be able to do after this article?

The practical takeaway is not “use AI to update FMEA.” It is to make reassessment evidence-triggered rather than calendar-triggered. A team can start now with one FMEA line, connect it to defect, detection and process data, define which signals justify review, document why occurrence or detection changed — or why they did not — and then measure whether treatment actually reduced risk.

Once that loop is disciplined — data → signal → explanation → FMEA question → expert validation → reassessment → treatment → effectiveness verification — FMEA stops behaving like a periodic document and starts behaving like a preventive learning system.

The Preventive Engineering question

If your organization already holds data on defects, inspections, process drift and corrective actions, does that information remain only a record of what happened? Or can it become an early-warning system that links a weak signal to a clearly defined failure mode, opens a preventive decision, and then verifies whether the decision reduced risk before the pattern becomes a recurring defect?
