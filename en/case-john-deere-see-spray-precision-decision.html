From Spraying the Whole Field to Targeting the Individual Weed: How John Deere Redesigned the Decision

What happens when a machine can distinguish where intervention is actually needed? See & Spray shows how a new sensing capability can redesign the decision itself.

Executive Snapshot

Conventional spraying treats the field as the primary unit of action. John Deere’s See & Spray changes that logic: cameras capture real-time images, machine-learning models identify weeds, onboard computing processes the information, and individual nozzles activate only where treatment is needed. The significance is not simply greater automation. The unit of decision becomes smaller and more precise.

Independent evidence supports meaningful reductions in herbicide use, but it also exposes an important limitation: maximizing input savings can undermine weed control if detection sensitivity is set too low.[1]

The Challenge: When the Intervention Is Larger Than the Problem

Broadcast spraying couples the scale of the intervention to the size of the field rather than to the actual distribution of weeds. Machine vision creates a deeper opportunity than simply spraying faster: where is intervention actually needed, and how little intervention can achieve the required result?

The 10X Move: Change the Unit of Decision Before Optimising the Decision

A conventional improvement programme might ask how to make broadcast spraying faster, cheaper or more accurate. A redesign question asks why the field should receive one decision at all. Integrating machine vision, machine learning and nozzle control turns one large decision — spray this field — into thousands of smaller decisions: spray here, skip here, continue sensing.

Evidence of Impact

Independent field evidence

In a three-year University of Arkansas field trial, appropriate use of See & Spray reduced post-emergence herbicide use by 43% to 59% relative to broadcast applications. At higher sensitivity settings, weed-control performance was generally comparable with conventional treatment.[1]

The highest saving was not the best result

The lowest sensitivity setting was associated with an approximately 280% annual increase in the Palmer amaranth seedbank. A metric can improve while the system deteriorates: a larger short-term chemical saving can permit more weeds to survive and reproduce.[1]

Commercial scale

John Deere reported that See & Spray was used across more than five million acres during the 2025 growing season, with customers reducing non-residual herbicide use by nearly 50% on average. These figures are company-reported operational evidence, not independent proof of average performance across all farms.[3][4]

Economics depends on context

A 2026 peer-reviewed study of commercial-use data reported an average 58% reduction in targeted herbicide use, while showing that profitability varied with weed pressure, herbicide cost, system configuration and technology cost. Under its assumptions, targeted herbicide expenditure generally needed to exceed about $27 per hectare for Premium and $39 per hectare for Ultimate to justify the technologies economically relative to broadcast spraying.[2]

The 10X Lens

Redefining Value

Performance shifts from covering the field efficiently to applying only the intervention required while preserving the agricultural outcome.

Multiplying Capability

The capability emerges from cameras, models, onboard computing, nozzle control, agronomic settings and operator judgement working together.

Reinventing the Operating Model

The process moves from one treatment decision across a large area to repeated decisions at a much finer level.

Innovation and Experimentation

The Arkansas trial revealed not only whether the technology worked, but how the optimisation target itself could become wrong.

Maximising Intelligence

Execution also creates information about weed pressure and application, allowing work itself to become a source of learning.

Maximising Sustainable Results

The better outcome combines lower unnecessary input, effective weed control, acceptable economics and lower long-term risk.

Practical Application: Redesign the Unit of Decision

When an organisation becomes capable of detecting where need actually exists, it should reconsider why it still applies the same intervention to everyone. The conventional model is large group → single rule → uniform intervention. A more intelligent model is sense condition → identify need → make a specific decision → apply proportionate intervention → learn. The transferable lesson is not the agricultural technology. It is the decision architecture.

Transferability and Limits

This logic works best when meaningful differences can be detected reliably, decisions can be made quickly enough for the information to remain useful, and the cost of individualising intervention does not exceed the value created. Precision also raises the cost of error. Human judgement does not disappear; it moves to thresholds, settings, objectives, trade-offs and the definition of success.

Where does your organisation still apply the same intervention to everyone simply because, historically, it could not tell who actually needed it? If you can now sense, distinguish and decide with greater precision, will you use that capability to make the old process faster — or redesign the unit of decision itself?
