Did an FMEA-Guided Redesign Get Frozen-Section Specimens to the Lab on Time at a Chinese Hospital?

During surgery, the surgeon waits for the pathology lab to answer about a specimen sent minutes earlier: is a growth cancer, and has all of it been removed? If the specimen is late, or its name or site is confused, the decision stalls while the patient is still under anesthesia. A teaching hospital in eastern China sends about 80 such specimens a month. Before the change, 80.7% were recorded at the pathology lab within five minutes of handover, and 75.2% of handover records were complete and consistent. A multidisciplinary team analyzed the pathway using failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA), a structured way to find what could go wrong, rate each risk, and set priorities. It built a barcode-based digital pathway. Three months later the two rates were 98.4% and 99.2%. But this is a before-and-after comparison at one hospital, and it did not measure the errors themselves.

What problem did the hospital face?

During surgery, a tissue specimen is sent to the pathology department for a quick examination called a frozen section. The surgeon uses the answer for decisions such as where to cut. The hospital is a tertiary teaching hospital in eastern China, a large referral hospital. It performs about 20,000 operations a year and sends about 80 of these specimens a month. Before the change, 46 of 238 specimens (19.3%) missed the five-minute target, by our arithmetic from the study’s figures. [1]

These specimens followed a path much like routine specimens: a mix of information systems, barcodes, and paper records, and the records of who handed over, who received, and when were not linked in one system. Hospital policy requires delivery within five minutes of handover. [1]

The study notes that surgical specimen errors mostly happen before analysis: incomplete information, labels that do not match the request, wrong patient identity, and delayed transport. [1]

How did the team set priorities?

The team included nursing leaders and nurses, a surgeon, a pathology technologist, and an information technology engineer. They mapped the path from specimen request to result communication and listed failure modes in brainstorming sessions. [1]

Team members rated each failure mode on severity, occurrence, and detection (S/O/D), each from 1 to 10, using criteria they agreed on locally. Severity is how bad the effect is. Occurrence is how often it happens. Detection is how hard it is to catch, and a high score means harder. Multiplying the three gives the risk priority number (RPN). They picked the four highest. The table below lists them, with the changes the team linked to each. [1]

The four highest failure modes (severity · occurrence · detection = RPN) and what was linked to them (as reported in the study)
Failure modeScoresWhat was linked to it

The two failures with the highest severity (10) ranked third and fourth because the team judged them less likely (occurrence 4). The incomplete record ranked first because, with a severity of 9, it was both more likely (5) and hard to catch (7). Delayed transport ranked second because it was the most frequent (6). That is our reading of the scores. [1]

What did the hospital change, and what did it measure?

The program launched on January 1, 2026. The study calls it five interconnected changes. First, a dedicated pathway, with standard verification steps, for frozen-section specimens. Second, one record for repeat specimens from the same patient. Third, barcode handover that ties operating room staff, the transporter, and pathology to a timestamp. Fourth, role-based training, checked with simulated scenarios. Fifth, live monitoring by nurses, plus a weekly audit. [1]

The table below sets three months before (October to December 2025, 238 specimens) with three months after (January to March 2026, 247 specimens). The first measure is a specimen recorded as received at pathology within five minutes of handover. The second is a handover record that is complete and consistent: patient and specimen identifiers, request matching label, sequence number, responsible staff, and timestamps. [1]

Both main measures rose by far more than chance alone would produce (p below 0.001). But they measure time and record quality. The study did not directly measure errors in specimen name, site, or side, and it counted a request-to-label mismatch only inside the record measure. It also reports no re-scoring of the failure modes. [1]

Which of FMEA’s nine stages does the study show?

FMEA can be described in nine stages, from setting the scope to ongoing review. The mapping below is ours, not the study’s. The table shows where the study covers each stage.

StageWhat the study shows
1. ScopeThe frozen-section path, from request to result communication.
2. Components and tasksRequest, identification, documentation, handover, transport, and receipt in pathology.
3. Failure modes, effects, causesThe top four only, each with its effect and contributing factors.
4. Current prevention and detectionAn existing detection method for each, such as manual review and verbal confirmation.
5. Risk evaluationThree scores from 1 to 10 from the team’s experience.
6. PrioritiesThe four highest failure modes (280 to 315).

8. VerificationBefore and after comparison of two path measures. No re-scoring is reported.
9. Ongoing reviewLive monitoring and weekly audits; the study says sustainability needs validation.

In our reading, the study reaches stage 8, with a measure of the path rather than of the risk itself.

What do we not know?

This is a single-hospital before-and-after study, with three months in each period. The study itself says the design cannot prove cause. [1]

  • Some of the gain may come from time trends or greater staff awareness, and the study cannot separate the effect of each of the five changes.
  • The study did not measure diagnostic turnaround, specimen-related errors as such, diagnostic agreement, or patient outcomes.
  • “Before” data came from paper registers and audit checklists. “After” data came from the barcode system plus registers and audits. In our view, changing the instrument along with the program may raise recorded accuracy by design. The study says it applied the same definitions in both periods.
  • The study shows only the four highest failure modes, not the scores of the rest.
  • Staff satisfaction was one yes-or-no question for 62 people, with no statistical test.

Shebl and colleagues, in a study of two teams at a British hospital, called multiplying these scores to rank priorities mathematically flawed. They also said FMEA teams need information beyond their members’ experience. [2] That is a different setting, but it fits our caution about relying on the scores alone.

How can you start a small pilot of your own?

These steps are our suggestion.

  • Pick a short, time-sensitive path and set a target time you can measure, such as five minutes.
  • Form a team of everyone who touches the path, and agree on what each score from 1 to 10 means before rating.
  • Watch for high severity even when it is unlikely, because it may rank lower.
  • Measure before the change with the same tool you will use after it. If the tool changes, record that.
  • Re-score after the change, and measure the error as well as the record, counting each specimen once. Decide who approves changes and who audits each week.

What does this case add to the 10X view?

The team ranked the risks on a time-sensitive path, and the two measures it tracked rose sharply after the redesign. The old assumption was that a paper register and a runner’s memory were enough for a five-minute path. A scan that timestamps each handover challenges that. Our inference is that what improved is timing and documentation. The study does not directly show that errors in specimen name, site, and side fell, which is the question behind its top scores.

What is your preventive engineering question?

In your time-sensitive paths, which step still depends on a paper record or someone’s memory? After a change, how will you know the error itself fell, not just the record?
