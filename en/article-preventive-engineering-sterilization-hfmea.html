FMEA · Failure Mode & Effects Analysis · Healthcare · Sterile Supply · Patient Safety

What happened to sterilization defects when a Chinese hospital applied FMEA?

Over four months in 2023, 185,382 packages, each a wrapped set of surgical instruments, passed through the central sterile supply department of West China Second University Hospital in Chengdu. The department logged 87 defects in the sterilization process. Some were packages that no one had scanned, so the tracking system recorded them as unsterilized; others were packages that came out of the sterilizer wet. These are process and record defects, not confirmed infections and not confirmed unsterile instruments reaching patients. The department applied Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA), which examines each step of a process for the ways it could fail, and addressed three problems with a set of practices. The next four months showed 11 defects among 189,531 packages. The study’s design, however, does not let us credit that drop to the changes alone.

What problem did the sterile supply department face?

A central sterile supply department (CSSD) cleans, sterilizes, and returns the surgical instruments that operating rooms and wards use every day. The study notes that reprocessed instruments are one possible source of surgical-site infection. Here, wrapped instrument sets go into a pressure steam sterilizer, a chamber that exposes them to hot, pressurized steam. [1]

The study itemized two kinds of defect among the 87. In 32 cases, staff had not scanned the package, so the tracking system recorded it as unsterilized. A package like that cannot be found quickly if a problem is discovered later. Ten packages came out wet, which can recontaminate a sterile package. The study did not itemize the other 45. [1]

How did the team analyze the process and score risk?

The department used the healthcare version of the method, HFMEA (Healthcare FMEA). Seven experienced staff formed the team, with advice from the sterilizer manufacturer’s engineers. The team mapped the process in four stages: pre-sterilization checks, loading, sterilization, and unloading. For each step, it listed the ways the step could fail (the failure modes). It then traced causes under five headings: people, machines, materials, environment, and method. [1]

The standard severity, occurrence, and detection (S/O/D) triad has three dimensions. Severity is how serious the consequence would be. Occurrence is how likely the failure is. Detection is how likely the current checks are to catch the failure before harm occurs; this team did not score it. The team scored the first two on four-point scales, and their product, from 1 to 16, is what the study calls the risk priority number (RPN). A failure mode counted as high risk if its RPN was 8 or more, or its severity was 4. The table below lists the highest-scoring failure modes from the study’s table. [1]

What did the department change?

The team picked three problems using these scores. It then rated candidate actions on a 1-3-5 scale for cost, benefit, and team capacity, and carried out those that cleared the team’s threshold. [1]

  • Staff were skipping the scan of instruments arriving from some hospital units. The sterile supply department added a prompt in its information system that sorts instruments by the unit they came from. It also completed the standard operating procedure (SOP) and posted it beside the workstations. Staff were trained, and compliance was tied to performance reviews.
  • The hand-over slip was not being checked against the system record. Quality supervisors began spot checks every day. The department also counted the next day’s scheduled surgeries in the system so it could add staff at peak times. The team’s plan also made a single failure to run the check disqualifying under staff evaluation rules; the study does not say who would be disqualified from what, or whether the rule was applied.
  • Packages were leaving the sterilizer before they had cooled for 30 minutes. There were too few instruments and racks, and the next batch had to be loaded promptly. The department bought more instruments and more racks.

Only one change is technology, a prompt in the system. In our reading, the rest changes how work is checked and what equipment is available.

What did the study measure afterward?

The team compared April to July 2023, before the changes, with August to November 2023, after them. Package volumes were nearly equal, so the counts can be compared directly. The table also shows P-values. A P-value estimates how often a difference this large would appear by chance if the changes had no effect; a small one means chance explains the difference poorly, though it does not show that the changes caused it. Overall defects fell from about 4.7 to about 0.6 per 10,000 packages, a drop of roughly 88% to about one-eighth of the earlier rate. Unscanned packages fell from 32 to 4, and wet packages from 10 to 2.

Where was the method completed, and where was it not?

FMEA is usually taught in nine stages. The table below, our summary of the study, shows how far the department got with each.

The nine stages as they appear in the study (our summary)
StageWhat the study reports
1. Define the scopePressure steam sterilization in one department.
2. List components and tasksA four-stage map with numbered sub-steps.
3. Failure modes, effects, and causesIdentified and traced to causes under five headings.
4. Current prevention and detectionTests that show whether sterilization worked, using microorganisms or chemical reactions, are mentioned, but no detection scores are shown for them.
5. Score the riskSeverity and occurrence only; no detection score.
6. Set prioritiesRPN of 8 or more, or severity of 4.
7. Design and carry out actionsThree problems; actions ranked on a 1-3-5 scale.
8. Verify effectivenessDefect counts compared across two periods; scores not reassessed.
9. Review and updateA daily checklist recording date, item, cause of anomaly, and responsible person; no long-term follow-up.

Analysis and action were largely completed; detection and re-scoring were not. The result therefore speaks to defect counts, since no re-scored risk is reported.

What we do not know

Confidence in this result is limited. This is a before-and-after study at one hospital, with no comparison group, and the two periods covered different months. [1]

  • The actions were introduced together, so we cannot tell which contributed most.
  • The study counted process defects (unscanned and wet packages). It did not measure microbial survival on instruments, patient infections, or instrument condition after sterilization.
  • It did not report risk scores after the changes, and it did not itemize the remaining defects (45 before, 5 after).
  • The authors acknowledge that the scores are the team’s subjective judgments, that follow-up was short, and that a Hawthorne effect is possible: staff may comply more because they are being watched.

How could you start a small pilot of your own?

  • Pick one process with a countable defect, and write down its denominator: how many units passed through the process.
  • Gather the people who do the work, and map the steps and failure modes together.
  • Score each failure mode on all three dimensions, including detection, which this study left out.
  • Change no more than three things, each with an owner and a date.
  • Measure the same defect against the same denominator for several months, rescore the risks after the change, and compare with a similar process you did not change, if you can.

What does this add to a 10X view?

The department did not need a new sterilizer, and its recorded defect rate fell to about one-eighth of its earlier level. That is a large change in a narrow, countable problem, not a tenfold improvement, and the study does not show that it lasted. The case suggests that prevention may pay off first in repeated steps that depend on someone remembering them, from one site’s experience.

The Preventive Engineering question

Which repeated step in your work depends on someone remembering it? What would it take for a system to prompt it, a supervisor to check it daily, and a monthly count to show whether it holds?
