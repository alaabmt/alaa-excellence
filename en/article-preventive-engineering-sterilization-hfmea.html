What Happened to Steam-Sterilization Defects After a Chinese Hospital’s Sterile Supply Department Applied FMEA?

Over four months in 2023, West China Second University Hospital in Chengdu sent 185,382 packages of surgical instruments through its central sterile supply department and logged 87 sterilization-quality defects. Among the ones the study itemized: a package that staff had not scanned into the tracking system, so it showed as unsterilized, and a package that came out of the sterilizer wet. After the department changed three practices, it logged just 11 defects among 189,531 packages in the next four months. The study, though, cannot attribute that drop to the changes alone.

What problem did the sterile supply department face?

A central sterile supply department (CSSD) cleans, sterilizes and returns the surgical instruments that operating rooms and wards use every day. The study notes that reprocessed instruments are one possible source of surgical-site infection. In this hospital, every load goes through a pressure steam sterilizer, a chamber that exposes wrapped instruments to hot steam under pressure. [1]

Between April and July 2023 the department logged 87 quality defects across 185,382 packages, about 4.7 per 10,000 (our calculation). The study itemized two kinds. In 32 cases staff had not scanned the package into the tracking system, so it showed as unsterilized, and a package that cannot be traced is hard to recall individually if a problem emerges later. Ten packages came out wet, which can recontaminate a sterile package. The other 45 defects were not itemized. [1]

How did the team analyze the process and score risk?

Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA) examines a process step by step before harm occurs: how could this step fail, what would follow, and why? The team used the healthcare version, HFMEA (Healthcare FMEA). Seven experienced staff, including nurses and a sterilization technician, formed the team, and engineers from the sterilizer’s manufacturer advised on equipment questions. The team mapped the process in four stages (pre-sterilization checks, loading, sterilization and unloading), listed the ways each step could fail (the failure modes), and traced causes under five headings: people, machines, materials, environment and method. [1]

Each failure mode received two scores on four-point scales: severity (how serious the consequence) and occurrence (how likely it is). Their product, from 1 to 16, is what the study calls the risk priority number (RPN). The third dimension of the usual severity, occurrence and detection (S/O/D) triad, detection, was not scored. A failure mode counted as high risk at an RPN of 8 or more, or at a severity of 4. The table below shows the highest-scoring failure modes from the study’s list. [1]

What did the department change?

The team chose three problems by these scores, then rated candidate actions on a 1-3-5 scale for cost, benefit and team capacity, and carried out those scoring above 84 points. [1]

  • To fix missed scanning by department, the department added department prompts to its information system, completed the standard operating procedure (SOP) and posted it beside the workstations, tied compliance to performance reviews and a reward-and-penalty system, and trained staff on why scanning matters.
  • To fix unchecked exchange slips, quality supervisors began spot checks every day, the department counted the next day’s scheduled surgeries in the system so it could add staff at peak times, and its plan provided that skipping the check would count as grounds for disqualification on a first offense.
  • To fix short cooling, it addressed the cause: racks were scarce, so crews could not wait 30 minutes before loading the next batch. The department increased its stock of instruments and bought more sterilizer racks.

Only one change is technology, and a small one: a prompt in the system. The rest changes how work is checked and resourced. A prompt replaces memory, a daily check replaces assumption, and extra racks remove the reason to hurry.

What did the study measure afterward?

The team compared April to July 2023, before the changes, with August to November 2023, after them. The number of packages was nearly the same in both periods, so the counts can be compared directly. Read each row of the table below across its “before” and “after” columns. [1]

The overall defect rate fell by about 88%, to roughly one-eighth of its earlier level. A P-value estimates how likely a difference this large would be by chance alone. A small one means chance explains it poorly, but it does not say the changes caused it.

Where was the method completed, and where was it not?

FMEA is usually taught in nine stages. The table below, our summary of the study, shows how far the department got with each.

The nine stages as they appear in the study (our summary)
StageWhat the study reports
1. Define the scopePressure steam sterilization in one department.
2. List components and tasksA four-stage map with numbered sub-steps.
3. Failure modes, effects and causesIdentified and traced to causes under five headings.
4. Current prevention and detectionBiological and chemical monitors are mentioned, but no detection scores are shown for them.
5. Score the riskSeverity and occurrence only; no detection score.
6. Set prioritiesRPN of 8 or more, or severity of 4.
7. Design and carry out actionsThree problems; actions ranked on a 1-3-5 scale.
8. Verify effectivenessDefect counts compared across two periods; scores not reassessed.
9. Review and updateA daily checklist recording date, item, cause of anomaly and responsible person; no long-term follow-up.

Analysis and action were completed; detection and re-scoring were not. The result is therefore a measure of defect counts, not of reduced calculated risk.

What we do not know

Confidence in this result: limited trial. The study is a before-and-after comparison at a single hospital with no comparison group, and the two periods covered different months. [1]

  • The actions were introduced together, so we cannot tell which contributed most.
  • The study counted process defects (unscanned and wet packages). It did not measure microbial survival on instruments, patient infections or instrument condition after sterilization.
  • It did not report risk scores after the changes, and it did not itemize the remaining defects (45 before, 5 after).
  • The authors acknowledge that the scores are the team’s subjective judgments, that follow-up was short, and that a Hawthorne effect is possible: staff may comply more because they are being watched.

How could you start a small pilot of your own?

  • Pick one process with a countable defect, and write down its denominator: how many units passed through the process.
  • Gather the people who do the work, map the steps together, and record failure modes and causes.
  • Score each failure mode on all three dimensions, including detection, which this study left out.
  • Change no more than three things, each with an owner and a date.
  • Measure the same defect against the same denominator for months, rescore after the change, and compare with a process you did not change if you can.

What does this add to a 10X view?

The department needed no new technology, and its recorded defect rate fell to about one-eighth of its earlier level. That is a large change in a narrow, countable problem, not a tenfold improvement, and the study does not show that it lasted. What the case does show is where prevention pays off first: in repeated steps that depend on someone remembering them.

The Preventive Engineering question

Which repeated step in your work depends on people remembering to do the right thing, and what would it take to turn it into a step that a system prompts, a supervisor checks every day, and you measure month after month against the same denominator?
