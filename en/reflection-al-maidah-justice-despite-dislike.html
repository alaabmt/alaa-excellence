Does Justice Change When We Disagree?

Justice is tested when fairness serves someone we oppose, not only when it confirms our preferences.

Justice can feel easy when a decision favors us or benefits someone we like. The harder test begins when fairness serves a person we distrust, a team we oppose, or an organization with which we have a difficult history. At that point, the question is no longer whether we understand justice. It is whether we will use the same measure when our emotions pull in another direction.

The Qur'an addresses that test directly:

“O you who have believed, be persistently standing firm for Allah, witnesses in justice, and do not let the hatred of a people prevent you from being just. Be just; that is nearer to righteousness. And fear Allah; indeed, Allah is Acquainted with what you do.”Al-Ma'idah 5:8 — Sahih International

The verse does not assume that hostility will disappear. Shana'an conveys intense dislike or enmity. What the verse forbids is allowing that hostility to become permission for injustice. Conflict does not alter what is right, and the force of our feelings does not reduce our duty to act justly.

Justice for Allah, not as a favor to others

The verse begins by calling believers to stand firm for Allah and bear witness with equity. Ibn Kathir explains that this means upholding the truth for Allah rather than for public approval, and testifying with justice rather than wrongdoing. Al-Tabari adds that believers must not wrong an enemy because of enmity or favor an ally because of loyalty. Both remain subject to the limits Allah has set.

This places justice on a different foundation. It is not a courtesy reserved for people we like, or a concession granted to those we oppose. It is an act of obedience to Allah. A believer cannot use emotion as a measure that enlarges or diminishes another person's rights.

The surrounding verses reinforce that responsibility. Al-Ma'idah 5:7 recalls the covenant to hear and obey, then reminds believers that Allah knows what is within the heart. Verse 8 closes by declaring that Allah is fully aware of what people do. Justice is therefore tested not only in public outcomes, but also before Allah's knowledge of the actions and motives behind them.

When hostility writes the verdict in advance

Al-Tabari reads the warning not to let hatred prevent justice as a prohibition against allowing enmity to produce unfair judgment or conduct. Ibn Kathir makes the same point: justice must be used with everyone, friend or enemy. Al-Qurtubi stresses that dislike does not permit unfairness toward an adversary. Al-Sa'di extends equity to words and actions, with those near and far, friends and opponents, without excess or neglect.

A related command appears near the opening of the surah: “do not let the hatred of a people for having obstructed you from al-Masjid al-Haram lead you to transgress” (Al-Ma'idah 5:2). A prior injury does not erase the boundary against aggression. Pain and anger may be real, but the limit remains a limit, and justice remains justice.

This invites a further reflection. Injustice may begin before anyone falsifies a fact. It can begin with a settled judgment that filters every new piece of information through an old conflict. A minor error becomes proof of a person's entire character. An incomplete record is treated as certainty. Evidence that confirms the previous view receives attention, while evidence that could correct it struggles to be heard.

The verse does not prevent accountability, and it does not ask us to treat truth and falsehood as equal. It prevents hatred from deciding the scale of guilt or punishment. Justice may establish wrongdoing and hold someone responsible, but it does not add what the evidence cannot support or withhold a right because its holder is disliked.

Why justice is nearer to taqwa

The verse says, “Be just; that is nearer to righteousness.” Al-Tabari explains that practicing justice is obedience to Allah and brings a person nearer to the people of taqwa—God-consciousness expressed through reverence, caution, and obedience.

It is easier to appear fair when fairness costs us nothing. The deeper test comes when justice requires us to acknowledge something good in an opponent, reject a popular accusation, or moderate a decision that the evidence does not justify. In that moment, justice becomes a struggle against the pull of the self rather than the routine application of a rule.

This does not mean that people can achieve perfect neutrality or know what is hidden in another person's heart. It means recognizing where our judgment may lean and refusing to turn that inclination into evidence. Taqwa does not erase emotion. It stops emotion from taking sole possession of the verdict.

An illustration: a complaint against a distrusted team

Consider a hypothetical workplace. A serious complaint is filed against a team that already has a difficult history with other departments. Some people enter the review convinced that the team cannot be trusted. Others defend it out of loyalty. The available documents are incomplete, and old stories begin to fill the gaps in the record.

Justice in such a situation may begin with simple questions. What exactly is being alleged? What do the documents establish, and what remains interpretation or suspicion? Have the people involved had a fair opportunity to respond? Could someone who is not burdened by the earlier conflict review the conclusion?

These questions are not a tafsir of the verse, nor are they a management system derived from it. They are possible human safeguards against biased judgment. They can also fail if they become empty procedure or a way to avoid responsibility. The enduring principle is that conflict does not entitle anyone to go beyond what the evidence supports.

What this can mean for Tamayuz 10X

An organization may invest in faster decisions, better data, and more sophisticated tools, yet remain incapable of fairness once conflict enters the room. More information does not produce better judgment when the conclusion has already been written in people's minds.

Excellence here can be understood as the ability to protect the integrity of a decision under pressure: keeping standards of evidence and accountability consistent, leaving room to correct a judgment, and recognizing when prior experience has become bias rather than insight. This is an ethical and human capability before it is a technical one.

The organizational connection remains a contemporary application, not a binding interpretation of the verse. Al-Ma'idah 5:8 addresses believers in their worship and obedience to Allah, and its command to justice and taqwa is wider than any workplace process. Review mechanisms are only imperfect human attempts to prevent conflict from becoming injustice.

The measure revealed by disagreement

The truth of our commitment to justice is not visible only when justice serves our preferences. It appears when we can say of an opponent: they were right here; this charge was not established; this right must be restored. That is not weakness. It is strength that prevents a position from corrupting the person who holds it.

The verse leaves us with a measure that does not change with the identity of the person before us: “Be just; that is nearer to righteousness.” When fairness benefits someone we oppose, do we examine the evidence by the same standard, or do we let hostility write the verdict before we have read the case?

Methodological boundary

This reflection distinguishes among the meaning established in recognized tafsir, the author's reflection, and the hypothetical organizational illustration. Contemporary review practices are not presented as a tafsir of the verse or as a management model extracted from it.
