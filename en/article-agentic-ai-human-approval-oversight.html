When a Person Approves an AI Agent's Actions, Is That Real Oversight or a Rubber Stamp?

Dr. Alaa Mohammad Ahmed

22 October 2026 · 7 min read

A vendor reassures the manager: nothing runs until a person approves it. But approval given with one click, dozens of times a day, can harden into a stamp. Anthropic reports that users of its coding tool accept 93% of permission prompts, and a systematic review of independent studies found that people sometimes follow a system's wrong advice and drop a correct decision they had already made. So what must a leader decide for approval to count as oversight?

What is the difference between decision support, automation, and autonomous decision?

An AI agent is software driven by a large language model (LLM) that takes a series of steps using tools it is given. The AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF), published by the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), describes three ways such a system can relate to a person: it can decide on its own, defer to a human expert, or serve as an extra opinion for a human decision maker. Published framework text [3]

This article sorts those relationships into three modes. In decision support, the agent recommends and a person decides. In automation, the agent carries out steps defined in advance, with no new decision. In autonomous decision, the agent chooses an action and carries it out before anyone reviews it. Approval of every action keeps the agent in decision support, but only if the approver is actually deciding.

What do the recognized frameworks ask of human oversight?

These sources do not treat a person's mere presence as enough. They ask whether that person understands the system, has the authority and time, and can refuse or halt it. One limit matters: Article 14 covers systems classified as high-risk, and being an agent does not make a system high-risk, since classification follows the area of use, such as employment. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 (July 2026) moved the Annex III start date from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027. [1][2]

What do we know about approval that turns into a habit?

A 2012 systematic review in the Journal of the American Medical Informatics Association (JAMIA) examined research on automation bias, the tendency to accept a system's output instead of checking it. Bias was associated with workload, time pressure, and task complexity; training, holding users accountable, and showing information rather than a recommendation reduced it. Established by an independent study [6]

The reviewers cited two examples. In one study, physicians overrode their own correct diagnosis for a support system's wrong advice in 6% of cases. In another, a correct unaided answer turned wrong after the advice in 21 of 272 cases. Established by an independent study [6]

For agents built on language models, Anthropic reported on 25 March 2026 that users of its coding tool, Claude Code, accept 93% of permission prompts. It called this approval fatigue: people stop paying attention to what they approve. It then introduced a mode that hands approval to an automated classifier, which its team said failed to block 17% of 52 real cases where the agent exceeded what the user had authorized. Company-reported [7]

What should a leader decide before naming a human approver?

The proposed decision: reserve human approval for actions the approver can really judge, and block the agent technically from everything else. This is our analysis, not a statement found in the sources. It connects to the five delegation limits on the Agentic AI 10X page: data, decision, action, money, and escalation.

  1. Sort the agent's actions into three groups: those it may never take because they cannot be undone, such as deleting live data or committing a large payment; those that need approval; and those that run without approval but are logged and reversible. The block belongs in the system, not the approval step; OWASP advises that downstream systems verify authorization themselves.
  2. Decide what the approver sees: what will change, what cannot be recovered, and the information behind the agent's proposal. In the review, showing information rather than a ready recommendation reduced bias.
  3. Cap the requests one person reviews each day and track the share accepted. Near-total acceptance may show that no one is checking, not that the agent is right. Planting a known-bad request to test attention is our suggestion.
  4. Give approval to someone with the competence, authority, and time, and let that person refuse a request or halt the agent without penalty for the delay, as Articles 14 and 26 require for the systems they cover.
  5. Log every request and human decision, review the log regularly, and adjust permissions accordingly, as NIST's post-deployment monitoring calls for.

The limits of what we know

I found no independent study measuring rubber-stamp approval among users of language-model agents. The 93% figure comes from the tool's maker, its method is unpublished, and it does not say how many requests were sound.

The systematic review mostly concerns clinical decision-support systems and searched the literature in 2009 and 2010, so it applies to agents only by analogy. It also finds the effect of training unsettled and few studies that deliberately tested wrong advice.

Framework texts show what their authors ask for, not what has been proved to work, and Article 14 does not cover every agent. The five decisions are this article's analysis, and I found no published test of them on agents. That the classifier failed to block 17% of cases does not mean humans do better; I found no published comparison. [1][2][3][6][7]

The 10X Lens

Agents are heading toward thousands of steps a day, and no person can approve each one. An organization that builds divided permissions, logs people actually review, and a working stop control today, and reserves human approval for decisions that deserve it, reaches that future with a capability an approver at every step cannot supply. The first step is to sort one agent's actions into the three groups. See the Agentic AI 10X page.

Key Terms

Human Oversight
A person's ability to understand how a system works, monitor it, disregard its output, or stop it.
Automation Bias
The tendency to accept a system's output instead of checking it.
Approval Fatigue
People stop paying attention to what they approve after too many requests.
Systematic Review
A review that gathers the available studies on one question by a stated method and summarizes their results.
High-Risk AI System
A legal category in EU law covering systems used in listed areas, such as employment.

The Question That Remains

If, years from now, your organization runs agents that take thousands of steps a day, what will human oversight look like in practice when no person can approve every step? What will the organization be able to stop and trace then that it cannot today? Which permissions, logs, skills, and ways of working should you start building now?
