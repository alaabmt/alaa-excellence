Operational Excellence Evidence

Why Do Teams Fix the Same Problem Every Day Without It Ever Going Away?

Dr. Alaa Mohammad Ahmed

18 October 2026 · 7 min read

In hospitals known for strong nursing care, staff worked around most everyday problems and kept going without telling anyone. More than 20 years later, a new study found nearly the same pattern. The lesson for leaders: individual dedication is not enough for a system to learn.

Back to articles

This article draws on a field study published in 2003 and two recent studies published in 2023 and 2026. All three took place in hospitals. Applying the idea to other organizations is the author's inference.

What did the researchers see in the hospitals?

Researchers Anita Tucker and Amy Edmondson spent 239 hours observing 26 nurses in nine U.S. hospitals, most of them chosen for their reputation for nursing excellence. They recorded 194 breakdowns in everyday work. None of the problems were complex. A medication had not arrived, a diabetic patient got a regular meal tray, supplies had run out, or an order was missing. [1]

For 93% of the problems, staff did just enough to finish the task and moved on, without telling anyone or looking for the cause. Only 7% of responses alerted someone who could fix the cause, even though the researchers used a generous definition. They estimated that about 33 minutes of each eight-hour shift went to coping with failures that could have been removed. [1]

This was not a lack of effort. Nine of the 10 nurses observed for a full shift stayed an average of 45 minutes after it ended, without extra pay, to finish caring for their patients. [1]

Why doesn't the system learn from these problems?

The researchers point to three practices that look positive on the surface. Nurses are trained to take personal responsibility and solve problems on their own, so each person fixes their own situation and the system never changes. Staffing is set to cover the work with little slack, so there is no time to trace causes. And empowering teams to run themselves took managers off the floor, leaving nurses alone with problems that started in other departments. [1]

There is a further trap. Working around an obstacle feels satisfying and competent, which makes people less likely to involve others. Seventy percent of the nurses interviewed said their manager expected them to deal with these disruptions on their own. On the surface, things look stable. Underneath, fatigue builds up and the same problems come back. [1]

Has anything changed in more than 20 years?

In a study published in 2026, researchers at Ghent University in Belgium gave 80 nurses two tasks in a simulated setting with built-in operational failures. Participants noticed the failures 96.2% of the time. But behavior that addressed the cause, such as formal reporting or escalating the problem, appeared in only 21.9% of cases. [2]

The researchers also tested whether emotion would help. A randomly chosen group listened to a moving patient-safety story before the tasks. The story stirred strong feelings, but it did not increase reporting. Participants named three barriers: deviations had become normal, time pressure, and no feedback on what happened after they reported something. What they found helpful was team debriefs, audits with feedback to the team, and simulation training on failures. [2]

A larger study, published in 2023, surveyed 11,709 nurses in 415 U.S. hospitals. More frequent operational failures were linked to lower patient satisfaction, worse ratings of quality and safety, and more burnout and job dissatisfaction among staff. The work environment explained part of this link. [3]

What can leaders do?

These steps come from the recommendations of the three studies. They need management decisions more than budget. [1][2][3]

  1. Be present where the work happens during every shift, even briefly, so small problems reach you before they pile up.
  2. Make reporting a problem safe, never a sign that someone can't do the job, and admit your own mistakes in front of the team.
  3. Answer every report with an action the team can see, and tell them what changed because of it. People stop reporting when they believe no one is listening.
  4. Set aside a regular, short debrief after work and ask: what got in our way today, and who can fix it?
  5. Track operational failures, such as missing medications, missing supplies, and incomplete orders, as a quality measure instead of waiting for them to show up in patient complaints.

Emotional awareness campaigns are not enough on their own. In the 2026 study, the moving story raised awareness but did not change behavior. [2]

What we don't know

The 2003 study is qualitative, based on observation and interviews, with a small sample. Its percentages cover only the problems seen during observation. [1] The 2026 study took place in a simulation, not on a real ward, with 80 participants. [2] The 2023 study is cross-sectional and relies on nurses' own ratings, so it shows association, not cause. [3]

The three studies come from U.S. and Belgian hospitals and did not include hospitals in the Arab region. Applying the idea to call centers, production lines, or service teams is a reasonable inference, but these studies did not test it.

The 10X lens

An organization that relies on individual heroics pays for the same problem every day. The real shift comes when every obstacle an employee meets turns into information that reaches someone who can remove it. Each report then becomes an investment that cuts tomorrow's work. At that point, a team is judged not by how well it works around problems, but by how fast they disappear.

Key terms

Operational failure
When the work system fails to provide the information, supplies, or services staff need, when and where they need them.
First-order problem solving
Working around an obstacle to finish the task, without dealing with its cause.
Second-order problem solving
Finishing the task, then alerting the responsible department or manager so the cause is removed and the problem does not come back.
Normalization of deviance
When departures from the correct procedure happen so often that the team starts to see them as normal.

The question that remains

Three years from now, do you want your organization to be a place where people skillfully work around problems every day, or a place where a problem disappears after the first report? If that second future is the one you want, what do you need to build today so that reporting a problem becomes easier than working around it?
