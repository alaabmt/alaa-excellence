Tawam: How Redesigning the Bariatric Surgery Journey Cut Operating Room Time and Hospital Stays

Tawam Hospital in Al Ain is a government specialty hospital in the UAE. In bariatric surgery, its team reorganized the patient journey before, during and after the operation, instead of managing each stage separately.

Executive Snapshot

A peer-reviewed study compared 462 patients treated before the program, in 2010–2014, with 1,602 patients treated after it was introduced, in 2015–2017. For sleeve gastrectomy, the average hospital stay fell from 3.2 days to 1.5 days, and the patient’s time in the operating room fell from 2 hours 27 minutes to 1 hour 23 minutes.[1]

The Challenge

Bariatric surgery helps people with severe obesity lose weight and improve related conditions. The study covered two common procedures:

Sleeve gastrectomy: an operation that removes a large part of the stomach, making it smaller. Patients can eat less, and other changes follow that help them lose weight.
Gastric bypass: an operation that creates a small stomach pouch and connects it to a lower part of the intestine, so that patients eat less and absorb part of their food differently.

But the operation is only one part of a journey that includes preparation, anesthesia, pain control, fluids, getting moving again, nutrition, discharge and follow-up. When each stage takes longer than it should, operating room hours, beds and clinical time are used up. The operational question becomes: does the organization always need more operating rooms and beds, or can it redesign the current journey to make it faster and safer?

The 10X MoveThe change was not in a single surgical step. It was bringing the whole recovery journey together around one clear protocol.

The Decisive Shift

Enhanced recovery after bariatric surgery (ERABS)is a structured care pathway that coordinates what happens before, during and after surgery so that recovery is safe and more efficient. Published ERABS protocols include practices such as shorter fasting before surgery, avoiding excess fluids, getting patients moving and eating early, and better control of pain and nausea.[2]

At Tawam, the program was applied as a standard pathway for patients having laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy or gastric bypass, and the results were compared with the period before it was introduced.[1]

What the Evidence Shows

For sleeve gastrectomy, the patient’s average time in the operating room fell from 2:27 to 1:23, and the hospital stay from 3.2 to 1.5 days. Major complications fell from 13.8% to 0.8%. For gastric bypass, operating room time fell from 3:17 to 1:59, and the hospital stay from 3.5 to 1.7 days.[1]

There was no statistically significant difference in readmissions or reoperations between the two periods for either procedure. The researchers concluded that introducing the standardized program was feasible and safe, and that it was associated with shorter operating room time and hospital stays.[1]

Evidence limits: The study is retrospective and compares two periods; it is not a randomized trial. It gives strong operational support for the link between ERABS and the improvement, but it does not prove that the program alone caused every difference, since experience and practice may also have changed over the years.

The 10X Lens

Value and strategic direction

Value here is not faster discharge alone; it is shorter time and hospital stays while complications, readmissions and reoperations stay under control.

Capability multiplication and empowerment

Shorter operating room time and hospital stays free up existing capacity to serve other patients, without assuming a matching increase in operating rooms and beds.

Operating-model redesign

The change covered the patient journey across several stages, not the improvement of a single step inside the operating room.

Value and sustainable results

The comparison combined efficiency and safety indicators, so success is not reduced to a shorter length of stay alone.

What Leaders and Organizations Can Use

Map the whole patient journey

Find where patients wait, where steps are repeated, and what slows the move between preparation, surgery, recovery and discharge.

Standardize what can be standardized

Turn recurring, evidence-based practices into one clear pathway for the team, instead of different decisions in every case.

Measure efficiency and safety together

Cutting time or length of stay is not enough; track complications, readmissions and reoperations at the same time.

Test existing capacity before buying new capacity

Before adding beds or operating room time, check how much capacity can be freed by removing unnecessary waiting and variation.

Where the Principle Transfers

The principle of a single standard pathway can transfer to other health services where the journey is frequent and measurable, such as some elective operations. The protocol details cannot be copied automatically; they must be adapted to the patient group, clinical evidence, resources and local safety policies.

The 10X QuestionBefore your organization adds beds, staff or extra time, what could be redesigned in the service journey to make better use of the capacity it already has?
