Case 10X UAE Health Excellence

Tawam: How did the redesign of the bariatric surgery trip reduce operating room time and accommodation?

Tawam Hospital in Al Ain is a state-of-the-art specialty hospital in the UAE. In bariatric surgery, his team has reorganized the patient’s journey before, during and after surgery, rather than dealing with each stage separately.

Executive Snapshot

A trial study compared 462 patients before the program was implemented during 20102014 with 1,602 patients after it was applied during 20152017. In gastric sleeve, the average stay decreased from 3.2 days to 1.5 days, and the patient's time in the operating room decreased from 2 hours and 27 minutes to 1 hour and 23 minutes.[1]

Challenge

Bariatric surgery is an operation designed to help severely obese patients lose weight and improve related diseases. The study looked at two common procedures:

Sleeve Gastrectomy: Surgery in which a large part of the stomach is removed to become smaller, reducing the amount of food that can be eaten and making changes that help reduce weight.
Gastric bypass: Surgery in which a small stomach is created and attached to a faraway part of the intestine, so that food intake decreases and absorption of part of it changes.

But surgery is only part of a journey that includes preparation, anesthesia, pain, fluid, movement, nutrition, and exit and follow-up. When each stage is too long, operating room hours, beds, and clinical time are consumed. So the operational question becomes: Does the organization always need more rooms and beds, or can it redesign the current journey to become faster and safer?

The 10X Move The Decisive ShiftThe shift was not in one surgical procedure; it was in uniting the entire recovery journey around one clear protocol.

The Decisive Shift

ERABS Enhanced Recovery After Bariatric Surgery Enhanced Recovery After Bariatric SurgeryERABS is a structured care pathway that coordinates what happens before, during, and after surgery with the goal of making recovery safer and more efficient. ERABS protocols published include practices such as shortening fasting before surgery, avoiding excess fluid, early movement and nutrition, and improving pain and nausea control.[2]

In twins, the program was standardised for gastric sleeve and laparoscopic bypass patients, and the results were then compared to the pre-application period.[1]

Impact Guide

In gastric sleeve, the average patient's time inside the operating room decreased from 2:27 to 1:23, and accommodation from 3.2 to 1.5 days. Major complications also decreased from 13.8% to 0.8%. In diversion the operating room time decreased from 3:17 to 1:59, and accommodation from 3.5 to 1.7 days.[1]

There was no statistically significant difference in re-entry or re-operations between the two periods in either procedure. The researchers concluded that the application of the standard program was feasible and safe and was associated with reduced operating room and accommodation time.[1]

Directory Limits: The study is retrospective and compares two time periods, not a randomized trial. It supports the ERABS application's link to improvement with operational strength, but it does not prove that the program alone causes every difference; there may be other changes in experience and practice over the years.

Lens 10X

Redefining Value and Strategic Direction

The value here is not the speed of exit alone; it is the reduction of time and accommodation with the control of complications, re-entry and re-operations.

Capacity Building and Empowerment

Reducing operating room time and accommodation frees up existing capacity that can be used to serve other patients, without assuming a similar increase in rooms and beds.

Reinventing the organization and its business model

The change involved the patient’s journey through multiple stages, not optimizing a single step within the operating room.

Maximizing Value and Sustainable Results

The comparison combines efficiency and safety indicators, which prevents reducing the success in reducing the length of stay only.

What can be used practically at the level of the leader and the organization?

Draw the whole patient journey.

Determine where the patient is waiting, where the steps are repeated, and what is delaying the transition between preparation, surgery, recovery, and exit.

Unify what can be unified.

Turn the repetitive practices supported by the manual into a clear path for the team rather than diverging decisions in each case.

Measure efficiency and safety together.

It’s not enough to cut down on time or accommodation; watch for complications, re-entry and re-operations in parallel.

Test the ability before buying a new one.

Before adding beds or operating room time, check how much power can be freed by removing unnecessary waiting and contrast.

Portability

The Single Path principle can be transferred to other health services when the journey is frequent and measurable, such as some elective surgeries. But the protocol details are not automatically copied; they must be adapted to patient type, clinical evidence, resources, and local safety policies.
