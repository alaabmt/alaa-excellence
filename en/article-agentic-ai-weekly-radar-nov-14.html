What did companies announce this week for building, testing, and controlling AI agents?

Dr. Alaa Mohammad Ahmed

November 17, 2026 · 6 min read

Three announcements on November 10 and 11, 2026, and a report published this month, speak to anyone building AI agents in their organization: a platform from IBM and Microsoft, a tool that checks every action before it runs, a platform that tests an agent on thousands of simulated users before launch, and a report on how freelance work is changing. Four announcements for anyone building AI agents

On November 10, IBM and Microsoft launched a platform for building and running AI agents, and the start-up Bay announced a tool that checks every action an agent proposes. On November 11, Plurai announced a platform that tests agents before launch. A joint OpenAI and Upwork report added a look at how freelance work is changing.

What did each organization announce?

On November 10, IBM and Microsoft announced Imagine Enterprise, a platform to build, run, and govern agents for industry-specific processes. IBM Consulting supplies more than 6,000 agents and assets. Microsoft supplies the AI, data, cloud, and security foundation, including identity, governance, and protection tools. It starts with frameworks for financial services, insurance, and retail; the companies name other industries for later, without dates. The platform includes controls, monitoring, and compliance support, and a client can have IBM Consulting run it or manage it itself. SEI, a financial technology firm, is exploring these technologies with IBM. [1][2]

Also on November 10, Bay launched with $50 million: $37 million in a Series A (a company's first major funding round) led by Battery Ventures, and $13 million in seed funding (a company's earliest funding) led by YL Ventures and Striker. Bay inventories the agents, coding assistants, tool servers, skills, extensions, and local models in use. It follows each session from the first prompt to the last action. Its Enforcer component evaluates every proposed action in the context of the whole session. In milliseconds it allows the action, refers it to the user, redirects it, or denies it. Bay deploys across a fleet in under five minutes, according to the announcement. [3]

On November 11, Plurai announced $13 million in seed funding led by Team8, along with general availability of its platform. The platform makes a simulated copy of a customer's agent and its environment. It runs thousands of realistic and hostile simulated users against the copy and looks for policy violations, invented answers (hallucinations), and data leaks. It then suggests fixes. Plurai's early customers cut failures that reached real users by 82% and cut the cost of running reliable agents by 97%, according to the release. [4]

In November, OpenAI and Upwork published "The Rebundling of Freelance Work in the AI Age," based on 36,896 ChatGPT conversations and Upwork postings. AI-related projects ask for 3.35 tasks each, against 3.19 for other projects. Pairs of tasks that clients rarely requested together before make up 8.4% of task pairs in AI-related projects, against 2.4% elsewhere. Their fixed-price budgets are about 52% higher after adjusting for other factors. The report describes a continuing role for workers who deliver implementation and finished work. [5]

What can a leader try this week?

Here are four steps a leader can start with:

  1. Before the first launch, write down the agent's tasks, the decisions it makes itself, and the ones it only suggests to a person. The platform that builds it will not decide that for you.
  2. Test the agent before it reaches customers. Have a team play ordinary and hostile users, and record the failures they find. Plurai's platform takes this approach.
  3. Set a rule for each action. For every action the agent proposes, decide whether to allow it, send it to a person, or deny it, and keep a log the agent cannot change. Bay's tool works this way.
  4. Plan for the whole project. The report says AI-related projects bundle a wider mix of tasks, so name one person accountable for the finished product, whoever carries out each task.

The 10X lens

Before launch, write down the agent's tasks, test it on simulated users, set a rule for each action, name a person accountable for the result, and measure that result before scaling. For more, see the Agentic AI 10X page.

Key terms

AI agent
Software that plans and carries out steps with tools and permissions a person grants.
Simulated user
A persona generated by software to talk to an agent and test it, sometimes by trying to provoke it.
Rebundling
A change in how tasks are grouped together in a single project.
Seed funding
A company's earliest outside funding.

A question to ask your team

If you launched your first AI agent tomorrow, would you know how to test it before it reaches customers, and how to stop it if it goes wrong?

The limits of what we know

Three of the four items are announcements from organizations that sell these solutions, or press reports of them. We know of no independent evaluation of any product and no independent performance figures. The figure of 6,000 for the IBM and Microsoft platform counts agents together with "assets," and the release uses both phrases in different places. Its one named client, SEI, is exploring only, and the release reports no results.

The deployment speed and the results that Bay and Plurai give (under five minutes, 82%, and 97%) are the companies' own figures. Plurai's release gives a cost advantage of 40 and 44 times in two places and "about 44" in its headline, so its figures are not consistent. Details of Bay's funding reached us through a trade publication.

The OpenAI and Upwork report describes associations, not causes. It does not establish a causal effect of AI, its ChatGPT and Upwork data are not matched, it does not represent the whole labor market, it does not count lost jobs, and it calls its task-diversity analysis an early baseline. We read the full report itself because the announcement page blocked automated access. We read the IBM and Microsoft release on both companies' sites, the Microsoft one through an automated summary of the page.

We found no independent incident or study this week on testing or controlling agents, and we left out other announcements because we opened only secondary coverage of them. This radar reports market developments. It is not an assessment or endorsement of any product. [1]–[5]
