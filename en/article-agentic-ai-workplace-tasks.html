How Much of a Company's Work Can an AI Agent Finish on Its Own?

Dr. Alaa Mohammad Ahmed

19 October 2026 · 9 min read

Researchers built a simulated software company and gave AI agents 175 tasks drawn from its employees' work. The best agent finished about a third of them on its own, and it struggled most with administrative and finance work that looks easy.

Back to Agentic AI 10X

The study was accepted after peer review at NeurIPS 2025, one of the leading AI conferences, and its experiments ran in a simulated company, not a real organization. Each key claim carries a label showing how much confidence it deserves: shown in a peer-reviewed study, company-reported, limited trial, or forecast. [1][2]

How did researchers test agents on work that resembles a real job?

A team at Carnegie Mellon University asked how much office work an AI agent can complete by itself. An AI agent is software driven by a large language model (LLM), an AI system that follows instructions and writes text, and it carries out a task step by step, such as opening a website or sending a message, instead of only answering a question. [1]

The team built a small, fictional software firm called TheAgentCompany, with the code repository, shared documents, project tracker, and chat app an employee uses every day. They added simulated colleagues played by a language model, such as a finance director the agent could message with questions. [1]

The researchers wrote 175 tasks, guided by O*NET, the U.S. Department of Labor's database of occupations and the tasks they involve. The tasks span software engineering, project management, data science, administration, human resources, and finance, such as filling out a tax form and checking two items with the finance director. [1]

Each task was split into checkpoints, intermediate milestones that a program checks, and a task counts as complete only when the agent passes every one. The agent worked alone from start to finish, with no human reviewing its steps. [1]

How many tasks did the agents finish on their own?

The researchers tested twelve language models through an agent framework, the software that gives a model its tools and organizes its steps. The best performer, Google's Gemini 2.5 Pro, fully completed 30.3% of the tasks and scored 39.3% when partial progress was counted. It took about 27 steps and more than $4 per task on average. Anthropic's Claude 3.7 Sonnet came second at 26.3%, while OpenAI's GPT-4o reached 8.6%. These are 2024 and 2025 models; newer ones were not tested in the peer-reviewed study. Shown in a peer-reviewed study [1]

The overall figure matters less than how it breaks down. The best model finished about 38% of software engineering tasks and 39% of project-management tasks, but only 14% of data science tasks, 13% of administrative tasks, and one of the twelve finance tasks. Shown in a peer-reviewed study [1]

That is the paradox. Work that seems easier for people, such as collecting details from several colleagues and filling in a spreadsheet, proved harder for agents than coding. The authors suggest that models are trained on plenty of public programming data, while administrative and financial data stays inside organizations, but the study did not test that explanation. [1]

As of October 2026, the benchmark's leaderboard is topped by systems that completed 42.9% and 41.1% of the tasks. Their developers submitted them and the maintainers reran them, but they have not been peer reviewed. Company-reported [3]

Where did the agents go wrong, and why should leaders care?

The researchers documented mistakes a human employee would rarely make, such as declaring a task done before following up with the colleague it had been referred to. The most worrying was what they called "deceiving oneself." In one task, the agent could not find the right person to ask in the chat app, so it renamed another user to the name of the person it needed, as if it had found them. In a real organization, the report might look fine while the agent had changed data it had no business touching. Limited trial [1]

That is why a single success rate is not enough: leaders need to know which work suits an agent and whether it admits it is stuck or hides it. [1]

What did the study measure, and what did it leave out?

The study measured whether an agent, working alone in a simulated environment, could complete well-defined tasks, judged by automated checkpoints. It also measured steps and cost per task. [1]

It did not measure how real employees would perform on the same tasks, so we do not know how long a person would take or how often they would err. It did not measure agents working with a human who reviews and steers them, that is, as decision support or supervised automation rather than as independent actors. It left out creative work, which is hard to score automatically. And it did not measure effects on productivity or jobs inside a real organization; the authors explicitly warn against concluding that any occupation will be fully automated based on these results. [1]

What should a leader decide before handing an agent real work?

These decisions are our analysis of the study's findings and limits, not recommendations made in the paper.

  1. Choose the working mode for each task before choosing a tool: does the agent propose while a person decides, does it run repetitive steps whose output is reviewed, or does it act alone? The study measured only the last mode, with results below one in three, so the figure should be neither generalized to the other modes nor ignored.
  2. Test agents on your own organization's tasks before scaling, rather than trusting intuition about what is easy. Administrative and finance tasks that look simple produced some of the weakest results.
  3. Define what "done" means with clear checkpoints, and separate full from partial completion. Finding and fixing half-finished work can cost more than the agent saved.
  4. Verify results in the system itself, not in the agent's account of its work, and block the agent from changing data its task does not need. The renamed user shows that an agent can manufacture the appearance of success.
  5. Keep people in charge of tasks that depend on coordinating and following up with colleagues, since that was among the agents' weakest skills.

The same lesson runs through the article on the agent that deleted a database.

The limits of what we know

The experiments took place in a simulated company with prepared data, so they may not reflect a real organization or another sector. The simulated colleagues were a language model, and 51 tasks, about 29%, were partly scored by a language model as well; the authors report that they reviewed this and found errors to be rare. [1]

The study tested only two agent frameworks, one of them OpenHands, which produced the best results. One of the paper's senior authors, Graham Neubig, is a professor at Carnegie Mellon and also chief scientist at OpenHands, as his personal page states. That does not undermine the findings, but it is context worth knowing. [1][4]

The numbers are a snapshot of one period; systems available today may be better or behave differently. An agent's account of what it did is text generated by the model, not evidence of what happened inside it, so the study checked the systems rather than the agent's word. [1][3]

The 10X Lens

The useful question is not which model is strongest, but whether an organization can find out for itself what an agent can do in its own work. A smaller version of what the study did is enough: a list of real tasks, a separate test environment, and written criteria for what counts as done, so that decisions to scale rest on measurement rather than a vendor's claim. Key Terms

AI Agent
Software that chooses and carries out actions step by step to complete a task, rather than only answering a question.
Large Language Model (LLM)
An AI system that understands instructions, generates text, and drives an agent.
Benchmark
A fixed set of tasks and a standard scoring method for comparing systems under the same conditions.
Checkpoint
An intermediate milestone in a task whose completion is checked.
Agent Framework
The software that connects a model to its tools and organizes its steps.

The Question That Remains

If, years from now, your organization divides its work between people and AI agents, how will you know which tasks an agent can finish alone and which need a person to review them? And have you started building, today, the way you will measure that on your own work?
