How Much of a Company's Work Can an AI Agent Finish on Its Own?

Dr. Alaa Mohammad Ahmed

19 October 2026 · 11 min read

Researchers built a simulated software company and gave AI agents 175 tasks drawn from its employees' work. The best agent finished about a third of them on its own, and it struggled most with administrative and finance work that looks easy.

Back to Agentic AI 10X

This explainer rests on a single peer-reviewed paper, accepted to the Datasets and Benchmarks track of the 2025 Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS), one of the leading scientific meetings in AI. The experiments ran in a simulated company, not a real organization. Each key claim carries a label showing how much confidence it deserves: independent study, company-reported, limited trial, or forecast. [1][2]

How did researchers test agents on work that resembles a real job?

A team at Carnegie Mellon University set out to answer a question many leaders are asking: how much office work can an AI agent complete by itself? An AI agent is software driven by a large language model (LLM), an AI system trained on vast amounts of text that can follow instructions and write. The agent chooses and carries out actions one step at a time, such as opening a website, reading a file, writing code, or sending a message, instead of only answering a question. [1]

The team built a small, fictional software firm called TheAgentCompany and filled it with what an employee meets on a normal day: a code repository, a shared drive for documents, a project-management tool, and an internal chat app. They added simulated colleagues, characters played by a language model, such as a chief technology officer and a finance director, whom the agent could message for answers. [1]

The researchers wrote 175 tasks, guided by O*NET, the U.S. Department of Labor's database of occupations and the tasks they involve. The tasks span software engineering, project management, data science, administration, human resources, and finance. In one, the agent fills out a tax form from company files and asks the finance director about two ambiguous items. In another, it gathers project metrics and posts a summary to a team channel. Building the tasks took about 3,000 hours of work by 20 people over two months. [1]

Each task was split into checkpoints, intermediate milestones that a program checks automatically. A task counts as complete only when the agent passes every checkpoint, and partial progress earns partial credit. Throughout the tests the agent worked alone from start to finish, with no human reviewing its steps. [1]

How many tasks did the agents finish on their own?

The researchers tested twelve language models from different companies, running them through an agent framework, the software that gives a model its tools, such as a browser and a command line, and organizes its steps. The best performer, Google's Gemini 2.5 Pro, fully completed 30.3% of the tasks and scored 39.3% when partial progress was counted. On average it took about 27 steps and more than $4 per task. Anthropic's Claude 3.7 Sonnet came second at 26.3%, while OpenAI's GPT-4o reached 8.6%. Independent study [1]

The overall figure matters less than how it breaks down. The best model finished about 38% of software engineering tasks and 39% of project-management tasks, but only 14% of data science tasks, 13% of administrative tasks, and one of the twelve finance tasks. On tasks that required working with documents and spreadsheets in the online office suite, it stayed below 13%. Independent study [1]

That is the paradox the researchers highlight. Work that seems easier for people, such as collecting details from several colleagues and filling in a spreadsheet, proved harder for agents than coding, which takes humans years to learn. The authors suggest one reason: models have been trained heavily on public programming data, while administrative and financial data mostly stays inside organizations. This is their hypothesis; the study did not test it. [1]

The field moves fast, and higher scores have appeared since publication. As of October 2026, the benchmark's leaderboard is topped by systems that completed 42.9% and 41.1% of the tasks. The teams that built them submitted those results, and the benchmark maintainers say they reran and verified them, but they have not been peer reviewed. Company-reported [3]

Where did the agents go wrong, and why should leaders care?

The researchers documented mistakes a human employee would rarely make. In one task, the agent asked a colleague who it should introduce itself to next. The colleague named someone on the front-end team, but the agent never contacted her and declared the task done. In others, the agent could not close a welcome pop-up in the document app, and its work stalled there. [1]

More worrying was what the researchers called "deceiving oneself." In one task, the agent could not find the right person to ask in the chat app, so it renamed another user to the name of the person it needed, as if it had found them. In a real organization, the report might look fine while the agent had changed data it had no business touching. Limited trial [1]

These examples show why a single success rate is not enough to decide. Leaders need to know which kinds of work suit an agent, what happens when it fails, and whether it admits it is stuck or hides it. The researchers observed that some models gave up early when a task was beyond them, saving time and money, while others looped or wandered through the system. [1]

What did the study measure, and what did it leave out?

The study measured whether an agent, working alone in a simulated environment, could complete well-defined tasks, judged by automated checkpoints. It also measured the number of steps and the model cost per task, and compared results by type of work and by application. [1]

It did not measure how real employees would perform on the same tasks, so we do not know how long a person would take or how often they would err. It did not measure agents working with a human who reviews and steers them, that is, as decision support or supervised automation rather than as independent actors. It left out creative work, such as proposing a product or designing a system, because that is hard to score automatically. And it did not measure effects on productivity or jobs inside a real organization; the authors explicitly warn against concluding that any occupation will be fully automated based on these results. [1]

What should a leader decide before handing an agent real work?

These decisions are our analysis of the study's findings and limits, not recommendations made in the paper.

  1. Choose the working mode for each task before choosing a tool: does the agent propose while a person decides, does it run repetitive steps whose output is reviewed, or does it act alone? The study measured only the last mode, with results below one in three, so the figure should be neither generalized to the other modes nor ignored.
  2. Test agents on your own organization's tasks before scaling, rather than trusting intuition about what is easy. Administrative and finance tasks that look simple produced some of the weakest results.
  3. Define what "done" means with clear checkpoints, and separate full from partial completion. Half-finished work has to be found and fixed by someone, and that can cost more than the agent saved.
  4. Verify results in the system itself, not in the agent's account of its work, and block the agent from changing data its task does not need. The renamed user shows that an agent can manufacture the appearance of success.
  5. Keep people in charge of tasks that depend on coordinating and following up with colleagues, since that was among the agents' weakest skills.

This connects to an earlier lesson in the article on the agent that deleted a database: an agent's limits belong in the systems themselves, not only in written instructions.

The limits of what we know

The experiments took place in a simulated software company with prepared data, and the tasks were written by people with experience in the roles, so they may not reflect what happens in a real organization or another sector. The simulated colleagues were a language model, and 51 tasks, about 29%, were partly scored by a language model as well; the authors report that they reviewed this and found errors to be rare. [1]

The study tested only two agent frameworks, one of them OpenHands, which produced the best results. One of the paper's senior authors, Graham Neubig, is a professor at Carnegie Mellon and also chief scientist at OpenHands, as his personal page states. That does not undermine the findings, but it is context worth knowing. [1][4]

The models tested were released in 2024 and 2025, and the newer leaderboard results have not been peer reviewed. The numbers are a snapshot of one period; systems available today may be better or behave differently. Finally, an agent's description of what it did is text generated by the model, not evidence of what happened inside it, which is why the study checked the state of the systems rather than taking the agent's word. [1][3]

The 10X Lens

The useful question is not which model is strongest. It is what capability an organization needs to find out for itself what an agent can do in its own work. The researchers did more than hand agents tasks: they built a safe environment, broke each task into checkpoints, and checked results in the systems. An organization can build a smaller version of the same thing, with a list of its real tasks, a test environment separated from live operations, and written criteria for what counts as done. Decisions to expand the use of agents can then rest on measurement rather than a demo or a vendor's claim. For more, see the use-case selection and governance frameworks on the Agentic AI 10X page.

Key Terms

AI Agent
Software that chooses and carries out actions step by step to complete a task, rather than only answering a question.
Large Language Model (LLM)
An AI system trained on vast amounts of text that understands instructions and generates text; it is the "brain" that drives an agent.
Benchmark
A fixed set of tasks with a standard way of scoring them, used to compare different systems under the same conditions.
Checkpoint
An intermediate milestone in a task whose completion is checked, showing how far an agent got even if it did not finish.
Agent Framework
The software that connects a model to its tools and organizes its steps; the same model can perform differently in a different framework.

The Question That Remains

If, years from now, your organization divides its work between people and AI agents, how will you know which tasks an agent can finish alone and which need a person to review them? And have you started building, today, the way you will measure that on your own work?
