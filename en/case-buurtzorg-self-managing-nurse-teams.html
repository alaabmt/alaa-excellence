Nurses Without Managers: What the Buurtzorg Home-Care Model Does, and Does Not, Prove

Most home-care providers split a patient's visits among several workers by task and cost. Buurtzorg reversed that logic: one qualified nurse does everything, and a small team runs itself without managers. So what does the evidence actually show?

Executive Summary

Dutch home care was usually organized by dividing the work: nurses handled tasks that required their training, while lower-paid staff helped patients with bathing, dressing, and other daily activities. Patients saw different people at different times, continuity suffered, and both patients and nurses were often dissatisfied. [1]

Buurtzorg, Dutch for "neighborhood care," started with one team in the town of Almelo and grew into a national organization. Its model gives a team of 10 to 12 highly trained nurses full responsibility for 50 to 60 patients in one neighborhood: assessment, care planning, the care itself, scheduling, and documentation. There are no managers above the teams, only coaches who help when asked, a small central office, and an IT system for scheduling, records, and billing. [1]

A KPMG study commissioned by the Dutch health ministry, using 2013 data, found that Buurtzorg patients needed 108 hours of home care a year, compared with 168 hours at other providers, even though Buurtzorg's hourly staff costs were higher. Once hospital, physician, and nursing home costs were added, however, total cost per patient was close to the national average. [1]

The 10X lesson is that Buurtzorg did not cut costs by hiring cheaper labor. It trusted nurses' professional judgment and gave teams control of their own work. The evidence supports efficiency in home-care hours more strongly than savings across the wider health system.

The Challenge: Care Divided by Task

Dutch home-care organizations contract with government-funded insurers, and each patient's authorized hours are set after an assessment of need. The prevailing logic was to send nurses only for tasks that required their skills and to assign everyday support to less trained, lower-paid staff. [1]

On paper that looks efficient. In practice it fragments the patient's experience: many faces, scattered information, and no one who sees the whole picture. The Commonwealth Fund case study notes that patients and nurses were often unhappy with the traditional model. [1]

The 10X Move

Buurtzorg rewrote the cost equation. Instead of assigning each task to the cheapest person able to do it, it gave a highly trained nurse responsibility for the whole patient and gave a small team responsibility for running itself. The bet was that a nurse who sees the full picture, and teaches patients and families to manage on their own, would need fewer hours, and that removing management layers would free money and time for care. Founder Jos de Blok's motto sums it up: "humanity over bureaucracy." [1][2]

How the Teams Work: Who Decides, Who Supports

  1. The team: 10 to 12 nurses responsible for 50 to 60 patients in one neighborhood. The team assesses needs, writes and delivers the care plan, schedules medical visits, and documents what is needed for continuity and billing. [1]
  2. Coaches, not managers: Nurses do not report to managers, although their hours are tracked. Coaches help teams solve problems; one coach said most of his work concerns how teams function, not clinical advice. [1]
  3. The central office: Fewer than 50 people in early 2015, handling payroll, office leases, and financial administration. [1]
  4. The IT system: Online scheduling, documentation of assessments and services, and billing, with information shared within and across teams. [1]
  5. Pay and surplus: Nurses are paid by education level under a union agreement, and surplus revenue goes to continuing education, neighborhood health projects, and innovation. [1]
  6. Growth: The organization says it reached more than 10,000 nurses in 850 teams within ten years. The Commonwealth Fund reports 8,000 nurses in 700 teams by 2015, caring for 65,000 patients in 2014. [1][2]

Evidence of Impact and Its Limits

The strongest published evidence is the KPMG study the Dutch health ministry commissioned after competitors accused Buurtzorg of cherry-picking profitable patients. It used case-mix adjustment, a statistical method that accounts for differences in how sick patients are so that providers can be compared fairly. Buurtzorg ranked among the best providers on patient experience, and its adjusted home-care costs sat at the 38th percentile, meaning 62% of providers were more expensive, even though its hourly staff cost was €54.47 versus €48.74. [1]

The picture changes when the lens widens. Buurtzorg patients were less likely to enter nursing homes, but their physician and hospital costs were higher (91st percentile: €7,787 versus €5,187). Taken together, adjusted total cost per patient was €15,357 versus €15,856, at the 49th percentile, close to the national average. The study did not explain the higher treatment costs. [1]

The KPMG report is a consultancy study of a single year of data (2013), and its figures are cited here through the Commonwealth Fund's independent review, not the original Dutch text. The Ernst & Young study dates from 2009, when Buurtzorg was small. Buurtzorg's website cites "40 percent savings" from Ernst & Young, while the Commonwealth Fund describes the same finding as using 40% of authorized hours versus about 70% at other providers, which is not the same claim. Current size figures are self-reported and undated. [1][2]

The Price and the Learning: Moving the Model Into Another System

In the UK, East London NHS Foundation Trust piloted a Buurtzorg-inspired team from June 2017 to August 2018: seven nurses and one healthcare assistant based in a single general practice serving about 12,000 people. A peer-reviewed evaluation in BMC Health Services Research described the result as "mixed success." [3]

Patients benefited from easier access, better continuity, and longer visits. Nurses said they had autonomy over clinical decisions but seldom over operational ones, such as managing the budget. At times the caseload ratio rose from about 6:1, the level expected of a Buurtzorg team, to 10:1, and morale fell. The authors concluded that embedding these principles would require a significant cultural shift at every level of the NHS. [3]

Back in 2015, the Commonwealth Fund flagged tests ahead for the model in the Netherlands itself: a new payment system for home care that year, teams growing faster than the support office, and the question of how an organization built around a charismatic founder sustains itself. [1]

The 10X Lens

Redefining Value

Value is measured as the fewest hours a patient needs to regain independence, not the lowest cost per hour. [1]

Leadership and Transformation Culture

Middle management replaced by coaches, and trust placed in nurses' professional judgment: "humanity over bureaucracy." [1][2]

Multiplying Capability

Each nurse assesses, plans, delivers, and coordinates care, while the team manages its own schedule and roles. [1]

Reinventing the Operating Model

Small, self-sufficient neighborhood teams, a small central office, and an IT system that replaces layers of supervision. [1]

From Future to Impact

Desired Future → Home care in which patients know who looks after them and regain as much independence as possible.

Required Capabilities → Nurses able to provide complete care, self-managing teams, a simple IT system, coaches, and a small support office.

What Was Built → Neighborhood teams, coaches instead of managers, and a shared system for scheduling, records, and billing.

New Operating Behavior → The nurse takes responsibility for the whole patient instead of a slice of tasks.

Outcomes and Impact → Fewer care hours and higher satisfaction, with total cost close to average, and only partial success so far when transplanted to other systems.

Insider Critique and Evidence Limits

A Buurtzorg team leader would likely say the numbers miss the core of the model: trust and relationships with patients and the neighborhood. That is a fair objection, but the available sources do not measure it. The same insider might also reject the idea that higher treatment costs signal weaker care. The Commonwealth Fund offers two possible explanations: well-trained nurses may spot problems that need a physician earlier, or physicians may refer patients who are already declining. The sources do not settle which is right. [1]

What can be said with confidence: the organization became able to deliver home care with fewer hours and better patient experience, using self-managing teams and a small central office. What cannot be said: that it reduced costs across the whole health system, or that the model works automatically in a different health system.

Putting It to Work

Start by asking where your organization assigns each task to the cheapest available person, leaving the client or patient lost between hands. Pilot a small team that owns a complete service for a defined group, and give it authority over scheduling and organization, not only technical decisions. Measure total hours, client outcomes, and total cost, not cost per hour alone. Watch workload from day one; rising caseloads were one reason the UK pilot struggled.

Transferability

The principles apply to services where one person's care is spread across many hands: home care, rehabilitation, and social care casework. But hourly payment in the Netherlands, strong nursing qualifications, and the freedom to remove management layers are specific to this case. The UK pilot shows that copying the form without transferring real authority is not enough.

The 10X Question

If we redesigned our service so that a small, qualified team owned each client from start to finish and ran its own work, which management layers would disappear? And what trust, skills, and systems must we build now so that this freedom does not become a burden on the team?
