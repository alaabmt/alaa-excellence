# What Changes When We Speak Too Certainly About Tomorrow?

A team lead leaves a long meeting after promising to issue an important decision the next day. The intention may be sincere. The team may have prepared carefully. The plan may look complete. Yet one word in that promise reaches beyond human possession: **tomorrow**. We can resolve, work, and arrange the means available to us. We do not own the coming day, know everything it will contain, or guarantee that our ability will remain unchanged. In this hypothetical situation, a missing fact appears before the decision and unsettles what had seemed certain.

The Qur’an draws the boundary clearly:

> **“And never say of anything, ‘Indeed, I will do that tomorrow,’ except [when adding], ‘If Allah wills.’ And remember your Lord when you forget [it] and say, ‘Perhaps my Lord will guide me to what is nearer than this to right conduct.’”**  
> Al-Kahf 18:23–24, Sahih International

These verses do not cancel resolve or discourage planning. They correct the place a person occupies within a plan. Work remains necessary, but the future cannot be spoken of as though it has already become ours. Saying **in shā’ Allāh — if Allah wills** does not reduce responsibility. It declares dependence on Allah, acknowledges that intention alone cannot secure an outcome, and places confidence where it belongs.

## Between the limits of knowledge and power

The preceding verse returns knowledge of the number of the People of the Cave to Allah and warns against argument built on speculation. Verses 23–24 then move from the limits of human knowledge about the unseen to the limits of human control over what comes next. That relationship in the sequence is a reflection on the context, not a tafsir claim attributed to a particular commentator.

The shared exegetical ground is clearer. Al-Tabari explains the prohibition against asserting that a future act will certainly occur without connecting it to Allah’s will. Ibn Kathir describes the passage as divine guidance for anyone who intends to do something in the future: the intention is referred back to the will of Allah. Al-Qurtubi discusses the wording and the range of views on remembering Allah after forgetting. Al-Sa‘di connects the verses to human dependence on Allah’s will, continued effort, and the request to be guided toward what is nearer to right conduct.

Human agency is not erased. It is placed within its true boundary. A person intends, decides, prepares, and acts, but does not possess the will, power, or result independently. Between intention and achievement lies a space known fully only to Allah.

For that reason, **in shā’ Allāh** is not a decorative ending attached to a promise. It is an inward correction before it becomes a spoken phrase. It joins trust in Allah with serious effort and humility about what remains unknown. The words may be present on the tongue while the heart still clings to an illusion of control. Their meaning becomes truthful when they lead to more faithful work, not less.

## What “if Allah wills” does not mean

The phrase can be emptied of its meaning. It may become a vague promise with no genuine intention behind it, a way to avoid saying no, or a cover for neglect that is already visible. None of those uses fulfills the responsibility carried by the verses.

Someone who says, “I will do it tomorrow, if Allah wills,” and then neglects every available means has not expressed dependence on Allah faithfully. The phrase has been made to conceal a failure the person chose. Someone who already knows that a commitment cannot be met, but leaves others waiting, does not become truthful by adding sacred words. Dependence on Allah does not cancel trustworthiness. Submission to His will does not excuse poor judgment or weak communication.

The opposite misunderstanding is also possible. Some may fear that qualifying a future intention weakens a decision. Yet recognizing human limits is not indecision. Resolve can be strong without claiming certainty. A plan can be exact without pretending to control what comes next. It may become more mature when its owner distinguishes among the effort promised, the result hoped for, and the conditions that lie outside human power.

Sincere resolve says: I will do what I can, prepare for what I know, honor what is within my ability, and return what lies beyond it to Allah’s will. Arrogant certainty treats the future as a guaranteed extension of the present.

## A way back after forgetting

The verse does not stop at the exception. It continues: **“And remember your Lord when you forget.”** Al-Tabari, Ibn Kathir, and Al-Qurtubi report several views on the phrase. A prominent view concerns making the exception when one remembers it. The commentaries also carry a broader meaning of remembering Allah after forgetfulness and returning to Him. There is no need here to settle the matters on which the exegetes differed or to move into detailed questions of oaths and expiation. The common ground is sufficient: forgetting does not close the door to remembrance, return, and correction.

That opening matters because human attention is not constant. A person may forget, speak too quickly, or discover that confidence exceeded knowledge. The command to remember can be contemplated as a path back from protecting an old image of certainty. The person remembers Allah, corrects what was said, and asks for right guidance.

The verse then teaches the supplication: **“Perhaps my Lord will guide me to what is nearer than this to right conduct.”** The issue is no longer only a missed phrase. It becomes a request for guidance toward something more right. An original intention may have been good, yet Allah may guide a person to something better. A plan may once have looked like the most faithful path, then new knowledge may show that revising it is more honest and just. Returning is not always defeat. It can be the movement from defending an image to seeking guidance.

A further reflection follows: someone who places the future under Allah’s will does not become less responsible for choices. Such a person may become more willing to review them—less inclined to treat a plan as untouchable, less threatened by an admission of incomplete knowledge, and less likely to turn a past promise into an argument against necessary correction.

## The hypothetical decision that was missing a fact

Return to the team lead from the opening. The draft decision is ready, the date has been announced, and everyone is waiting. Hours before release, the team discovers that a material fact was never reviewed. Relying on the incomplete record could treat someone unfairly or produce a weak decision.

One option is to proceed in order to preserve the appearance of decisiveness. The lead said “tomorrow,” and a delay might look uncertain. Another option is to invoke **in shā’ Allāh** as though it removes the duty to explain what changed or to take responsibility for the timetable. Both choices move away from trustworthiness.

A disciplined response begins with honesty. The lead explains that a consequential fact has emerged, pauses the decision only as long as the review requires, identifies what must be completed, corrects the date, and looks for the course nearer to what is right. There is no claim to have known what was not known and no use of dependence on Allah as a shield for negligence. The team acts on the means now available and accepts that correcting a promise can be more truthful than carrying out a promise whose basis has changed.

This is not an interpretation of the verse or a management model extracted from it. It is a proposed human application of humility before the future. Revelation remains the source of guidance; work practices are human designs that may help and may fail.

## When correction becomes part of excellence

Workplaces often praise confident forecasts, fixed dates, and visible resolve. Those qualities have value. They become dangerous when admitting the unknown is treated as weakness or revising a decision is seen as a threat to authority. The capacity to execute is incomplete without the capacity to correct course when knowledge changes.

This meaning can bring an important balance to Tamayuz 10X. Building stronger future capability does not require inflating certainty. It requires better preparation while the heart remains humble and the mind remains able to learn. A mature organization does not measure leadership by the number of promises defended at any cost. It looks for honesty in distinguishing resolve from guarantee, information from assumption, and principled steadiness from attachment to a plan.

That connection does not turn the Qur’an into a management manual, nor does it turn **in shā’ Allāh** into a risk-control technique. The foundation is faith: worship of Allah, dependence on His will, remembrance after forgetting, and a request for guidance. Any contemporary organizational benefit remains secondary to that foundation and limited by the fallibility of human practice.

When this foundation is present, the language of the future changes. “We will do this” is no longer a concealed claim of control. It becomes a commitment to sincere effort within the limits of ability. Revising course is not automatically a humiliation; it can be an act of trustworthiness when the known facts change. Seeking right guidance is no closing slogan. It is an admission that even a good plan may need to yield to a better path.

## Tomorrow is not ours

The two verses bring together four movements of faith and action: resolve that continues, an exception that returns the matter to Allah’s will, remembrance that repairs forgetfulness, and a supplication for what is nearer to right conduct. Together they free a person from two errors: paralysis before the future and the illusion of possessing it.

The problem is not sincere resolve to act tomorrow. The problem begins when tomorrow is spoken of as though the road to it does not pass through Allah’s will and as though preparation guarantees what will be. Between delay disguised as piety and certainty swollen by self-reliance stands Qur’anic discipline: full responsibility for the means, and full dependence on Allah.

Each future promise therefore carries an honest question: when we say **in shā’ Allāh**, does it lead us toward more truthful work, quicker remembrance, and braver correction—or does it remain on the tongue while the heart still clings to an illusion of control?
