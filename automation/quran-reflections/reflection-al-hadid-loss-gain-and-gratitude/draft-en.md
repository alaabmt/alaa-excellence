# How can we meet loss and success without losing our balance?

A proposal we have prepared carefully is rejected. Instead of asking what needs to improve, we begin asking why this happens to us. Later, a revised proposal wins approval, and we forget the people who helped us. The outcome has changed, but our response can still give it too much power over us.

Imagine a team that has spent weeks planning a service for the public, only to have its proposal turned down. The disappointment is real. Some members want to review their work; others would rather close the file because looking at it hurts. How might they acknowledge that pain without treating rejection as a final verdict on their worth, or as a reason to abandon what they can still put right? This is a hypothetical example.

## What escapes us, and what we receive

**Meaning summarized from Al-Hadid 57:22–23; quoted phrase from Sahih International.**

Al-Hadid 57:22 teaches that calamities on earth and in human lives are recorded before they come into being, and that this is easy for Allah. Verse 23 connects that knowledge with our response to loss and gain, ending with a warning against conceit and boasting. In Sahih International's translation, one short phrase is “not exult [in pride] over what He has given you.” [1, 2, 9]

Ibn Kathir explains verse 22 in terms of Allah's prior knowledge and the writing of decrees. The next verse connects that belief with how people receive what eludes them and what they are given. An outcome does not fall outside Allah's knowledge, and a blessing is not something a person has brought into existence independently through personal strength. [1, 2]

The preceding verses describe worldly display, rivalry, and impermanence, then call people to strive for Allah's forgiveness and Paradise. The following verse condemns stinginess and encouraging others to be stingy. The horizon is spiritual and moral: what we do with worldly life, and where our hearts and actions are directed. It extends far beyond managing pressure at work. [3–5]

## Patience leaves room for sorrow

Read in isolation, the instruction not to grieve over what has escaped us might sound like a demand to feel nothing. Al-Qurtubi explains that the blameworthy forms of sadness and joy are those that carry a person beyond permissible bounds. Al-Tabari transmits the understanding that misfortune should be met with patience and blessing with gratitude, while acknowledging that people naturally feel sadness and joy. [6, 7]

That opens a useful question for personal reflection. There is a difference between grieving over something lost and allowing it to consume our attention so completely that we neglect what remains within our care. Grief is human. But a present duty, a relationship, or an opportunity to repent and make amends may still need us.

Belief in divine decree does not give us knowledge of the hidden purpose of every event. Nor does it allow us to declare that a particular person's loss is a punishment. It does not erase a mistake we made or another person's rights. We can acknowledge a known failing and correct it while accepting that we do not understand every cause of an outcome and cannot bring back the past.

## When success arrives

Improved circumstances bring their own test. Al-Sa'di explains the censured joy as an exultation that leads to arrogance and looking down on others. Recognizing Allah's favor directs a blessing toward gratitude rather than self-glorification. Ibn Kathir likewise connects the verse's closing words with conceit and boasting over other people. [2, 8]

Enjoying a worthwhile achievement does not require contempt for those who have not achieved the same thing. Recognizing our effort need not erase Allah's favor, other people's contributions, or the circumstances that helped us. Gratitude can be expressed in sincere words and in honoring a right, fulfilling a trust, or using what we have received for good.

The hypothetical team could return to the stated reasons for rejecting its proposal. Was the need clear? Had it listened to the people who would use the service? Could it explain the budget it requested? These are proposed practical questions, not an interpretation of the verse or a promise that review will secure approval.

If the project later receives support, the team still has work to do. Approval is not proof that it was right about everything. It could acknowledge those who helped, retain the criticisms that improved the proposal, and test the service's effects before expanding it. Success does not remove the need for scrutiny, just as rejection does not remove the capacity to learn.

## What remains beyond an outcome

At work, this reflection may help us reconsider what counts as success. Are we protecting the team's image, or building a useful service that can withstand criticism? From a Tamayuz 10X perspective, a change in how work is organized might begin with honest review after both good and bad results, and fair recognition of contributions, instead of stopping at celebration or blame. This is a proposed contemporary application. The verses do not prescribe a management model or promise a numerical improvement.

The meaning reaches beyond organizations. What escapes us may be an appointment, an expected income, or something on which we had placed our hopes. What we receive may be health, help, or an opening we had not anticipated. We do not choose everything that happens, but we remain responsible for the response within our ability: patience that leaves room for feeling, and gratitude that does not become superiority.

Looking at what has escaped us and what we have received, which calls for clearer action today: patience that protects what remains, or gratitude that honors the blessing?

**Scope of this reflection:** Exegetical meanings are attributed to their sources. The workplace example is hypothetical. Its organizational application is a proposed reflection, not a binding interpretation of the Quran or a judgment about anyone's intentions or the reasons for their circumstances.
