# Is Listening Enough, or Do We Follow the Best of What We Hear?

We hear many opinions every day. Some confirm what we already prefer. Others unsettle assumptions we had stopped questioning. A few reveal that a decision we considered complete has left something important out. Yet exposure to many voices does not, by itself, produce sound judgment. We may listen courteously and still return unchanged to the view we brought into the room. We may collect feedback and quietly give the greatest weight to the answer closest to our desire.

The Qur’an sets a more demanding standard than hearing alone. Allah says:

> **“Who listen to speech and follow the best of it. Those are the ones Allah has guided, and those are people of understanding.”**  
> Az-Zumar 39:18, Sahih International

The verse does not praise ears that merely receive words. It describes people whose listening leads to discernment and whose discernment leads to following. Between hearing and action lies a demanding space: humility, judgment, and a willingness to change course.

## Before listening: the direction of the heart

The picture does not begin with verse 18 in isolation. The preceding verse describes those who avoid worshipping false objects of worship, turn back to Allah, and receive good tidings. It then introduces the next verse by commanding that good news be given to Allah’s servants.

That context places listening inside a corrected spiritual direction. The verse cannot be reduced to a neutral maxim in which each person simply selects whatever seems “best” according to personal taste. Its standard belongs within worship of Allah, a sincere search for truth, and readiness to follow what that truth requires.

Verse 19 then turns to the contrasting condition of one upon whom the word of punishment has become due. Listening and following therefore belong to a response of faith. They are not an intellectual exercise detached from divine guidance and moral responsibility.

## Listening that leads to following

The verse brings two actions together: they **listen**, then they **follow**. The movement from one verb to the next carries us from receiving speech to allowing it to shape conduct. The excellence described here is not that words pass through the ear, but that understanding reaches the point at which judgment and behavior may change.

Ibn Kathir explains the phrase in terms of understanding speech and acting upon what it contains. In al-Tabari’s explanation, Allah has guided those described to right conduct and sound judgment. The issue, then, is not admiration for a fine idea or the ability to remember an impressive phrase. It is a response to what has become clear as true and more rightly guided.

A contemporary reader can notice three different levels:

- hearing in order to know what was said;
- listening in order to understand the speaker’s case;
- following what is best once it becomes clear, even when it challenges the first preference.

The third is the most difficult. It tests sincerity after courtesy ends and revision begins to carry a cost.

## What does “the best of it” mean here?

The commentators record more than one explanation of **“the best of it.”** Al-Qurtubi reports views that include following the Qur’an rather than other speech, following its decisive guidance, and choosing pardon instead of retaliation in its proper setting. These reported explanations should not be flattened into a claim that there is one narrow, uncontested interpretation.

Al-Sa‘di draws out the broader act of distinguishing between statements, while explicitly affirming that the speech of Allah and the speech of His Messenger are best in the absolute sense. This keeps the center of the verse clear. “Best” is not a label we attach to whatever comforts us, nor permission to select whatever serves our preference. It concerns what is more faithful to truth, sounder in guidance, and better in its effect.

The verse therefore does not grant people a license to invent a private standard and then bless the result. It trains the believer to weigh what is heard in the light of revelation and truth, and to accept where that measure leads.

## Guidance is not an independent human achievement

After describing listening and following, the verse says: **“Those are the ones Allah has guided.”** That sentence leaves no room for intellectual vanity. The ability to recognize what deserves to be followed is not an autonomous mental triumph. It is a gift of guidance from Allah, joined to the servant’s honest search for truth and willingness to submit to it.

The verse closes by describing them as **“people of understanding.”** Ibn Kathir’s commentary connects this description with sound minds and upright dispositions. Intellect is not praised here merely for the quantity of information it can gather, but for the integrity with which it distinguishes and the sincerity with which it follows.

A person may be widely read, quick in reply, and formidable in argument, yet remain unable to leave an opinion after a sounder one appears. Another person may possess less information but be less attached to personal victory and therefore more ready to accept the truth.

## When listening becomes a responsibility

As a contemporary reflection—not as tafsir or a method derived from the verse—we can examine our listening through four connected questions:

1. **Were we truly present?** Did we understand the statement as its speaker intended, or were we preparing our reply while the other person spoke?
2. **What determined our preference?** Did we weigh the argument itself, or the speaker’s status, familiarity, or closeness to us?
3. **Do we have the courage to revise?** Can we admit that our first position is no longer the strongest after better evidence appears?
4. **Did listening have a practical effect?** Did the decision or behavior change, or did “the best” remain a respected idea that no one followed?

These questions do not replace the verse’s interpretation, nor do they claim to exhaust its meaning. They are limited applications inspired by the movement from listening to following and from following to guidance.

## A hypothetical example: the quieter view may be stronger

Imagine a team considering the launch of a new public service. Most participants favor the fastest proposal because it is attractive and easy to present. Near the end of the meeting, an analyst quietly shows evidence that the same route may be difficult for some older people and people with disabilities to use.

The team could record her concern in the minutes and continue with its original decision. It would then have heard her without seriously testing whether it should follow the stronger course. Or it could pause, examine the evidence, and revise the design and timeline—not because the quieter voice has “won,” but because the better-supported argument has revealed something the first plan missed.

This is a hypothetical scenario for reflecting on responsibility in decision-making. It is not a direct application of the verse’s praise to a particular person, team, or institution. The question it raises is narrower: do we allow what proves more rightly guided and more just to change what we had intended to do?

## Listening in institutional excellence

In workplaces, “participation” can become ceremonial: an open meeting, a survey, a channel for comments, and then a decision untouched by anything that was gathered. Genuine value cannot be measured only by the number of voices heard. It also depends on how fairly their claims were examined and whether the decision shows any trace of what was learned.

Mature leadership separates the status of a speaker from the strength of the statement. Rank does not make an argument correct. Quiet delivery does not make an observation weak. Disagreement is not the same as hostility. Changing a decision after stronger evidence emerges need not diminish authority; it may show that the purpose mattered more than personal pride.

Institutional intelligence also begins with more than the accumulation of data. It requires the capacity to turn data into discernment, discernment into choice, and choice into action that can be explained and reviewed. This is a contemporary connection with the verse, not a claim that the Qur’an supplies a ready-made management model.

## Three questions before a decision becomes final

Three further questions can stay with the reader:

- Which view did I fail to understand because I was occupied with defending my own?
- Did I prefer the argument, or did I prefer the name and position of its speaker?
- What practical change will I make if a path other than my first choice proves better?

The answers may show that the problem is not missing information. It may be an unwillingness to follow where the available truth points.

## From listening to guidance

The verse traces a concise and profound movement: speech is heard, the best is followed, guidance is attributed to Allah, and sound understanding proves itself in action. It is not enough to describe ourselves as open-minded or to take pride in how much we read and hear. The clearer test comes afterward: what did we do when a better course became visible?

One of the verse’s most compelling effects is that it can reshape our idea of strength. Strength is not remaining fixed on an opinion no matter what appears. It is remaining faithful to the search for truth, even when that fidelity requires us to change our mind.

## Methodological note

This is a contemporary reflection grounded in the context of Az-Zumar 39:17–19 and in meanings recorded by al-Tabari, Ibn Kathir, al-Qurtubi, and al-Sa‘di. Statements attributed to the commentators are presented as tafsir; the personal and institutional applications are contemporary reflections, not a new interpretation of the verse. The scenario is hypothetical and does not describe a particular event or organization.
