# How Copenhagen Used Buildings to Reduce Peak Heating Demand

Cold mornings place concentrated demand on urban heating networks. Meeting every peak through additional production can require extra generating resources. Copenhagen Municipality and utility HOFOR tested a different approach: heat buildings ahead of peaks and temporarily reduce heat input during high demand, within thermal comfort limits.

## Buildings as part of the network
District heating delivers heat through hot water. Walls and floors retain some of that heat, allowing buildings to delay further demand for a limited period. They do not generate new energy; they shift consumption in time. Flexibility varies with insulation, weather and building use.

Digital controls, weather forecasts and a platform aggregating available flexibility connect building operation with network planning. Owners participate voluntarily, making contractual coordination as important as equipment.

## What changed?
Across the 2021–22 and 2022–23 heating seasons, project partners reported a 13.2% reduction in average building-level morning peak demand across a trial involving 120 municipal buildings, and a 5–7% reduction in heating consumption with predictive solar compensation. These are participant-reported results, not an independent randomized evaluation [2].

In the subsequent Flexumers4Future project, the municipality reported 150 participating buildings covering roughly one million square metres and 6–8 MW of activatable flexibility [1]. MW measures power, not stored energy. The earlier percentages cannot be extrapolated to this later project or the entire city.

A 2020 academic study also supports the feasibility of shifting demand using the thermal mass of a low-energy residential building in Nordhavn. Its simulation does not validate the later project's outcomes [3].

## Working backwards from the future
As heat production becomes more closely linked to variable renewable electricity, matching demand timing to supply becomes an operational capability. This requires building behaviour data, comfort boundaries, coordinated control signals and measurement. This is a backcasting analysis, not evidence that the organisations formally employed foresight.

## The eight Tamayuz 10X pillars
1. Foresight and ambition: prepare the network for a changing energy mix.
2. Redefining value and strategic direction: treat demand timing, as well as consumption volume, as a manageable resource.
3. Leadership, transformation culture and future governance: cooperation among municipality, utility and owners through voluntary agreements.
4. Multiplying capabilities and empowerment: forecasting, building control and aggregation.
5. Reinventing the institution and business model: experimental movement beyond heat sales towards demand coordination; a proven sustainable tariff model is not established [4].
6. Innovation and experimentation: moving from individual building trials towards larger coordinated groups.
7. Maximising organisational and technological intelligence: using building data and weather forecasts to schedule control.
8. Maximising value and sustainable outcomes: initial peak reductions; network-wide financial savings remain estimates.

## Transferable lessons
Measure hourly demand first. Select buildings with different characteristics and test comfort limits. Compare results against a weather-adjusted baseline, monitor rebound demand after curtailment, and track complaints and control reliability. Expand only after demonstrating that aggregated flexibility actually reduces network peaks without degrading service.

## Evidence limitations
The sources do not establish realised citywide financial savings or avoided construction of generating facilities. Scaling depends on compatible controls, agreements and regulation. Published measurements do not establish preserved comfort in every building.

**10X question:** If we want an energy network able to adapt to supply without sacrificing service quality, what capabilities, data and agreements must we build today?
