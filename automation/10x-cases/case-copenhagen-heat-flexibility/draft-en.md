# How Copenhagen used buildings to ease peak heating demand

Cold mornings place concentrated demand on urban heating networks. Meeting every peak through additional production can require extra generating resources. Copenhagen Municipality and utility HOFOR tested a different approach: heat buildings ahead of peaks and temporarily reduce heat input during high demand, with occupant comfort as a condition of participation.

## Buildings as part of the network
District heating delivers heat through hot water. Walls and floors retain some of that heat, allowing buildings to delay further demand for a limited period. They do not generate new energy; they shift consumption in time. Flexibility varies with insulation, weather and building use.

Owners opt in, a controls provider adjusts heat delivery, and an aggregator combines the flexibility offered by participating buildings. HOFOR works with Varmelast, which dispatches heat production, while municipal departments coordinate their contributions and engage owners. The arrangement depends on contracts and clear responsibilities, not a single algorithm controlling the whole network [4, 6].

## What changed?
Across the 2021–22 and 2022–23 heating seasons, project partners reported a 13.2% reduction in average building-level morning peak demand across a trial involving 120 municipal buildings, and a 5–7% reduction in heating consumption with predictive solar compensation, which anticipates solar warming and reduces supplied heat accordingly. These are participant-reported results, not an independent randomized evaluation [2].

In the subsequent Flexumers4Future project, the municipality reported 150 participating buildings covering roughly one million square metres and 6–8 MW of activatable flexibility [1]. MW measures power, not stored energy. The earlier percentages cannot be extrapolated to this later project or the entire city.

A 2020 academic study also supports the feasibility of shifting demand using the ability of a low-energy residential building’s materials to retain and gradually release heat in Nordhavn. Its simulation does not validate the later project's outcomes [3].

## Working backwards from the future
As heat production becomes more closely linked to variable renewable electricity, matching demand timing to supply becomes an operational capability. This requires building behaviour data, comfort boundaries, coordinated control signals and measurement. The sources do not establish the use of a formal foresight method.

## What implementation revealed
The municipality’s May 2024 decision set an ambition of 1,000 participating buildings and a planned period from May 2024 to April 2026. The closing account reports 150 buildings. The public sources do not adequately reconcile participation definitions or explain the gap. These figures cannot establish a reliable completion rate or a continuous expansion of the same cohort [1, 5].

Supplier compatibility and contracting arrangements complicated participation. Subsequent work explores more complex building controls. Coordinating customer data, advising owners, and making enrollment easier also require people and organizational capacity; installing equipment does not resolve those tasks [4, 6].

## The Tamayuz 10X interpretation
Buildings can offer value through the timing of demand as well as the amount of heat consumed. The case supports analysis of value redefinition, control and coordination capabilities, experimentation, and data-informed operation. A sustainable business model and system-wide financial impact remain unproven; the evidence does not warrant assigning all eight pillars to this case.

## Transferable lessons
Measure hourly demand first. Select buildings with different characteristics and test comfort limits. Compare results against a weather-adjusted baseline, monitor rebound demand after curtailment, and track complaints and control reliability. Expand only after demonstrating that aggregated flexibility actually reduces network peaks without degrading service.

## Evidence limitations
The sources do not establish realised citywide financial savings or avoided construction of generating facilities. Scaling depends on compatible controls, agreements and regulation. Published measurements do not establish preserved comfort in every building. The averaging method, variation between buildings, comfort thresholds, and complaints are not reported in enough detail to reproduce the results. Reducing one building’s peak is also different from reducing the network’s coincident peak.

**10X question:** If we want an energy network able to adapt to supply without sacrificing service quality, what capabilities, data and agreements must we build today?
