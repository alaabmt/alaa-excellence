# Backcasting and 10X Analysis — Costa Rica Payments for Environmental Services

## Future state first

The relevant future is not simply “more trees.” It is a land-use system in which forest owners can earn a durable, measurable return from climate regulation, biodiversity, water protection and landscape value; public finance buys additional outcomes in the places where loss risk is real; excluded landholders can participate; and funding is resilient to declining fossil-fuel use.

## Backcasting chain

| Backcast step | Institutional requirement | Costa Rica evidence | Remaining gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Forest services become economically visible | Recognise public benefits that markets do not price | Forest Law 7575 recognises four services | Legal recognition does not ensure service-level measurement |
| 2. Landholders can transact with the state | Standard contracts, eligibility rules, payment administration | FONAFIFO manages contracts and payments | Title and application rules can exclude some groups |
| 3. Funding survives annual political bargaining | Earmarked, diversified finance | 3.5% fuel-tax share plus water fees, budgets, donors and results-based finance | Fuel-tax dependence is structurally misaligned with decarbonisation |
| 4. Public money buys additional outcomes | Target high-threat/high-value land and verify compliance | Selection criteria, monitoring and evolving contracts | Additionality remains heterogeneous; some enrolled forest would stand anyway |
| 5. The mechanism learns | Evaluation changes contract design and incentives | Criteria and modalities evolved; 2024 introduced differentiated service stacking | Outcome evidence for the 2024 design is not yet available |
| 6. Scale does not erase fairness | Reduce title, information and transaction barriers | Preferential conditions and Indigenous contracts exist | Women, Indigenous associations and some smallholders remain underrepresented |
| 7. Results finance feeds the system | Independent verification triggers payment | USD 16.4m paid for 3.28m verified tonnes in 2018–2019 | Broader REDD+ result cannot be attributed to PES alone |

## Transformation mechanism

The transformation operates through a closed institutional loop:

1. **Law defines value:** forest services become a compensable public good.
2. **A dedicated fund aggregates finance:** FONAFIFO collects earmarked and external resources.
3. **Contracts convert policy into action:** landholders accept land-use commitments.
4. **Monitoring creates accountability:** programme compliance and broader forest/emissions outcomes are measured.
5. **Evaluation exposes weak additionality and access:** independent studies identify where money changes behaviour and who is excluded.
6. **Design adapts:** priorities, modalities and payment differentiation evolve.

The 10X significance is therefore not the payment itself. It is the conversion of conservation from an unfunded prohibition into a managed public-value purchasing system.

## Official pillars — evidence-supported only

### 1. Foresight and ambition — supported

The policy shift responded to a future in which continued clearing would erode climate, biodiversity, water and tourism value. The ambition moved beyond isolated reserves to a nationwide mechanism covering private land.

**Evidence limit:** the 1990s legal design did not yet have today's service-level outcome metrics.

### 2. Redefining value and strategic direction — strongly supported

The central redefinition was explicit: standing forest produces services with economic and public value. The state moved from treating conservation mainly as a restriction or subsidy to purchasing recognised ecosystem services.

**Transferable lesson:** define the public value unit before designing the payment.

### 3. Leadership, culture and governance — supported

Forest Law 7575, FONAFIFO's mandate, earmarked finance and a monitoring role created governance continuity across political cycles. The model coordinates law, finance and contracting rather than relying on a campaign.

**Gap:** overlapping environmental institutions and access rules still create friction.

### 4. Capabilities and empowerment — evidence insufficient for a full pillar

There is evidence of regional administration, technical procedures and efforts to include communities. It is not sufficient to establish broad capability uplift or empowerment across participant groups. Participation gaps and title barriers prevent a positive pillar claim.

### 5. Reinventing the institution and operating model — strongly supported

The operating model connects a public fund manager, multiple financing streams, standard contracts, technical review, land-use commitments and monitoring. This is the core institutional transformation.

**Transferable lesson:** assign one accountable integrator for funds, contracts and verification.

### 6. Innovation and experimentation — supported with limits

The programme evolved across contract types, priority criteria, water and carbon finance, and 2024 service stacking. Independent findings on additionality and access informed a continuing redesign logic.

**Gap:** adaptation is documented; improved outcomes from the newest design are not yet proven.

### 7. Institutional and technical intelligence — supported

Geospatial evaluation, administrative contract data, monitoring and third-party emissions verification allow the system to distinguish activity from some outcomes. Independent research makes weak additionality visible.

**Gap:** biodiversity, water and scenic-value outcomes are not consistently verified contract by contract.

### 8. Sustainable value and results — supported with explicit attribution limits

Scale is substantial: about 1.3 million hectares covered and more than USD 600 million paid over the cited 24-year period. Verified emissions reductions unlocked results-based payment. Causal studies find real but heterogeneous forest-protection effects.

**Limit:** national forest recovery is multi-causal; PES is one component alongside the clearing ban, protected areas, economic change, agricultural policy and tourism.

## What can be transferred

1. **Translate externalities into a legal service definition.**
2. **Create an institutional buyer, not merely a grant window.**
3. **Link payment to contracts and monitoring.**
4. **Use counterfactual evaluation to test additionality.**
5. **Target threat and value, not only willingness to enrol.**
6. **Design access for people without conventional title or technical capacity.**
7. **Diversify finance before the original tax base declines.**
8. **Separate programme reach, observed outcomes and causal impact in public reporting.**

## Non-transferable context

- Costa Rica's tourism economy, forest history, legal system and national land scale shaped feasibility.
- A fuel-tax earmark may be politically or fiscally unsuitable elsewhere.
- Voluntary enrolment works differently where agricultural opportunity costs are higher.
- Results cannot be copied without credible land tenure, monitoring and payment administration.

## Editorial architecture

1. Open with the institutional contradiction: forests produced public value, but clearing produced private income.
2. Explain why bans alone were insufficient.
3. Show the value-recognition and fund-manager design.
4. Follow money → contract → land-use commitment → monitoring → evaluation → redesign.
5. Present scale separately from causal impact.
6. Surface additionality and access gaps before the conclusion.
7. Close with a transfer question: what public value does the reader's institution depend on but still leave economically invisible?
