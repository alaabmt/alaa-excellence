# Backcasting and 10X Analysis — Brazil Family Health Strategy

## Future condition

The defensible future condition is not “more clinic visits.” It is a health system in which every community can reach continuous primary care, prevention begins before avoidable deterioration, and a team knows the people and risks in its territory well enough to coordinate care rather than wait for episodic demand.

This is a policy ambition and operating direction. It is not a claim that Brazil has already achieved universal, uniformly high-quality primary care.

## Backcasting chain

### 1. Desired future
Universal, equitable, continuous primary care that can prevent, detect, treat, and coordinate health needs close to where people live.

### 2. Required capabilities
- A defined population for which a team is accountable.
- Multidisciplinary clinical capacity.
- Community presence through community health workers.
- Household registration, home visits, local risk knowledge, prevention, and surveillance.
- Referral and coordination links to specialist and hospital care.
- Federal financing and standards combined with municipal delivery.
- Workforce continuity, training, infrastructure, and data use.

### 3. What had to be built
- Family-health teams with explicit composition and workloads.
- Basic health units linked to territories.
- Community health-worker roles that connect households to the health system.
- Funding and accreditation mechanisms for national scale.
- Registration and information practices to make the covered population visible.
- Quality and access programs, though their design and continuity changed over time.

### 4. New operating behavior
The service does not depend only on patients presenting at a facility. Teams are assigned to a community, collect knowledge through registration and home contact, combine clinical and public-health work, and maintain continuity over time. Responsibility therefore shifts from processing visits to managing the health of a defined population.

### 5. Outcomes and evidence limits
Independent studies repeatedly associate expanded coverage with lower infant, amenable, and selected cardiovascular mortality. The evidence is strongest as contribution evidence across time and municipalities, not proof that the strategy alone caused national gains. A major national study did not find lower avoidable hospitalizations, and quality, staffing, infrastructure, and care integration remain uneven.

## How the transformation happened

The mechanism is a linked operating system:

1. Federal policy defines and co-finances the strategy.
2. Municipalities organize teams and basic health units.
3. Each team is responsible for a defined territory and registered population.
4. Community health workers connect household knowledge, prevention, and follow-up to the clinical team.
5. The team provides and coordinates primary care.
6. Coverage and continuity create opportunities to identify risk earlier and manage conditions before deterioration.
7. Governance quality, staffing stability, referral capacity, and infrastructure determine whether that opportunity becomes a result.

The transfer lesson is therefore not “hire community health workers.” It is to join population accountability, team design, local knowledge, continuity, financing, and referral governance. Removing any one of those links weakens the model.

## Official 10X pillars supported by evidence

### Pillar 1 — Foresight and ambition: supported with limits
The strategy reflects a documented ambition to universalize and rebalance care toward primary and community settings. Public sources do not establish a formal foresight method, so the case must not claim scenario planning or formal futures practice.

### Pillar 2 — Redefining value and strategic direction: supported
Value moved from counting episodic encounters toward maintaining access, prevention, continuity, and population health within a territory.

### Pillar 3 — Leadership, culture, and future governance: supported
National standards and financing operate through decentralized municipal delivery. Evidence also shows that stronger local health governance changes results, making governance part of the mechanism rather than an administrative backdrop.

### Pillar 4 — Capability multiplication and empowerment: supported
Multidisciplinary teams and community health workers expanded the system's ability to see household needs, reach underserved communities, and act before facility attendance.

### Pillar 5 — Reinventing the institution and operating model: strongly supported
The unit of responsibility changed from the visit to the registered population and territory. This is the central 10X move.

### Pillar 6 — Innovation and experimentation: not sufficiently supported
The public record describes gradual rollout and repeated policy redesign, but the reviewed sources do not provide a sufficiently specific national pilot protocol, baseline, learning loop, and scale decision to analyze this pillar rigorously. It remains a gap.

### Pillar 7 — Maximizing institutional and technical intelligence: supported with limits
Registration, territorial knowledge, household visits, and surveillance convert community information into team action. This is institutional intelligence; the case should not inflate it into an advanced-digital or AI claim.

### Pillar 8 — Sustainable value and outcomes: supported with limits
The longevity and scale of the model, together with repeated mortality associations and progressive reach, support this pillar. Mixed hospitalization evidence and uneven quality prevent a universal outcome claim.

## Pillar gap

- **Pillar 6** is excluded from the published analysis because evidence about an explicit pilot-to-learning-to-scale protocol is insufficient.
- Technology is not treated as a standalone pillar or transformation. The case is primarily about operating design and institutional capability.

## Transferable principles and conditions

1. Assign every service team a defined population, not only an incoming queue.
2. Put a trusted boundary-spanning role between households and professionals.
3. Combine clinical delivery, prevention, surveillance, and follow-up in one operating rhythm.
4. Finance continuity and territorial responsibility, not only transactions.
5. Treat governance, staffing stability, and referral capacity as outcome-producing components.
6. Use mixed evidence: access and mortality gains do not erase weak or negative evidence on avoidable hospitalizations.
7. Track both reach and quality. Coverage without continuity, equipment, and integration can overstate capability.

## Transfer risks

- Copying community-health-worker roles without team authority, referral access, and reliable funding.
- Expanding enrollment faster than workforce and infrastructure.
- Treating household data collection as paperwork rather than a decision input.
- Using national averages to hide municipal inequality.
- Presenting a target coverage figure as realized service quality.

## Future-facing 10X question

If every community were to have a team responsible for keeping people well—not only treating those who arrive—what population data, local presence, workforce, decision rights, referral capacity, and financing would have to be built now?
