# How the Netherlands Gave Rivers More Space Instead of Raising Dikes

**10X Excellence Case | Netherlands | Flood-risk management and institutional transformation**

## Executive summary

The Dutch challenge was not simply that dikes were a little too low. The protection model itself was confining rivers to narrower corridors while future peak flows were expected to grow. After extreme high water in 1993 and 1995—when roughly 250,000 people were evacuated—the Netherlands did not rely only on stronger barriers. It redefined protection: lower design water levels by giving rivers more room, while improving spatial quality at the same time.

Between 2006 and 2019, central and regional authorities, working with residents, delivered measures at 34 locations along branches of the Rhine. Rijkswaterstaat reports a total programme cost of €2.3 billion. The work was not one engineering megaproject but a portfolio that included moving dikes inland, digging side channels, lowering floodplains and removing flow obstacles. At Nijmegen, a dike was moved 350 metres inland and a 3-kilometre side channel was excavated. European Climate-ADAPT reports that the project achieved a 35-centimetre design-water-level reduction, exceeding its 27-centimetre target, and that the channel carries about one-third of flow during high water.

The institutional model is the more transferable achievement. National government held the outcome, funding and programme assurance; regional and local bodies could propose alternatives and deliver projects. Hydraulic models, a tool for comparing measures and costs, and a multidisciplinary Quality Team kept safety and spatial quality inside the same decision process.

The evidence also sets a firm boundary. The 16,000 cubic metres per second figure is a design discharge for the Rhine branches, not proof that the complete system has been empirically tested at that flow. The programme’s system-wide effect on national flood risk—probability multiplied by consequences—was not comprehensively quantified in the evaluation reviewed here. The precise lesson is not that the Netherlands “defeated flooding.” It rebuilt an operating model around a changing river and made the limits of its evidence visible.

## When the old solution becomes part of the problem

Dikes protect land from water, but they can also reduce the space available to a river. Confining peak flow to a narrower corridor raises water levels and increases sensitivity to bottlenecks. Continually raising barriers at every location was therefore an incomplete answer to a more volatile future.

In 2000, “room for the river” became a guiding Dutch flood-protection principle. That direction became an implementation programme along the Rhine branches. Its target future was clear: a river basin able to carry more water while remaining liveable, economically useful and ecologically functional. Backcasting from that future changed the questions asked in the present. Which land must remain available? Which intervention lowers water levels at acceptable cost? Who should control local design? How can spatial quality survive schedule and budget pressure?

## The product was no longer a dike; it was a portfolio of outcomes

The programme changed the unit of work. Rather than treating each bottleneck as an isolated protection project, it distributed the task across connected locations. A dike could be moved inland at one site; elsewhere, a floodplain could be lowered, a side channel dug or an obstacle removed.

The stated objective was for the Rhine branches to accommodate a design discharge of 16,000 cubic metres per second without flooding. The distinction matters: this was a design and selection standard, not evidence that the whole system had experienced that discharge and passed a real-world test.

Rijkswaterstaat describes verified delivery as measures at 34 locations, a €2.3 billion total cost and official completion in early 2019. Older studies and programme documents often refer to 39 measures or projects. The difference reflects counting units and programme stages, so this case uses the current official count of completed locations and does not combine the figures.

Rijkswaterstaat also says the programme improved safety, spatial quality and quality of life for around four million people. That is an official statement about programme reach; it is not a causal estimate of lives saved or losses avoided.

## How strategy became an operating model

### 1. Foresight and ambition

The programme was not designed only around the high-water events of the 1990s. Preserving physical space was part of preparing for larger future flows, because land developed today may be unavailable for adaptation tomorrow. Room for the River 2.0, launched in April 2025, is examining basin needs through 2100 and beyond. That is evidence of continuing adaptation, not proof that the first programme solved the challenge permanently.

### 2. Redefining value and strategic direction

The programme paired flood safety with spatial quality. The decision was no longer “How high can the barrier be built?” It became “Which intervention lowers water levels while improving the surrounding urban or natural environment?”

That definition gave design teams room to search for multiple forms of value. It also made decisions harder. Nature, recreation, heritage, farming and navigation do not share one metric. “Spatial quality” does not mean that every benefit was measured or that every stakeholder benefited equally.

### 3. Leadership, culture and governance

National government retained the outcome, budget and overall assurance. Provinces, municipalities, regional water authorities and local actors contributed to design and delivery. The Dutch Water Sector portal records 19 institutional partners.

The balance was deliberate: the centre prevented fragmentation of the hydraulic outcome, while local actors reduced the risk of imposing one solution on places with different settlement, ownership and economic conditions. Academic studies associate the programme’s performance with a combined strategic and delivery focus, collaboration and adaptation to new knowledge.

Participation did not eliminate conflict. A study of three programme projects found coproduction between residents and government to be the most viable path for local initiatives to have positive impact. It also found that agencies could respond defensively and that outcomes depended on timing and access to decision-makers.

### Why pillar 4 is not scored separately

The evidence shows regional discretion, multidisciplinary review and public participation. It does not provide a consistent programme-wide measure of workforce capability building, community empowerment or lasting skill development. Those mechanisms are therefore treated under governance and institutional intelligence, not claimed as a separately proven capabilities-and-empowerment pillar.

### 5. Reinventing the institution and operating model

The central innovation was not any one channel or dike. It was the operating model: one national programme, a portfolio of local projects, shared hydraulic standards, review gates, secured funding and room for regional alternatives.

The institution moved from “protect each location with its barrier” toward “distribute water-level reduction across the system.” Dikes were not abandoned. They remained part of the mix where river widening was not feasible. The transformation changed the rule for choosing a solution rather than replacing one technology everywhere.

### 6. Innovation and experimentation

The programme used a menu of spatial and hydraulic measures instead of a single prescription. Regional bodies could propose alternatives within the outcome and budget boundaries. Locally suitable measures were adapted and some practices were extended across the portfolio without assuming one uniform experimental standard.

Not every project was an experiment, and novelty was not outcome evidence. The institutional value was allowing local variation without losing the system result.

### 7. Institutional and technical intelligence

A Planning Kit combined hydraulic-model results and cost estimates to compare options. A multidisciplinary Quality Team reviewed spatial design, making it less likely that the second objective would disappear under delivery pressure.

Information became part of governance: the planning tool compared hydraulic effect and cost; the Quality Team challenged spatial design; programme management used those inputs at decision gates; and local authorities designed and delivered within approved boundaries. Models still contain assumptions. Their outputs cannot be recast as observed results.

### 8. Sustainable value and results

Nijmegen provides the clearest verifiable result in this case. The river narrowed at the city, so the dike was moved 350 metres inland and a 3-kilometre side channel was created. The intervention also formed an island now used as an urban river park. The reported design-water-level reduction reached 35 centimetres against a 27-centimetre target, while the channel carries roughly one-third of flow during high water.

That is one project result, not a national average. At programme level, the evidence establishes completion of a large portfolio and a change in planning and delivery. It does not provide one percentage for avoided national losses or a comprehensive empirical measure of total risk reduction.

## What became possible?

Before the shift, an institution could strengthen a line of defence at one site. Afterwards, it could compare interventions across a connected river system, distribute an outcome among them, put spatial quality into a formal decision gate and give local authorities design room within one national result.

That institutional capability matters more than any single structure. It allows the programme logic to adjust when climate assumptions change, riverbed erosion emerges or drought and navigation pressures intensify. Room for the River 2.0 shows that the operating model has become a platform for another adaptation cycle.

## The price hidden by the aerial photograph

Giving land back to a river takes it from another use. Households or businesses may have to move. Land may require acquisition or compensation. Farming, navigation and maintenance can face new costs. The current official page explicitly acknowledges that price.

Participation quality also varied. Listening is not the same as transferring decision rights, and local initiatives need timing, institutional access and negotiating capacity. Another country cannot transfer this case by copying a side channel. It must first understand property, compensation, trust and the government’s ability to coordinate an entire basin.

## Evidence boundaries

This case separates four layers:

- **Stated target:** greater safety and better spatial quality.
- **Designed capacity:** 16,000 cubic metres per second for the Rhine branches.
- **Verified execution:** 34 locations, €2.3 billion and official completion in 2019.
- **Measured project result:** a 35-centimetre design-water-level reduction at Nijmegen, alongside new spatial functions.

The reviewed sources do not support a national causal percentage for reduced flood probability and consequences, a total realized value for ecosystem services, or a count of lives saved. That boundary is not a footnote. It separates analysis from promotion.

## What can other institutions transfer?

Start with the future in which the institution must operate, then preserve options before they disappear. Distribute a system outcome across a portfolio instead of asking one project to solve a systemic problem. Give the second objective—spatial quality in this case—its own review mechanism, or delivery pressure will consume it. Separate local design freedom from national outcome unity. Finally, publish the difference between what was designed, what was delivered and what was measured.

## The 10X question

What future do we want our institution to operate in several years from now, and what should it be able to see, decide and execute then that it cannot do today? If that is the future we want, which space, data, skills, governance and ways of working must we begin building now—before the available options become more expensive or impossible?
