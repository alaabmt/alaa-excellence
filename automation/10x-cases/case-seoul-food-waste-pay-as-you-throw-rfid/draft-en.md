# How Seoul Made Every Household See the Weight of Its Food Waste

The shift was not a smart bin recognizing a resident's card. It began when the cost of food scraps could no longer remain hidden inside a flat fee paid by everyone. What emerged was a system connecting what a household discarded to a visible weight, a specific charge and a separate treatment route.

## Executive Snapshot

By the early 1990s, South Korea's landfills were running out of room, and wet food scraps intensified odor and polluted leachate. Collecting mixed waste and moving it farther away was no longer a durable answer. The country introduced quantity-based waste charges in 1995, banned food waste from landfills in 2005 and required households to separate it. Seoul later moved from designated bags and chips toward bins that weigh each household's food waste and add the charge to its bill.

That sequence redesigned the full service. Households separate their scraps. The bin records identity and weight. Municipalities collect a dedicated stream. Treatment plants remove contaminants and water, then route the remaining material to animal feed, compost or biogas. Government data reported independently show that Seoul's food waste fell from 3,181 tonnes a day in 2013 to 2,419 in 2023, a decline of 23.9%.[1] The bins cannot be credited with the entire reduction; they operate within a wider system of rules, pricing, public education and processing capacity.

The case weakens an assumption larger than the technology itself. When the cost of waste is visible at the point of disposal—and the city can receive and process the separated stream—throwing food away stops being an anonymous act and becomes a measurable signal. Yet South Korea's reported national recycling rate of 96.8% of 4.81 million tonnes in 2023 does not mean that food was never wasted, nor does it independently verify the quality of every final output.[1] Prevention remains more valuable than recovery.

## When landfills filled up, the flat fee revealed its flaw

A flat fee treated a household that discarded little like one that discarded a great deal. It sent no price signal into the kitchen, where decisions about buying, cooking and leftovers are made. As cities expanded and land became scarce, wet food waste created problems beyond volume: odor, contaminated liquid and extra collection weight.[2]

In 1995, South Korea replaced the flat waste charge with **pay as you throw (PAYT)**, under which fees rise with the quantity discarded. Recyclables were collected without the same charge, making separation less costly than mixing.[2]

The first reform exposed another problem. Once paper and plastic were removed, food scraps remained in general waste, with all their moisture and smell. A landfill ban for food waste took effect in 2005, and separation became mandatory. In 2013, a ban on dumping food-waste leachate at sea increased the need for land-based treatment.[1]

This was not a single plan completed at launch. Each rule exposed the next capability the system had to build.

## From a paid bag to a weight the household can see

Seoul used designated bags that residents bought in advance. The bag connected cost to volume, but only approximately. It became waste itself, could contaminate material intended for feed or compost, and was awkward for wet food scraps that could be compressed or stored until the bag was full.[3]

Weight-based bins changed the unit of account. **Radio-frequency identification (RFID)** lets the bin read a card linked to a household. A resident taps the card, the lid opens, the scraps are deposited, and the bin records their weight for billing. The Korea Environment Corporation describes that operating function directly: it measures each household's food-waste disposal automatically and charges by weight.[4]

The physical change looks small; the operating change is not. The household no longer pays only when it buys a bag. It sees the weight at the moment of disposal. The municipality can see where quantities arise instead of relying only on a building or district total.

In 2025, Seoul operated 27,289 RFID units serving 81.6% of apartment residents. Across all housing types, coverage was 37.9%.[1] Those denominators matter: high penetration in apartment complexes does not mean every home in the city is covered.

## The bin is only the front end

Measurement begins a longer chain. Trucks move the separated material to facilities such as the Gangdong Resource Circulation Center in eastern Seoul. There, food waste is shredded, and metals, netting and other contaminants are removed. Water is pressed out. The liquid enters anaerobic digesters, while the solids are dried and screened before becoming, in the facility documented by field reporting, poultry feed.[1]

**Anaerobic digestion** uses microorganisms to break down organic material without oxygen, producing gas that can be used for energy. Without treatment capacity, measurement and separation would simply create another pile elsewhere.

National figures say about 42% of recycled food waste becomes animal feed, 33% compost and 16% biogas.[1] The government classifies 96.8% of 4.81 million tonnes in 2023 as recycled.[1] That is evidence of a broad collection and treatment route, not an independent certificate that every tonne became a safe, valuable and demanded product.

## What the results establish—and what they do not

The clearest citywide measure is the decline from 3,181 tonnes a day in 2013 to 2,419 in 2023, or 23.9%.[1] RFID coverage expanded over that period, but so did a wider system of landfill restrictions, source separation, leachate treatment, designated bags and education. The number establishes the direction of change, not the share attributable to each intervention.

A Seoul case published through SDG Local Action attributes a reduction of 47,000 tonnes over six years to RFID bins. It reports reductions of 10% to 30% for weight charging, compared with 13% for bags and 14% for chips.[3] The page does not disclose a full counterfactual method, so these should be treated as institutional results, not an independently established citywide causal estimate.

Academic evidence gets closer to the incentive mechanism. A natural experiment in Mapo-gu compared 12 apartment complexes using household-level weight charging with 61 control complexes. It concluded that assigning the cost to the household's actual weight was more effective than charging the building collectively.[5] A later study of Korean household data from 2019 to 2022 found weight-based PAYT more effective than volume-based systems and found that public awareness and food-preservation practices strengthened performance.[6]

The implication is not that price works alone. It works when the fee is understandable, the weight is visible, separation is convenient and collection is reliable.

## Recycling is not the same as preventing waste

The system's limits appear at the end of the chain. Compost can carry too much salt, and supply can exceed demand. Feed routes have paused during animal-disease concerns. Earlier machines have corroded in contact with salty Korean food. Central installation funding ended in 2014, leaving municipalities with less fiscal capacity slower to expand and replace equipment.[1][2]

Measurement adds another ambiguity. Draining water before disposal lowers the recorded weight, but it does not necessarily mean a household bought or cooked less food. A city therefore needs separate measures for three outcomes: lower wet weight, prevention of edible food from becoming waste, and useful treatment of what cannot be prevented.

Seoul has set a target to reduce food waste by 20% by 2030 from a 2019 baseline while expanding household measurement tools.[7] It is a target, not an achieved result.

## The 10X lens

The evidence supports seven of the eight official 10X pillars. Pillar 1 remains an evidence gap that should not be filled by inference.

- **Redefining value and strategic direction:** The service shifted from removal alone to prevention, measurement and recovery.
- **Leadership, transformation culture and future governance:** National rules set the direction; municipalities financed, implemented and operated the system.
- **Multiplying capabilities and empowerment:** Households see weight, municipalities see more granular data, and plants receive a dedicated stream. “Empowerment” should not disguise the fact that separation is also mandatory.
- **Reinventing the institution and operating model:** Household behavior, billing, collection and treatment became one operating chain.
- **Innovation and experimentation:** The system evolved from bags and chips to more precise household weight charging, with comparative evidence from apartment complexes.
- **Institutional and technical intelligence:** Each deposit became linked data—identity, weight and fee—not anonymous waste.
- **Sustainable value and outcomes:** Reported quantities fell and treatment routes expanded, while cost, maintenance and output-quality constraints remained visible.

The evidence does not show that the historical transformation began with a formal foresight method. It was a practical response to landfill and leachate pressure. Seoul's 2030 target adds a future direction, but it does not rewrite the system's origin.

## What another city or organization can transfer

Buying bins is the last question, not the first. Before selecting technology, leaders have to decide who pays, what unit is measured, how disputes are handled, how low-income households are protected, who funds maintenance and where the material goes after collection.

They also need a baseline that does not confuse weight with actual food prevention, trials across different housing types and a scale decision tied to data quality and safe final outputs. The public record does not disclose a complete citywide pilot protocol or formal scale gates, so another city would need to design those controls before investing.

If no facility can process the separated stream, the bin may measure successfully while the city fails to create value.

## The 10X question

If a city ten years from now must prevent edible food from becoming waste and safely recover what cannot be prevented, what must it be able to see and decide at household, neighborhood and treatment-plant levels? Which pricing rules, data, skills, maintenance systems and output markets must it start building now?
