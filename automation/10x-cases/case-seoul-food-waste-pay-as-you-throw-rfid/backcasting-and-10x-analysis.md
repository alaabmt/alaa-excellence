# Backcasting and 10X analysis — Seoul food waste

## The desired future

A city in which avoidable food waste falls before collection, unavoidable organic material is separated cleanly, households can see the consequence of what they discard, and recovery capacity produces safe outputs with real demand rather than merely moving waste to a different accounting category.

Seoul's published 2030 target—20% less food waste than in 2019—gives this future a measurable direction. It is an announced target, not proof that the end state exists.

## Work backward from that future

### Required future outcomes

- Lower absolute food-waste generation, not only higher collection.
- Fair and visible charging linked to actual disposal.
- High-quality separation with low contamination.
- Reliable municipal collection and maintenance.
- Processing routes whose feed, compost or biogas outputs meet safety and market requirements.
- Data capable of showing who is reducing waste, where coverage is weak and which interventions work.
- Governance that can update pricing, incentives and technology as housing patterns and costs change.

### Capabilities that must exist now

1. **Household identification and measurement:** distinguish individual household disposal from building-level totals.
2. **Pricing and billing:** translate weight into a fee without making access impractical.
3. **Citizen understanding:** make sorting rules and the reason for them visible.
4. **Operations and maintenance:** keep bins, sensors, cards and billing links working despite moisture, salt and corrosion.
5. **Collection logistics:** move separated material without recontamination.
6. **Processing capacity:** dewater, screen and route solids and liquids safely.
7. **Outcome measurement:** separate reductions in wet weight, actual prevention, collection coverage and final beneficial use.
8. **Adaptive governance:** decide when to change fees, offer rewards, subsidize installation or replace aging equipment.

## What changed in the operating model

The central change was not “smart bins.” It was the redistribution of information, responsibility and cost.

Before: disposal cost was weakly connected to the quantity a household discarded; mixed waste pushed the problem downstream; communities and municipalities absorbed consequences collectively.

After: the household separates food waste; the system records a unit or weight; the fee follows that unit; municipal systems collect the stream separately; dedicated facilities recover material; city and national systems can see patterns that a flat charge hid.

This is the 10X move: **from paying for disposal as an invisible common service to making discarded food a measured household signal inside a citywide recovery system.**

## Supported 10X pillars

### 2. Redefining value and strategic direction — supported

Value moved from “remove waste” to “prevent, separate, measure and recover.” The charging design makes discarded quantity part of the service relationship.

Evidence strength: high.

### 3. Leadership, transformation culture and future governance — supported with limits

National rules, municipal implementation and public education created a consistent direction over decades. Local financing and uneven capacity show governance is not frictionless.

Evidence strength: medium-high. Internal decision rights and escalation mechanisms are not fully public.

### 4. Multiplying capabilities and empowerment — supported

Households gained immediate information about quantity and price; municipalities gained granular operational data; processing operators gained a cleaner dedicated stream.

Evidence strength: medium-high. “Empowerment” should not be romanticized: compliance is also mandatory and priced.

### 5. Reinventing the institution and operating model — strongly supported

The system links household behaviour, billing, collection, processing and recovery markets. This is a value-chain redesign, not a standalone digital feature.

Evidence strength: high.

### 6. Innovation and experimentation — supported with limits

Seoul moved from bags and chips toward household-level weight charging, and comparative apartment evidence tested incentive design. No complete public citywide pilot-to-scale protocol was found.

Evidence strength: medium-high.

### 7. Institutional and technical intelligence — strongly supported

RFID turns each deposit into operational data linked to a household account. The critical intelligence is not the tag itself but the capacity to connect identity, weight, fee and service operations.

Evidence strength: high for function; medium for published evidence on how managers use analytics beyond billing.

### 8. Sustainable value and results — supported with explicit boundaries

Food-waste tonnage fell in Seoul; studied complexes show stronger reductions; national recycling classifications are very high. Yet compost quality, surplus outputs, feed safety interruptions and maintenance costs prevent a simple “closed loop solved” conclusion.

Evidence strength: high for reported outcomes; medium for net environmental benefit and final beneficial use.

## Unsupported / gap

### 1. Future foresight and ambition — not claimed as a full pillar

The present 2030 target is future-oriented, but the historical redesign emerged largely from landfill and leachate pressures. No evidence was found that the original transformation used formal foresight, scenarios or an explicit ten-year capability architecture. The article may use backcasting analytically, but it must not say Seoul formally used foresight.

## Causal map

- Landfill and leachate pressures made mixed disposal untenable.
- Separation rules created a distinct material stream.
- PAYT linked cost to quantity.
- RFID made the link household-specific and visible.
- Separate collection made dedicated processing possible.
- Processing capacity created recovery routes.
- The combined system coincided with falling tonnage and produced high reported recycling rates.
- Maintenance, local finance and output quality constrain the model.

No single arrow should be treated as the whole explanation.

## Transferability conditions

A different city should not begin by buying bins. It should test:

- Does law require source separation?
- Can billing identify the responsible unit fairly?
- What will happen in informal housing, high-rise buildings and small households?
- Who pays for installation and replacement?
- Where does the material go after collection?
- Is there demand for compost, feed or biogas?
- Can the city distinguish moisture removal from true food-waste prevention?
- What protection is needed for low-income households?
- Which institution owns performance across the whole chain?

## Transferable management lessons

1. Price the behaviour only after the operating chain can absorb the changed behaviour.
2. Put feedback at the point of action; a monthly city statistic cannot shape a household decision as directly as a visible weight and charge.
3. Treat technology as the meter, not the transformation.
4. Measure prevention separately from recovery.
5. Design for maintenance and local fiscal capacity before scaling.
6. Keep the target on avoidable waste; recycling is a fallback, not the highest-value outcome.

## Closing 10X question

If a city ten years from now must prevent avoidable food waste before it is created—and recover the unavoidable remainder safely—what must it be able to see and decide at household, neighborhood and processing levels? Which pricing rules, data, citizen capabilities, maintenance systems and recovery markets must it begin building now?
