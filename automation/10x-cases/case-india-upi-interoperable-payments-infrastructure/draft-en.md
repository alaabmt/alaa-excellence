# How India Turned Instant Payments into Shared Infrastructure

**Publication date:** 22 October 2026  
**Reading time:** About 9 minutes

## Executive snapshot

India did not need one more payment app. It needed to prevent digital payments from fragmenting into closed networks, where an app is useful only if the bank, customer, merchant and counterparty all join the same environment. The decisive move therefore began below the interface: redesign the infrastructure to which interfaces connect.

The National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI), promoted by banks under Reserve Bank of India guidance, launched the Unified Payments Interface (UPI) in April 2016. It enabled instant transfers between participating bank accounts through different apps under shared participation and oversight rules. “Open” in this case means regulated interoperability, not an unrestricted network. Third-party app providers and merchant QR codes subsequently broadened access.

In 2025, UPI processed **228.28 billion transactions** worth **₹300 trillion**, according to an RBI report published in May 2026. In September 2026 alone, NPCI recorded **24.07 billion transactions** worth **₹29.37 trillion** across **756 banks**. An International Monetary Fund study using granular transaction data found evidence consistent with interoperability—the ability to pay across banks and apps—materially supporting adoption.

Scale, however, is not the same as inclusion or sustainability. Adoption coincided with digital identity, bank-account expansion, affordable mobile data and the 2016 demonetisation shock. More than 95% of user-initiated transactions in the IMF study were concentrated in three apps. The case therefore carries a double lesson: shared infrastructure can free a market from closed networks, but it does not automatically prevent power from concentrating at the access layer.

## The challenge: when the app becomes part of the problem

In a closed payment network, a provider has to attract both sides of a transaction into the same environment. A bank or app must build separate connections and agreements; a merchant faces more acceptance paths; and the customer encounters a deceptively simple but systemically expensive question: can the other party use the same channel?

Improving each standalone app could make interfaces faster, but it would not remove the coordination cost among banks, apps and merchants. India needed an environment in which a transaction remained useful even when sender and recipient chose different providers.

The constraint was not purely technical. The system also depended on bank accounts, digital identity, affordable mobile connectivity and rules assigning responsibility among the operator, banks and app providers. UPI cannot therefore be treated as the single cause of India’s shift to digital payments.

## The future state: competition above a shared rail

The strategic move separated two layers that closed networks usually combine: the core transfer infrastructure and the customer interface.

At the first layer, UPI supplied common rules and a real-time path among participating bank accounts. At the second, banks and technology companies could offer different apps and compete on usability, services and reach. A successful payment no longer required both parties to use the same app.

That separation redefined value. Each provider no longer had to own a network from end to end. Connection to the shared rail became a collective capability, while competition remained in the layers customers could see.

## How the capability was built

### 1. One operator and common rules

NPCI operated the system as an organisation promoted by banks under RBI guidance. The model gave the ecosystem an entity to coordinate rules and connectivity while regulated banks continued to hold customer accounts.

Its value was not centralisation for its own sake. A common connection and rule set reduced the need for every participant to assemble a separate closed network. It did not remove banks’ and other participants’ obligations for compliance, settlement, fraud management and customer service, nor did it make the system costless.

### 2. Opening the interface to app providers

UPI began with bank participation, then third-party app providers expanded customer choice. The app became an access point to a bank account that could transact across the network, rather than an isolated wallet that required both parties to hold value inside the same environment.

This did not distribute the market evenly. Interoperability at the back end can coexist with significant concentration at the front end—and it did.

### 3. Lowering merchant-acceptance friction

QR codes made it easier to extend payment acceptance without a complex acceptance stack for every network. This completed the supply side: easy money transfer does not become an everyday payment system unless customers encounter merchants that can accept it.

The evidence reviewed here does not compare merchant acceptance cost or dispute experience across providers, so the article does not claim uniformly lower merchant economics.

### 4. Scaling a working capability

The system did not stop at technical readiness. Participation and use grew, and smaller payments became more common. The average UPI ticket fell from **₹1,848 in 2021** to **₹1,313 in 2025**. That pattern is consistent with more frequent everyday use, but it does not by itself prove that previously excluded consumers adopted UPI.

## Results: from technical layer to widespread behaviour

The RBI Payment Systems Report shows UPI volume rising from **38.73 billion transactions in 2021** to **228.28 billion in 2025**, a reported compound annual growth rate of **55.8%**. Value rose from **₹72 trillion** to **₹300 trillion**, a **43%** compound annual growth rate.

In financial year 2024–25, UPI accounted for **84% of India’s retail-payment volume**. NPCI’s September 2026 figures show continued operation at vast scale: **24.07 billion transactions**, **₹29.37 trillion** and **756 participating banks**. This is a dated monthly snapshot, not an annual forecast.

More important than the headline growth is what IMF research says about the mechanism. Using granular transactions and two episodes in which participation conditions changed, the study found evidence consistent with interoperability playing a central role in adoption. That supports the conclusion that shared design affected diffusion; it does not justify calling it the only cause.

## What the numbers do not say

Transaction counts do not automatically measure economic welfare or consumer protection. Aggregate value does not show who benefited most, who remained outside the system or how operating costs were distributed.

Nor does shared infrastructure eliminate the power of large platforms. More than **95%** of user-initiated transactions in the IMF study were concentrated in three apps. Cross-app transfer may be technically possible while distribution, habit and network effects still pull users towards a small number of interfaces.

At this scale, outages, fraud, dispute resolution and operational resilience become structural concerns. The sources reviewed do not provide a basis for comparing all participants’ failure rates, merchant costs or dispute experience, or for concluding that the current pricing model guarantees sustainable economics for every participant.

## Where the 10X distinction lies

### Future foresight and ambition

The desired future was not a larger digital wallet. It was a market in which customers and merchants could pay across different service providers. That shifted the unit of design from the app to the ecosystem.

### Redefining value and strategic direction

Value moved from keeping both parties inside one channel to completing transactions across a shared network. Common infrastructure let participants compete on service instead of duplicating the core rail.

### Leadership, transformation culture and future governance

The sources establish coordination among the RBI, banks and NPCI and show an institutional division of roles. They are not sufficient to judge leadership culture inside those organisations. The strength of the case is therefore shared governance, not an undocumented cultural transformation.

### Multiplying capabilities and empowerment

Common rules enabled hundreds of banks and more than 200 apps, according to the IMF’s 2025 analysis, to use the same rail. QR acceptance expanded merchants’ ability to participate. Participant counts, however, do not prove equal use across them.

### Reinventing the institution and operating model

UPI separated operation of the shared rail from delivery of the interface. Roles changed: banks held accounts, the operator coordinated the network, and apps competed for access and experience.

### Innovation and experimentation to accelerate transformation

Implementation evolved from a bank-led launch to third-party apps and widespread QR acceptance. These stages show practical adaptation, but the available sources do not document a complete formal experimentation portfolio or isolate the effect of every rollout decision.

### Maximising institutional and technical intelligence

A digital rail generated granular data and made it possible to monitor transactions across a broad ecosystem. Nothing in the reviewed evidence supports attributing UPI’s success to artificial intelligence or claiming comprehensive superiority in fraud detection.

### Maximising value and sustainable results

Scale, payment share and everyday use show that the design became a widely used operating capability. Full sustainability remains conditional on resilience, consumer protection, market diversity and participant economics.

The case does not establish a uniform tenfold performance gain. Its 10X value lies in redesigning the coordination architecture of an entire market and turning that design into measurable use.

## What other institutions can transfer

- Start with the coordination failure, not the tool. If customers and partners are trapped in incompatible channels, another app may deepen fragmentation.
- Separate the layer that should be shared from the layers that should compete. Common standards can expand the market for innovation.
- Design participation, settlement and risk rules alongside the technology.
- Build both sides of the network. Customer ability to pay is insufficient without merchant acceptance, and vice versa.
- Measure actual use, not participant counts or design capacity alone.
- Watch interface concentration even when back-end infrastructure is interoperable.
- Do not use platform scale as a proxy for inclusion, public value or consumer protection.

## A future-facing 10X question

**Which layer in your organisation is still rebuilt inside every product or unit, when it could become a shared capability that lets others innovate above it?** What access and governance rules would stop that shared capability from becoming a new monopoly?
