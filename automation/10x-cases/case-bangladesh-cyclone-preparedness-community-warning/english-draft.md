# How Bangladesh Turned Cyclone Warnings into Community Action

## Executive Snapshot
A forecast cannot protect a coastal family unless the warning is understood and evacuation is possible. Bangladesh's Cyclone Preparedness Programme (CPP), established in 1972 and jointly governed by the government and Bangladesh Red Crescent Society since 1973, connects official meteorological warnings with trained community volunteers who help residents prepare, evacuate and reach shelter. The Red Crescent reports approximately 76,020 volunteers in 3,801 local units. In December 2019, the UN Office for Disaster Risk Reduction reported that CPP evacuated 2.1 million people ahead of Cyclone Bulbul. These are meaningful indicators of organisational reach and event-level action, not a credible estimate of lives saved by CPP alone. A separate study of two communities found that approximately 49% of surveyed residents could not interpret the warning message, exposing a critical gap between dissemination and understanding. [1–4]

## The problem was never just forecasting
Bangladesh's low-lying coast is exposed to tropical cyclones, storm surges and flooding. Better forecasts help, but they do not solve the household decision to leave, the practical difficulty of moving an older relative, or the risk of abandoning possessions. A warning can be accurate and still fail to trigger protective action.

A 2022 study surveyed 177 residents and 23 CPP volunteers in two subdistricts. It found shortcomings in warning comprehension and identified constraints involving equipment, volunteer training, coordination and contact with communities. The sample does not represent all of Bangladesh; it nevertheless shows why delivering a signal is a different outcome from securing an effective response. [3]

## Working backward from a safer coast
The intended future is one in which a resident at risk receives a clear, trusted warning and can act on it in time. That requires more than meteorological capability. It calls for trusted local intermediaries, shelters, accessible evacuation support, equipment, training and arrangements to learn after each storm. This is an analytical backcasting of required capabilities, not a claim that CPP formally adopted a particular foresight method.

## The institutional redesign
CPP began in 1972 and became a joint government–Red Crescent programme in 1973. Its design joins official forecasts to a distributed volunteer network. Volunteers communicate warnings, assist evacuation and sheltering, undertake rescue and provide first aid. The Bangladesh Red Crescent reports 3,801 units and approximately 76,020 volunteers, evenly divided between women and men in its published inventory. Those figures describe organisational resources; they do not establish how many volunteers were deployed in a particular storm or how many households received and understood the message. [1, 2]

The crucial operating shift is a connected chain: **official warning → local interpretation → protective action**. Community volunteers can identify residents needing assistance and explain warnings in familiar settings. That advantage depends on sustained equipment, safety provisions, training and coordination. The independent study found weaknesses in precisely these areas. [3]

## What the evidence actually shows
In a report dated 16 December 2019, UNDRR stated that CPP evacuated **2.1 million people** before Cyclone Bulbul. This is a reported event-specific operational result—not a design capacity or annual reach estimate. The same report described approximately 55,000–56,000 volunteers at the time and an ambition to expand to 200,000. An ambition is not an achieved staffing level. The later Red Crescent figure of 76,020 is not precisely dated on its source page and must not be retroactively assigned to 2019. [1, 4]

Cyclone deaths have declined compared with some historic catastrophes, but the storms are not interchangeable experiments. Intensity, track, population exposure, shelter provision, infrastructure and warning capability all vary. The evidence reviewed here does not support a CPP-specific percentage reduction in deaths. Nor does operational reach guarantee comprehension: approximately 49% of respondents in the two communities studied in 2022 said they could not interpret the warning message. That is a sample finding, not a national failure rate. [3, 5]

## The 10X institutional lens
**Redefining value:** The output is not a bulletin; it is a warning that people can use. **Leadership and governance:** Government and the Red Crescent share responsibility within a joint programme. **Capabilities and empowerment:** Community volunteers provide local capacity, but their equipment and continuity remain constraints. **Reinventing the operating model:** A central forecast becomes a decentralised protective service. **Institutional intelligence:** Official weather information is translated into local action, without implying an undocumented AI system. **Results and sustainable value:** Evacuations are documented, while mortality attribution remains unresolved. There is insufficient evidence here to claim formal strategic foresight or a specific experimental innovation method.

## What other institutions can transfer
Four questions matter: Who owns the authoritative signal? Who makes it understandable? Who helps people unable to act alone? What evidence shows that people acted, rather than merely received a message? A transferable model requires resources, drills, accountability and accessible evacuation—not just a volunteer roster. Inclusion and responder safety should be designed into the system and measured.

## The 10X question
If an alert reached a community but people could neither understand nor act on it, did the institution complete its job?
