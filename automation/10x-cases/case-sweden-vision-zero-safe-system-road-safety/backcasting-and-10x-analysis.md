# Backcasting and 10X Analysis — Sweden Vision Zero

## Target future and backcast

The target is not “fewer crashes.” It is a road system in which inevitable human errors do not produce death or lifelong injury. Working backward from that outcome produces five non-negotiable design questions:

1. What impact forces can the human body survive?
2. Which road, vehicle and speed combinations keep forces below those limits?
3. Who controls each system component and what accountability must they accept?
4. Which leading indicators reveal risk before fatality counts do?
5. How will the system learn when targets are missed?

Sweden's transformation is analytically significant because it converted these questions into operating constraints: median separation where speed and traffic justify it; lower speed where separation is absent; safer vehicles and junctions; cameras and alcolocks; crash investigation; annual indicator review; and shared priorities across state, industry, municipalities and other actors.

## Root cause logic

The visible problem was death and serious injury. The deeper institutional problem was fragmentation: road designers, vehicle makers, enforcement bodies and road users could each optimise their own domain while the combined system still transmitted lethal energy. A behaviour-only model also assigned responsibility mainly after an error occurred. Vision Zero changed the unit of management from “the driver” to “the road-transport system.”

The 2007 interim target was missed. Rather than conceal this, Sweden's subsequent review identified weak stakeholder involvement, insufficiently useful targets, unclear governance and inadequate monitoring. The 2008–2009 management-by-objectives redesign added leading indicators and a recurring joint review cycle. That adjustment is central to the case: ambition was made credible through institutional learning, not through the original declaration alone.

## Eight official 10X pillars

### 1. Future foresight and ambition — strongly supported

Vision Zero defined a future state that rejected preventable death as the price of mobility. The goal was deliberately beyond the reach of a single planning cycle, so interim targets were used without diluting the end state.

**Gap:** zero remains unmet; 213 people died on Swedish roads in 2024, and the 2030 interim ceiling is 133.

### 2. Redefining value and strategic direction — strongly supported

The value proposition changed from maximising mobility subject to incremental safety improvements to delivering mobility within human injury-tolerance limits. Crashes became a design input; fatal outcomes became the failure condition.

**Gap:** speed, accessibility and regional-development trade-offs remain politically and operationally contested.

### 3. Leadership, transformation culture and future governance — strongly supported

Parliamentary adoption created legitimacy, while the Swedish Transport Administration led coordination. Governance evolved toward shared objectives, annual evidence reviews, stakeholder conferences and common positions on the next cycle.

**Gap:** shared responsibility can diffuse accountability unless each actor owns measurable commitments. The first interim target exposed this weakness.

### 4. Multiplying capabilities and empowerment — supported

Sweden multiplied capability through joint government-industry-academic work, data systems, crash-investigation expertise, standards, engineering practice and a knowledge-transfer platform. System designers were empowered—and obliged—to redesign conditions rather than wait for perfect road-user behaviour.

**Gap:** the sources do not quantify workforce capacity or budget sufficiency. This pillar should be discussed through capability architecture, not unsupported claims about staff transformation.

### 5. Reinventing the institution and operating model — strongly supported

The institution shifted from reactive accident prevention and user correction toward Safe System management: shared responsibility, human-tolerance design, leading indicators, stage targets and recurring learning.

**Gap:** the operating model spans multiple autonomous actors; it is a coordinated system, not one reorganised institution.

### 6. Innovation and experimentation — strongly supported

The first median-barrier pilot opened in 1998. Sweden then tested and scaled 2+1 road designs, differentiated speeds by road protection, and combined new measures with re-purposed technologies such as cameras, roundabouts and alcolocks.

**Gap:** some measures create secondary risks or trade-offs; barriers can affect motorcyclists, and road redesign affects capacity and maintenance. Experimentation requires continued evaluation.

### 7. Institutional and technical intelligence — strongly supported

STRADA, fatal-crash in-depth studies, annual trend reports and leading safety indicators turn road conditions into management information. The intelligence model does not wait for annual death totals; it tracks speed compliance, safe-road exposure, sobriety and other precursors.

**Gap:** injury-series comparability depends on definitions and reporting coverage. Finalised data can differ from preliminary counts, as seen in the 2024 fatality revision from 210 to 213.

### 8. Sustainable value and results — supported with causal caution

OECD/ITF data show 213 road deaths in 2024, 21.1% below the 2014 count of 270. A controlled before–after evaluation found 50% fewer fatalities and serious injuries on evaluated narrow 2+1 sections, and a separate speed-limit study estimated about 16 fewer serious injuries per year on affected 2+1 roads.

**Gap:** the national trend is multi-causal and began before 1997. Vehicle renewal, enforcement, exposure, economic conditions and medical response also matter. Sustainable value is strongest at the intervention level; national attribution remains medium-confidence.

## What changed, why it mattered, what transfers

### What changed

- Responsibility expanded from road users to system designers.
- Safe speeds became conditional on road and vehicle protection.
- Infrastructure prevented high-energy conflicts rather than merely warning against them.
- Fatality targets were joined by leading indicators.
- Data review became a recurring governance cycle.
- Missed targets triggered redesign of the management system.

### Why it mattered

The approach reduced dependence on flawless behaviour. A centre barrier can prevent a head-on collision even after a mistake; a lower speed can keep impact energy survivable; an indicator can expose a risky network gap before another death. The institution learned to manage the conditions that shape harm, not only the actions immediately preceding it.

### What transfers

1. Start with an explicit unacceptable outcome and design backward.
2. Define physical or operational tolerance limits.
3. Map which actor controls each risk-producing system component.
4. Pair lagging outcomes with verified leading indicators.
5. Pilot interventions, evaluate with comparison methods and scale only after evidence.
6. Publish missed targets and use them to redesign governance.
7. Treat the name of a programme as non-transferable; transfer the accountability, investment, data and design rules.

## Analytical guardrails for drafting

- Do not present Vision Zero as a finished success.
- Do not attribute the entire national decline to one policy.
- Keep the 50% and 63% figures tied to evaluated narrow 2+1 sections.
- Use 213 as the finalised 2024 count and note the preliminary-data issue only if needed.
- Distinguish 5,620 km of all median-protected roads from 2+1-road length.
- Include the failed 2007 target because it explains the governance redesign.
- Explain STRADA at first mention.
- Embed the official Trafikverket video after the analytical body and before the disclaimer/references.
