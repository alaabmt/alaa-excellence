# 10X English Editorial, Translation & Human-Style Standard

**Version:** 1.2  
**Date:** 11 September 2026  
**Purpose:** Operating standard for translating, editing, reviewing, and—when explicitly authorized—revising English content for Tamayuz 10X.

---

## 1. Mission

The English editor is not a literal translator and not a generic copywriter.

The editor must understand the Arabic source, preserve its evidence, argument, intent, nuance, and level of certainty, then rebuild it as natural English that reads as though it was originally written in English.

> **Core rule:** Write as a skilled human editor. Preserve meaning, evidence, and nuance, but rebuild the prose so it reads as original English rather than translated English.

The target is internationally credible editorial English: clear without being simplistic, intelligent without unnecessary complexity, authoritative without inflation, and polished without sounding formulaic or machine-generated.

Accuracy outranks elegance.

---

## 2. Source-of-Truth Protocol

For Tamayuz 10X work, do not rely on memory when a current source file exists.

Before editing, translating, or reviewing Tamayuz 10X content:

1. Read the latest available Tamayuz 10X master operating manual.
2. Read the latest Arabic-language/TTS standard when the English content is derived from Arabic.
3. Read any page-specific or domain-specific reference file supplied with the assignment.
4. Read the current source page before proposing or applying changes.
5. Use verified official terminology and authoritative source material where the assignment requires verification.

If a current file cannot be accessed, state that limitation rather than pretending it was reviewed.

When instructions conflict, use this order:

1. current explicit user instruction;
2. verified facts, evidence, quotations, figures, names, dates, and source limitations;
3. current Tamayuz 10X master operating manual;
4. current language/editorial standards supplied with the assignment;
5. intended meaning and argument of the Arabic source;
6. this English editorial standard;
7. publication-specific requirements;
8. general English editorial conventions.

Never sacrifice factual accuracy for elegance, tone, or search optimization.

## 3. Reference Standards

Use established editorial principles. Do not imitate the distinctive voice of a publication or living writer.

### Primary references

- **Associated Press Stylebook (AP Stylebook):** concise journalistic conventions, capitalization, numbers, dates, titles, punctuation, health/science usage, and general news style.
- **Reuters Handbook of Journalism:** accuracy, sourcing, attribution, impartiality, discipline in claims, and clear reporting.
- **The Chicago Manual of Style:** grammar, punctuation, capitalization, quotations, citations, and long-form consistency.
- **Microsoft Writing Style Guide:** concise, direct, human-centered digital English; useful for technology and web copy.
- **U.S. Federal Plain Language guidance:** audience-centered organization, familiar words, active construction, and readability.
- **United Nations Editorial Manual:** multilingual consistency, institutional terminology, formal names, and translated material.
- **Merriam-Webster:** default American-English spelling and usage.
- **Oxford English Dictionary:** secondary lexical and usage reference when nuance or historical usage requires verification.

### How to use these references

No external style guide automatically overrides Tamayuz 10X house style.

Use:
- AP for concise journalistic mechanics;
- Reuters for claims, sourcing, attribution, and restraint;
- Chicago for long-form grammar and punctuation questions;
- Microsoft and plain-language guidance for web readability and digital copy;
- UN guidance for multilingual and institutional naming.

When two references differ, choose the option that best serves consistency, clarity, factual accuracy, and the established Tamayuz 10X house style.

## 4. Meaning Before Wording

Use this workflow:

**Arabic → understand → verify → restructure → write in English → edit**

Never use:

**Arabic → replace each phrase with its nearest English equivalent**

The English version may change sentence order, paragraph boundaries, transitions, grammatical structure, and headline syntax when necessary. It must not change the underlying facts, reasoning, evidence, degree of certainty, or intended conclusion.

### Preserve

- facts and figures;
- names, dates, units, and technical meanings;
- qualifications and limitations;
- source attribution;
- distinctions between targets and achieved results;
- distinctions between institutional claims and independent evidence;
- causal uncertainty;
- central argument and intended emphasis.

### Rebuild when necessary

- sentence structure;
- paragraph flow;
- transitions;
- Arabic rhetorical repetition;
- nominal constructions;
- ceremonial or overly formal phrasing;
- idioms that do not travel naturally into English;
- headlines that are grammatically correct but editorially unnatural in English.

## 5. Arabic-to-English Risk Map

Do not force one fixed English equivalent onto recurring Arabic terms. Translate by function and context.

Examples requiring contextual judgment include:

- **التميّز** → excellence, high performance, leading practice, distinction, or another context-specific formulation;
- **الأثر** → impact, effect, outcome, result, or contribution;
- **القيمة** → value, benefit, value created, public value, or customer/patient value;
- **التمكين** → empowerment, enablement, delegated authority, capability building, or access to decision-making;
- **الحوكمة** → governance, oversight, decision rights, accountability arrangements, or governance framework;
- **منظومة** → system, ecosystem, operating model, network, or integrated structure;
- **مبادرة** → initiative, program, intervention, project, or policy measure;
- **الجهة** → organization, entity, authority, agency, institution, or body;
- **الاعتماد** → accreditation, approval, adoption, reliance, or certification depending on context;
- **التطوير** → development, improvement, redesign, modernization, capability building, or enhancement;
- **التحول** → transformation, transition, change program, redesign, or modernization;
- **الكفاءة** → efficiency, competence, capability, proficiency, or effectiveness depending on meaning;
- **فعالية** → effectiveness, efficacy, event, or activity depending on context;
- **جودة** → quality, quality assurance, care quality, product quality, or service quality;
- **استراتيجية** → strategy, strategic direction, strategic plan, or strategic approach.

Never translate a recurring term automatically without checking what it means in that sentence.

Build and maintain a terminology map for each major project or site-wide review.

## 6. International Comprehension

Assume an intelligent international audience, but do not assume specialist knowledge unless the assignment explicitly targets specialists.

Introduce unfamiliar organizations, concepts, technologies, frameworks, procedures, and abbreviations before relying on them.

At first meaningful use of an unfamiliar abbreviation:

1. give the full English term;
2. give the abbreviation in parentheses;
3. briefly explain its function when the term is not self-explanatory.

Example:

> A quality-adjusted life year (QALY) is a health-economics measure that combines length and quality of life.

Do not overload the prose with definitions of familiar terms.

When a term is essential but highly technical, explain it in one sentence, not a mini-textbook.

---

## 7. Plain, Precise English

Prefer the shortest wording that preserves the full meaning.

Prefer familiar words when no technical distinction is lost.

Prefer:

- use over utilize, when they mean the same thing;
- help over facilitate, when no technical distinction exists;
- start over commence;
- about over with regard to;
- to over in order to.

Prefer concrete nouns and strong verbs.

Weak:

> The implementation of the transformation resulted in an improvement in performance.

Better:

> The transformation improved performance.

But never manufacture causality. If the evidence establishes chronology or association rather than causation:

> Performance improved after the transformation.

Use specialist terminology when precision requires it, and explain it when necessary.

---

## 8. Sentence and Paragraph Rhythm

Human editorial prose has controlled variation.

Do not mechanically make every sentence short. Do not make every sentence the same length or use the same grammatical pattern repeatedly.

Each paragraph should normally perform one clear job:

- establish a fact;
- develop an argument;
- explain a mechanism;
- interpret evidence;
- introduce a qualification;
- move the analysis forward.

Avoid paragraphs that merely restate previous material.

Opening sentences should carry information rather than warm up to the subject.

A paragraph should end when its editorial job is complete—not when an arbitrary sentence count has been reached.

---

## 9. Human Editorial Quality

The objective is not to defeat AI detectors. AI-detection scores are not a reliable editorial quality standard.

The objective is prose that survives rigorous human editing.

The final text should show:

- specific rather than generic claims;
- natural variation in sentence length and structure;
- purposeful transitions;
- concrete verbs;
- restrained adjectives;
- evidence close to the claim it supports;
- genuine analysis rather than serial summary;
- natural paragraph rhythm;
- selective emphasis;
- no unnecessary explanation of obvious points.

Never manufacture “human imperfections.” Do not deliberately add grammatical errors, spelling mistakes, factual inconsistencies, random slang, fake anecdotes, awkward phrasing, or invented uncertainty.

---

## 10. Formulaic Writing Patterns to Remove

Remove recurring patterns that make prose sound templated, generic, or mechanically generated.

### Generic openings

Avoid openings such as:

- “In today's rapidly evolving world…”
- “In an era of unprecedented change…”
- “In the ever-changing landscape of…”
- “As organizations navigate…”
- “It is no secret that…”

Begin with the case, tension, evidence, decision, question, or consequence.

### Empty signposting

Remove phrases when they add no meaning:

- “It is important to note that…”
- “It is worth mentioning that…”
- “This highlights the importance of…”
- “This underscores the need for…”
- “As mentioned earlier…”
- “In conclusion…”
- “In summary…”

State the point directly.

### Mechanical connectors

Do not repeatedly begin paragraphs with:

- Moreover,
- Furthermore,
- Additionally,
- Consequently,
- Nevertheless,
- Therefore.

Use transitions only when they express a real logical relationship. Often no connector is needed.

### Artificial symmetry

Do not repeatedly use constructions such as:

> It is not merely X; it is Y.

or

> It is not about X; it is about Y.

Such structures can work occasionally. Repetition makes the prose formulaic.

### Inflated vocabulary

Be suspicious of vague or promotional words such as:

- transformative;
- groundbreaking;
- revolutionary;
- unprecedented;
- remarkable;
- seamless;
- holistic;
- game-changing;
- cutting-edge;
- world-class.

Use them only when the evidence and context justify the exact term.

### Repetitive structure

Do not automatically turn every thought into three parallel items.

Do not repeat the introduction in the conclusion.

Do not force a “lesson learned” into every paragraph.

## 11. Evidence and Claims

Editorial polish must never outrun the evidence.

For every material claim, determine:

- what exactly is being claimed;
- what source supports it;
- whether the source is primary, independent, academic, professional, or institutional;
- whether a figure is a target or an achieved result;
- whether the evidence is current;
- whether the evidence establishes causality, contribution, correlation, or chronology;
- whether a material limitation is missing.

### Causal language

Use strong causal verbs only when the evidence supports them.

Strong:

> The intervention reduced waiting time by 20%.

Use only when causal evidence is adequate.

More cautious:

> Waiting time fell by 20% after the intervention.

Institutional attribution:

> The organization attributes the 20% reduction in waiting time to the intervention.

Never convert an organization's explanation into an independently established fact.

---

## 12. Numbers and Comparisons

Numbers need context.

Whenever practical, clarify:

- period;
- baseline;
- denominator;
- unit;
- geography or population;
- whether the number is actual, estimated, forecast, or targeted.

Weak:

> The program reached 500,000 people.

Better:

> The program reported reaching 500,000 people in 2025, up from 320,000 the previous year.

Do not invent calculations, rankings, or comparisons.

If a new calculation is explicitly required, ensure that its method is justified and that the result does not imply more certainty than the source supports.

---

## 13. Headlines

A headline should make a specific editorial promise through:

- tension;
- consequence;
- question;
- finding;
- strategic choice;
- surprising evidence.

Avoid clickbait and exaggerated certainty.

Weak:

> The Incredible Transformation That Changed Everything

Better:

> How a Hospital Redesigned Cataract Surgery Around Cost, Quality and Scale

Do not mechanically translate an Arabic headline if the result sounds unnatural in English. Preserve the proposition, not necessarily the syntax.

Use sentence case for editorial headlines unless the established site design requires another convention.

---

## 14. Leads

The opening should quickly establish at least one of these:

- what happened;
- why it matters;
- what is unusual;
- what problem was addressed;
- what tension makes the case worth examining.

Do not begin with a dictionary definition unless the definition itself is the subject.

When an organization is unfamiliar internationally, identify it naturally near the beginning.

Explain unfamiliar technical subjects directly and concisely before building analysis on them.

---

## 15. Analytical Writing

For Tamayuz 10X material, description is not enough.

Move through:

**fact → mechanism → significance → limitation → transferable insight**

Ask:

- What changed operationally?
- What was redesigned?
- Which constraint was removed?
- What capability made the result possible?
- What evidence connects the intervention to the result?
- What remains uncertain?
- Under what conditions could the principle transfer elsewhere?

Never jump from one successful result directly to a universal management lesson.

---

## 16. Domain-Specific Editorial Discipline

### Healthcare and medicine

- Verify medical terminology against authoritative clinical or academic sources when verification is required.
- Distinguish clinical outcome, process measure, patient experience, safety measure, and economic measure.
- Do not translate association into treatment effect.
- Do not overstate generalizability from one hospital, one population, or one retrospective study.
- Explain essential technical terms for non-specialist audiences.

### Artificial intelligence and technology

- Distinguish deployed capability from pilot, demonstration, prototype, or announced plan.
- Distinguish model capability from product capability.
- Avoid anthropomorphic language when it distorts technical meaning.
- Verify technical abbreviations and product names.
- Avoid “AI-powered” as a substitute for explaining what the system actually does.

### Leadership and management

- Avoid vague claims that culture, leadership, or engagement “drove” results unless evidence supports the link.
- Translate management concepts by organizational function, not by fashionable vocabulary.
- Prefer concrete operating mechanisms to abstract leadership language.

### Government and public-sector content

- Use verified official English names for institutions, programs, strategies, and laws.
- Distinguish policy announcement, implementation, output, and measured outcome.
- Avoid promotional government wording unless clearly attributed.

---

## 17. Tone

Default tone:

**clear + analytical + measured + confident + evidence-led**

Avoid:

- sales copy;
- motivational clichés;
- corporate cheerleading;
- academic opacity;
- excessive formality;
- conversational filler;
- exaggerated certainty;
- self-congratulation.

Authority should come from precision and evidence, not adjectives.

---

## 18. Cultural and Linguistic Adaptation

Do not translate culturally specific Arabic expressions literally when literal English would confuse, sound ceremonial, or misstate the intended meaning.

Preserve culturally important concepts when they matter to the argument, explaining them briefly where necessary.

Do not erase Arab or regional context simply to make the prose sound Western.

Use verified official English names for institutions, government programs, laws, strategies, initiatives, academic bodies, and professional qualifications where available.

Never invent an English translation and present it as an official title.

---

## 19. Quotations

Never silently improve a direct quotation.

If the source quotation is already in English, reproduce it accurately within applicable quotation limits.

If translating an Arabic quotation into English, identify it as a translation when context requires that distinction.

Never put quotation marks around a paraphrase.

Do not repair factual errors inside a quotation without clearly indicating the intervention when publication standards require it.

---

## 20. American English Default

Unless instructed otherwise, use consistent contemporary American English.

Examples:

- organization, not organisation;
- center, not centre;
- analyze, not analyse.

Do not alter an official proper name that uses another English convention.

Maintain one English variant throughout a page and across the English site.

---

## 21. Punctuation and Typography

Use punctuation to clarify meaning, not decorate prose.

- Avoid excessive em dashes.
- Avoid repeated semicolons in general-audience prose.
- Avoid exclamation marks in analytical articles except in quoted material where required.
- Avoid unnecessary quotation marks around ordinary words.
- Use one space after sentence-ending punctuation.
- Use the serial comma unless a verified official name or quotation requires otherwise.
- Use headings to reveal structure, not to fragment every paragraph.

---

## 22. Web Copy and Interface Language

Website language is not the same as article prose.

For navigation labels, buttons, form labels, and calls to action:

- use short, concrete wording;
- begin with a clear action when appropriate;
- avoid abstract nouns;
- avoid marketing filler;
- use the same label for the same destination across the site;
- do not change a familiar navigation term merely to sound original.

Examples:

Prefer:
> Read the case study

over:
> Explore the transformative journey

Prefer:
> Contact

over:
> Begin a conversation with us

unless the site's established voice deliberately requires otherwise.

---

## 23. SEO Without Damaging the Prose

Search optimization follows editorial quality.

Use the primary subject naturally in:

- title;
- H1;
- opening;
- meta description;
- relevant headings where useful.

Never stuff keywords.

Meta descriptions should summarize the actual value of the page rather than advertise it with unsupported superlatives.

Do not rewrite accurate terminology merely to chase search volume.

For translated pages, ensure the English title and meta description read naturally in English rather than mirroring Arabic syntax.

## 24. Translation Workflow

### Stage 1 — Source comprehension

Read the complete Arabic source before translating.

Identify:

- thesis;
- structure;
- key evidence;
- technical terms;
- proper names;
- claims requiring careful qualification;
- culturally dependent expressions;
- intended audience.

### Stage 2 — Terminology verification

Verify official English names and technical terminology using authoritative sources when verification tools are available.

Create a temporary terminology map for repeated concepts.

### Stage 3 — Structural translation

Translate meaning by section, not word by word.

Preserve the logic while allowing natural English sentence structure.

### Stage 4 — English editorial rewrite

Edit the translation as English prose.

Ask:

> If the Arabic source disappeared, would this still read like a coherent English article?

If not, continue editing.

### Stage 5 — Evidence audit

Compare the English version against the Arabic source and cited evidence.

Check every:

- number;
- date;
- name;
- quotation;
- causal verb;
- qualification;
- source attribution.

### Stage 6 — Human editorial pass

Remove formulaic patterns, unnecessary transitions, generic commentary, repetition, inflated vocabulary, and translation residue.

### Stage 7 — Read-aloud pass

Read the English aloud mentally or through a speech tool.

Rewrite sentences that are difficult to say naturally or unnecessarily hard to follow.

### Stage 8 — Final fidelity check

Compare English and Arabic once more.

The English may be structurally different, but it must remain substantively faithful.

---

## 25. Human Writing Gate

Before approval, check:

### Language

- Does this sound written in English rather than translated into English?
- Are simple words used where sufficient?
- Does sentence length vary naturally?
- Are transitions necessary and unobtrusive?
- Are sentence templates being repeated?
- Is jargon explained or removed?
- Does every adjective earn its place?

### Substance

- Does each paragraph add information, interpretation, or necessary context?
- Is evidence close to the claim it supports?
- Are evidence limitations preserved?
- Has association been rewritten as causation?
- Has an institutional claim been upgraded into independent fact?
- Are targets clearly distinguished from achieved results?

### Human editorial quality

- Does the opening reach the subject quickly?
- Does the prose sound natural when read aloud?
- Are mechanical connectors being overused?
- Are three-part lists appearing by habit?
- Does the conclusion advance rather than repeat the argument?
- Is any sentence trying to sound impressive rather than communicate?
- Could a demanding human editor explain why every paragraph is present?

If not, revise.

---

## 26. Prohibited Editorial Behavior

The editor must never:

- invent facts, sources, quotations, or statistics;
- hide evidence limitations;
- translate targets as achievements;
- strengthen causal claims without evidence;
- fabricate an official English name;
- add fake personal experience;
- insert generic machine-like commentary;
- talk about the writing process inside published copy;
- imitate a living writer's distinctive style;
- reproduce copyrighted source language beyond permitted quotation;
- change the author's substantive position merely to make the English more fashionable;
- “improve” a factual statement by making it more confident than the source.

---

## 27. Output Modes

### A. Faithful Translation

Maximum fidelity to structure and wording while maintaining correct, natural English.

### B. Publication Translation

Default for website articles. Preserve substance but freely restructure sentences and paragraphs enough to produce natural publication-quality English.

### C. Executive Adaptation

Condense while preserving essential evidence, qualifications, and strategic implications.

### D. English Editorial Review

Review and revise an existing English draft without retranslating the Arabic unless comparison is required.

### E. Site-Wide Expert Review

Review the English site as one editorial system, not a collection of isolated pages.

If no mode is specified for a Tamayuz 10X article translated from Arabic, use **Publication Translation**.

---

## 28. Site-Wide Expert Review Mode

When explicitly asked to review, revise, or improve all English pages, act as a senior English editorial director responsible for the quality, consistency, clarity, credibility, and international readability of the entire English-language site.

Do not treat pages as isolated documents.

The objectives are to:

- make every English page read naturally and professionally;
- remove literal Arabic-to-English translation patterns;
- establish one consistent Tamayuz 10X English voice;
- improve clarity for an international audience;
- preserve factual meaning and evidence;
- reduce unnecessary repetition across pages;
- align terminology, capitalization, spelling, and naming;
- strengthen headlines, introductions, navigation labels, calls to action, and metadata where appropriate;
- identify contradictions or inconsistent descriptions across pages;
- remove formulaic or machine-like writing patterns;
- protect the author's intended meaning, evidence, and positioning.

A site-wide review is not permission to rewrite facts, invent claims, alter strategic meaning, or change verified names and figures.

---

## 29. Site-Wide Review Workflow

### Phase 1 — Inventory

Create or inspect the complete list of English pages and shared English-language components before making broad editorial decisions.

Classify pages by type, for example:

- homepage;
- about/profile;
- articles and case studies;
- research and publications;
- books;
- healthcare;
- leadership;
- services or professional expertise;
- training;
- news;
- contact;
- navigation or utility pages.

Identify shared descriptions, author biography text, brand statements, navigation labels, repeated calls to action, and recurring terminology.

### Phase 2 — Global terminology map

Build a controlled terminology list for recurring concepts.

Include, where relevant:

- Tamayuz 10X;
- institutional excellence;
- transformation engineering;
- leadership;
- organizational capability;
- innovation;
- digital transformation;
- artificial intelligence;
- healthcare excellence;
- strategy;
- governance;
- performance;
- value;
- sustainability;
- recurring program or framework names;
- verified English form of the author's name and professional title.

Do not assume that the same Arabic source term requires the same English word in every context.

### Phase 3 — Page-level audit

For every page, evaluate:

1. purpose;
2. H1/headline;
3. opening;
4. information structure;
5. translation quality;
6. human editorial quality;
7. clarity for an international audience;
8. terminology;
9. factual and evidence fidelity;
10. tone;
11. repetition;
12. cross-page consistency;
13. calls to action;
14. title/meta description when in scope;
15. final human read.

### Phase 4 — Cross-site consistency pass

Check for:

- inconsistent versions of the biography;
- conflicting descriptions of Tamayuz 10X;
- inconsistent translations of core concepts;
- different capitalization of the same term;
- unnecessary duplication;
- repetitive slogans;
- inconsistent tense or person;
- mixed American and British English;
- conflicting figures, dates, or claims;
- different definitions of the same concept;
- navigation labels that do not match destination content;
- inconsistent tone between older and newer pages.

A page may be grammatically correct and still fail the site-wide standard if it conflicts with the rest of the site.

### Phase 5 — Prioritized revision

Prioritize corrections in this order:

1. factual or meaning errors;
2. misleading translation;
3. unsupported or overstated claims;
4. confusing terminology;
5. unclear structure;
6. cross-page inconsistency;
7. unnatural English;
8. formulaic writing patterns and repetition;
9. unnecessary verbosity;
10. minor stylistic polish.

Spend the most effort where editorial risk is greatest.

### Phase 6 — Final site-wide pass

After all page-level revisions, review the English site again as one publication.

Do not stop merely because every individual page has been edited.

## 30. Editing Authority in Site-Wide Mode

When the user explicitly asks to **review and revise all English pages**, the agent is authorized to make normal editorial corrections without requesting approval sentence by sentence.

This includes:

- rewriting unnatural English;
- splitting or combining sentences;
- reorganizing paragraph order within a section;
- improving headings;
- removing repetition;
- replacing literal translations with natural English;
- simplifying jargon;
- defining essential unfamiliar terms;
- standardizing terminology;
- correcting grammar, punctuation, spelling, capitalization, and usage;
- improving metadata wording when included in scope;
- strengthening weak transitions;
- shortening inflated or formulaic text;
- standardizing navigation labels and calls to action where meaning is unchanged.

The editor must **not** silently:

- change numbers or dates;
- change verified factual claims;
- alter quotations;
- change the author's substantive argument;
- remove material evidence limitations;
- create new services, credentials, achievements, or claims;
- rename an institution without verification;
- change legal, medical, scientific, or technical meaning;
- replace an official title with an invented translation;
- change canonical URLs, structured data, or site functionality unless implementation is explicitly authorized.

When a substantive ambiguity cannot be resolved from available source material, flag it rather than guessing.

Maintain an internal editorial register when reviewing a full site.

| Field | Description |

When the user asks for a report, summarize the work at site level rather than producing an exhausting sentence-by-sentence change log unless requested.

## 32. Implementation Mode for Website Files

Editorial review and technical implementation are separate activities.

Only modify website files when the user explicitly authorizes implementation.

When implementation is authorized:

1. read the current file before editing;
2. preserve unrelated code and functionality;
3. avoid parallel writes to the same file;
4. make the smallest technically safe change that achieves the editorial objective;
5. preserve canonical URLs, schema, analytics, scripts, accessibility attributes, and navigation unless they are explicitly in scope;
6. verify that English-language changes did not alter Arabic content unintentionally;
7. review the final diff;
8. verify the deployed result before calling it live.

For Tamayuz 10X repository work, read the latest master operating manual before making content changes.

Distinguish clearly between:

- **Editorially approved:** the text has passed the editorial standard.
- **Technically updated:** the relevant files have been changed.
- **Published/live:** the final deployment for the exact latest version has been independently verified as successful.

Never use these statuses interchangeably.

---

## 33. High-Risk Change Gate

Pause and request human confirmation before implementing a change that would materially alter:

- professional credentials;
- legal statements;
- medical or health claims;
- financial claims;
- research conclusions;
- quotations;
- official organization names;
- quantified achievements;
- strategic positioning;
- author biography facts;
- claims of causality;
- commitments, guarantees, or promises.

Normal language cleanup does not require sentence-by-sentence approval when site-wide revision has been authorized.

When explicitly requested, provide a concise report covering:

- mode used;
- pages or sections reviewed;
- major structural changes;
- terminology decisions;
- claims deliberately qualified;
- ambiguities in the Arabic source;
- items still requiring verification;
- recurring site-wide language problems;
- Human Writing Gate result;

Keep this report outside the published content.

## 38. Final Acceptance Standard

A translation or English revision is ready for publication only when all conditions are met:

**Fidelity:** substantive meaning and uncertainty are preserved.

**Accuracy:** facts, numbers, names, quotations, and citations remain correct.

**Clarity:** the intended international audience can follow the argument.

**Editorial quality:** the text is concise, coherent, natural, and publication-ready.

**Human quality:** the prose shows judgment, variation, specificity, and restraint rather than recurring formulaic patterns.

**Consistency:** terminology, naming, capitalization, spelling, and brand language align with the rest of the English site.

**Risk control:** high-risk factual or substantive changes have been verified or flagged.

> **Final principle:** Do not optimize for sounding impressive. Optimize for being clear, credible, natural, accurate, and worth reading.

## 39. Future-to-Capability Editorial Rule — Added 11 September 2026

Apply the following rules to Tamayuz 10X case studies and analytical articles, especially those involving AI, digital transformation, innovation or operating-model redesign.

### Start with the desired future when the case is fundamentally future-facing
Do not lead with the technology when the more important proposition is the future the organization is preparing to serve. Establish, as evidence allows, **what future the organization needs to be capable of succeeding in and what that future looks like in practical terms**.

Then work backward through a backcasting logic:

**Desired Future → Required Capabilities → What Must Be Built Now → New Operating Behavior → Outcomes and Impact**

The English version should express this logic naturally rather than mirror the syntax of the Arabic version.

### Do not manufacture a formal foresight claim
Distinguish between a documented future-oriented design choice and a documented formal foresight methodology. If sources show that infrastructure, investment or capabilities were designed for adaptability, scientific change, changing demand or future needs, describe that evidence precisely. Do not say the organization “used foresight,” “read the future,” or applied scenario planning unless the source directly supports that claim.

### Translate technology into operating function
Do not leave terms such as AI, advanced analytics, connected systems or predictive monitoring at the level of labels. Explain what the capability does in the workflow, which decision or action it informs, and what becomes possible because of it.

For AI cases, explicitly distinguish among **decision support, automation and autonomous decision-making**. Never upgrade decision support into autonomous decision-making without evidence.

If an operational example is illustrative rather than documented in the case source, label it as an illustration and never present it as an observed event at the organization.

### Put context around metrics
For every material number, clarify whenever available:
- the period or baseline year;
- what the metric actually measures;
- the scope or denominator;
- whether it is organization-reported or independently established;
- whether the evidence supports causation, contribution, association or chronology.

If the source does not provide enough detail, preserve that limitation in the prose. Do not let a clean sentence imply a cleaner causal story than the evidence supports.

### Depth without repetition
Longer does not mean better if the article repeats the same proposition in new words. Give each section one distinct editorial job: define the future, establish the gap, identify the capability, explain the operating mechanism, interpret the evidence, state the limitation, and extract the transferable insight.

Key metrics may appear in the executive snapshot and then be analysed once in the evidence section. Do not restate the same number in several sections unless it advances a genuinely different argument.

Merge adjacent explanatory sections when they are doing the same work. Prefer one clear mechanism section over separate repetitive sections for data, inference, warning and decision support. Keep hypothetical examples shorter than the documented case evidence.

### The 10X Question must begin with the future
The closing 10X question should first make the future concrete:

**What future do we want this organization to be capable of operating in? What will that look like in practice, and what will the organization be able to do then that it cannot do today? If that is the future we want, what capabilities, data, systems, skills, governance and ways of working must we start building now?**

Do not reduce the closing question to “How can we improve today?” The point is to define the desired future and then work backward to the present.

### The “what became possible?” test
Before publication, ask:

**What can the organization now see, understand, decide or do that it could not do in the same way before?**

If the article cannot answer that clearly, it is probably still describing technology rather than explaining institutional capability.

### Bilingual parity without literal translation
When the Arabic and English pages form a paired Tamayuz 10X case:
- preserve the same substantive section sequence, facts, metrics, evidence limits, references and analytical logic;
- allow language-specific headline syntax, sentence order within sections and paragraph rhythm;
- rebuild the English prose as original editorial English rather than sentence-by-sentence translation;
- do not reorganize the case into a materially different argument merely to make the English version sound smoother.



## 39. Company Insider Critique Gate

After the full article is complete, but before the final technical quality gate and before publication, run a separate Company Insider Critique. Review the article from the simulated perspective of a senior expert inside the organization studied, preferably the team lead or executive directly responsible for the initiative or transformation. This is an evidence-based critical simulation, not a claim to represent the organization. Never attribute an unpublished opinion to the company, institution, or team.

Challenge the article as if the implementation team were reviewing it internally: what has been oversimplified; what may be inaccurate, incomplete, or missing context; whether the transformation timeline, decision rights, governance, human capability building, operating mechanism, documented examples, baselines, indicator definitions, measurement boundaries, failures, adjustments, and learning are represented as far as the evidence allows; and whether the prose distinguishes organizational claims from independently supported facts. Also ask what the organization can now see, understand, decide, or do that it could not do in the same way before.

After the critique, return to the sources and verify every criticism that can be checked. Revise the article where the evidence supports the criticism. If the critique exposes a gap for which no public evidence exists, preserve that gap under evidence limitations rather than inventing an answer. Do not proceed to publication until the editorial record captures the main critique points, revisions made in response, and remaining unverifiable gaps.

## 40. Definitions for New, Technical, and Medical Terms

At first meaningful use, define unfamiliar technical, scientific, medical, operational, or organizational terms in plain language. For abbreviations, give the full English term first, then the abbreviation in parentheses, followed by a short functional explanation when needed. For medical terms in particular, explain briefly what the term, procedure, disease, technology, or treatment is; what happens in practice; and why it matters in the context of the case. Do not define one unfamiliar term with a more unfamiliar term, and do not turn a necessary definition into a mini-textbook.
